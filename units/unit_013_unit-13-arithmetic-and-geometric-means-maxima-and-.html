Reading

1. Two ways of averaging

Given two positive numbers a and b there are two natural 'averages'. The arithmetic mean (AM) is (a + b)/2: add and divide. The geometric mean (GM) is √(ab): multiply and take the square root. Use the AM when quantities add (the average of two test scores), and the GM when they multiply (the average growth factor of two successive years). For 4 and 9 the AM is 6.5 and the GM is √36 = 6; for 5 and 5 both are 5.

Notice that the GM is smaller than the AM in the first example. This is no accident but a theorem, the AM–GM inequality: for all non-negative a and b, (a + b)/2 ≥ √(ab), with equality exactly when a = b. Proof: (√a − √b)² ≥ 0, and expanding gives a − 2√(ab) + b ≥ 0, so a + b ≥ 2√(ab). Equality needs √a = √b, i.e. a = b.

Geometric meaning. A rectangle with sides a and b has area ab, and a square with the same perimeter has side (a + b)/2. The inequality says the square has at least as much area: (a + b)²/4 ≥ ab. Among all rectangles with the same perimeter, the square has the largest area. Equivalently, among rectangles of the same area the square has the smallest perimeter. A second picture: in a circle of diameter a + b a chord perpendicular to the diameter at a point splitting it into a and b has half-length √(ab) (the geometric-mean theorem), and it cannot exceed the radius (a + b)/2.

Why it is useful. Whenever you need the largest value of a product with a fixed sum, or the smallest sum with a fixed product, AM–GM answers instantly without any calculus or completing the square, and tells you exactly when the extreme is reached.

Try it

Find the AM and GM of 2 and 18, and say when AM = GM.

Answer. AM = 10, GM = √36 = 6. They would be equal only if the two numbers were equal.

2. Maximising a product with fixed sum

If x and y are positive with x + y = S fixed, then xy ≤ (S/2)² = S²/4, with equality when x = y = S/2. For S = 10 the product is at most 25, reached at 5 and 5 (other splits give 24, 21, 16, …). This is the same as the vertex of the parabola x(10 − x) from Unit 10, but the AM–GM argument needs no formula: √(xy) ≤ (x + y)/2 = 5, so xy ≤ 25.

Worked example 1 (a fence). A farmer wants to enclose a rectangular pen against a wall using 60 m of fence for three sides. If the two short sides have length x, the long side is 60 − 2x and the area is x(60 − 2x) = ½ · (2x)(60 − 2x). The two factors 2x and 60 − 2x add to the constant 60, so their product is at most 30² = 900, hence the area is at most 450, attained when 2x = 60 − 2x, i.e. x = 15. A 15 m by 30 m pen has area 450 m². The trick was to rearrange so that the sum of the factors is fixed.

Worked example 2 (three terms). Maximise x(30 − 2x) for 0 < x < 15. Write it as ½ · 2x · (30 − 2x); the factors 2x and 30 − 2x sum to 30, so their product is at most 15² = 225 and the expression is at most 112.5, at x = 7.5. A common beginner's error is to apply AM–GM directly to x and 30 − 2x, whose sum is not constant.

The principle: to bound a product above you need a sum that stays fixed. If the given sum is not constant, look for a way to adjust the factors (multiplying one factor by a constant) so that it becomes constant.

Try it

Two positive numbers add to 14. What is the largest possible product, and for which numbers?

Answer. 49, when both numbers are 7.

3. Minimising a sum with fixed product

The same inequality read the other way says: if x and y are positive and xy = P is fixed, then x + y ≥ 2√P, with equality when x = y = √P. A classic special case is x + 1/x ≥ 2 for x > 0, with equality at x = 1 (the product of x and 1/x is 1). More generally x + k/x ≥ 2√k.

Worked example 1. The smallest value of x + 9/x for x > 0 is 2√9 = 6, reached when x = 9/x, i.e. x = 3. Check: 3 + 3 = 6; at x = 2: 2 + 4.5 = 6.5; at x = 4: 4 + 2.25 = 6.25; both bigger ✓.

Worked example 2. Show that (a + b)(1/a + 1/b) ≥ 4 for positive a and b. Expand: 2 + a/b + b/a, and the middle pair is at least 2 by AM–GM since its product is 1. So the whole is at least 4, with equality when a = b. A similar trick shows (a + b + c)(1/a + 1/b + 1/c) ≥ 9.

Worked example 3 (a rectangle). A rectangle has area 36. What is the least possible perimeter? The perimeter is 2(x + y) with xy = 36, so x + y ≥ 2√36 = 12 and the perimeter is at least 24, achieved by the 6 × 6 square. Compare a 4 × 9 rectangle, with perimeter 26, and a 3 × 12 one, with perimeter 30.

Try it

Find the minimum of 4x + 25/x for x > 0 and where it occurs.

Answer. Apply AM–GM to the two terms: 4x + 25/x ≥ 2√(4x · 25/x) = 2√100 = 20, with equality when 4x = 25/x, i.e. x² = 25/4, x = 5/2.

4. AM–GM for more than two numbers

The inequality extends to any number of non-negative numbers: (a₁ + a₂ + … + aₙ)/n ≥ ⁿ√(a₁a₂⋯aₙ), with equality exactly when all the aᵢ are equal. For three numbers: (a + b + c)/3 ≥ ∛(abc). For example for 1, 3, 9 the AM is 13/3 ≈ 4.33 and the GM is ∛27 = 3. Its power comes when a sum is fixed and many factors are multiplied.

Why it is true (three numbers). There is an identity a³ + b³ + c³ − 3abc = ½(a + b + c)[(a − b)² + (b − c)² + (c − a)²]. For non-negative a, b, c the right side is ≥ 0, so a³ + b³ + c³ ≥ 3abc. Putting a = ∛x, b = ∛y, c = ∛z gives x + y + z ≥ 3∛(xyz). For larger n there is a neat induction proof (Cauchy's), or one can 'smooth' unequal numbers towards their average, which raises the product.

Worked example (a box). A box without a lid has a square base of side x and height h, and must hold 32 cubic units. Its surface area is x² + 4xh. Since h = 32/x², the area is x² + 128/x. Split the second term into two equal halves: x² + 64/x + 64/x. These three terms have product x² · 64/x · 64/x = 4096 = 16³, so the sum is at least 3 · ∛4096 = 3 · 16 = 48, with equality when x² = 64/x, i.e. x = 4, and then h = 2. The cheapest box is 4 × 4 × 2 with surface area 16 + 32 = 48. The splitting was chosen so that the product of the terms has no x left in it.

Checking equality. Always check that equality is attainable. If the 'equal terms' condition leads to a contradiction then the minimum is bigger than the bound; AM–GM then only gives a weak bound. Here x² = 64/x gave x = 4, a perfectly valid value.

Try it

Find the smallest value of a + b + c if abc = 27 and a, b, c > 0, and when it occurs.

Answer. AM–GM: a + b + c ≥ 3∛27 = 9, with equality when a = b = c = 3.

5. Weighted splitting: when the terms are not equal

Sometimes the natural terms cannot be equal at the optimum, but you can split them so that they are. Worked example. Given x + y = 12 with x, y > 0, maximise x²y. Write x²y = 4 · (x/2)(x/2)y. The three numbers x/2, x/2, y have the constant sum x + y = 12, so by AM–GM (x/2)(x/2)y ≤ (12/3)³ = 64, hence x²y ≤ 256. Equality needs x/2 = y = 4, so x = 8 and y = 4 (and indeed 8 + 4 = 12). Check: 64 · 4 = 256; and nearby, x = 7, y = 5 gives 245 and x = 9, y = 3 gives 243.

The recipe: to deal with xᵐyⁿ under a fixed x + y, split x into m equal parts and y into n equal parts (adjusting by constants) so that the pieces sum to the given constant and all equal at the optimum. Without calculus you can maximise products of powers.

Another example. For 0 < x < 1, find the maximum of x(1 − x)². Write 2x · (1 − x) · (1 − x) = 2x(1 − x)²; the three numbers 2x, 1 − x, 1 − x add up to 2x + 2(1 − x) = 2, which is constant. So 2x(1 − x)² ≤ (2/3)³ = 8/27, and x(1 − x)² ≤ 4/27 ≈ 0.148, with equality when 2x = 1 − x, i.e. x = 1/3. Check: (1/3)(2/3)² = 4/27 ✓.

Try it

For positive x, y with 2x + y = 12, find the largest possible value of xy and where it happens.

Answer. Write xy = ½ · (2x) · y with 2x + y = 12 constant, so (2x)y ≤ 6² = 36 and xy ≤ 18, at 2x = y = 6, i.e. x = 3, y = 6.

6. A longer problem

Problem. Positive numbers a, b, c satisfy a + b + c = 6. (a) Prove that 1/a + 1/b + 1/c ≥ 3/2. (b) Find the maximum of abc. (c) Say when equality holds in each part.

Solution. (a) By AM–GM, (a + b + c)(1/a + 1/b + 1/c) ≥ 3∛(abc) · 3∛(1/(abc)) = 9. Since a + b + c = 6 this gives 6(1/a + 1/b + 1/c) ≥ 9, so 1/a + 1/b + 1/c ≥ 9/6 = 3/2. (b) By AM–GM, ∛(abc) ≤ (a + b + c)/3 = 2, so abc ≤ 8. (c) Equality occurs in both exactly when a = b = c = 2: then 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 3/2 and 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 ✓.

Reflection. Both parts have the same 'extremal configuration' a = b = c, which is a typical pattern: when the problem is symmetric and the constraint is symmetric, the extremum is very often at the symmetric point (but this must still be proved, as the next unit shows with counterexamples).

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Using AM–GM on numbers that may be negative: the inequality needs non-negative numbers (for a = −4, b = −9 the 'GM' √36 = 6 exceeds the AM −6.5). (2) Using it when the sum or product is not constant. (3) Forgetting to check that equality is attainable. (4) Mixing up the direction: AM ≥ GM, so AM gives an upper bound on GM. (5) Writing x + 1/x ≥ 2 for negative x, where actually x + 1/x ≤ −2.

Checks. Plug in the equality case and one other point. If your bound claims min = 6, then test values on both sides of the claimed minimiser.

How to practise. For each problem circle the quantity that is constant (sum or product) and write the term(s) that must be equal. Then do a numerical check. The Unlimited practice family on AM–GM gives problems with random constants.

Hint(1 + a)/2 = 7.
Answer13
Method: Solve
