1. Jensen's inequality and smoothing

A function is convex if the chord lies above the graph; then f((x + y)/2) ≤ (f(x) + f(y))/2, and in general Σ f(xᵢ) ≥ n·f(mean). For 1/x on positive reals with Σ xᵢ = 8 and n = 4 the sum is at least 4 · f(2) = 4 · ½ = 2. For concave f the inequality flips: with Σ xᵢ = 9 and n = 4, Σ √xᵢ ≤ 4 · √(9/4) = 6.

Smoothing. For a convex f, replacing two numbers by two closer numbers with the same sum lowers Σ f. With integers adding to 17 in five places the sum of squares is smallest for 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, namely 59. On an interval, a convex function is largest at the endpoints: with x₁, …, x₅ in [0, 1] and sum 2.5 the largest Σ xᵢ² is 1 + 1 + 0.25 = 2.25.

Try it

Minimum of Σ x² if three reals sum to 6?

Answer. 3 · 4 = 12.

Try it

Maximum of √x + √y if x + y = 8?

Answer. 2 · √4 = 4.

2. Balancing the coefficients

When AM-GM is used for a minimum, the terms must be arranged so the equality case is reachable. For the minimum of x + 4/x² write x as x/2 + x/2: then x/2 + x/2 + 4/x² ≥ 3·∛(1) = 3, and equality needs x/2 = 4/x², i.e. x = 2, where the value is 2 + 1 = 3 ✓. Had one used x + 4/x² ≥ 2·√(4/x), the bound would not be a constant and would not give the minimum. The same idea with Cauchy–Schwarz: (1/x + 4/y)(x + y) ≥ 9 with equality at x : y = 1 : 2.

Try it

Minimum of x + 32/x² for x > 0?

Answer. x/2 + x/2 + 32/x² ≥ 3·∛8 = 6, at x = 4.

Try it

Largest product of positive integers adding to 10?

Answer. 3 · 3 · 2 · 2 = 36.

3. A worked problem

Problem. Positive reals x, y satisfy x + y = 6. Find the minimum of 1/x + 4/y. Solution. By Cauchy–Schwarz (1/x + 4/y)(x + y) ≥ (1 + 2)² = 9, so 1/x + 4/y ≥ 9/6 = 3/2, with equality at x = 2, y = 4.

Check. 1/2 + 4/4 = 3/2 ✓.

Problem p1. Positive reals x₁, …, x_4 have sum 8. What is the minimum value of 1/x₁ + … + 1/x_4? (fraction if needed)

HintThe function 1/x is convex for x > 0; use Jensen's inequality.
Answer2
Method: Jensen

For convex f, Σ f(xᵢ) ≥ n·f(mean) = 4·f(2) = 4·1/2 = 2.

Method: AM-HM

The harmonic mean is at most the arithmetic mean, so Σ 1/xᵢ ≥ 4²/8 = 2.

Source: Technique from Pham Kim Hung ch. 4, 6 and Cîrtoaje ch. 3 (original problem)

Problem p4. Reals x₁, …, x_3 have sum 6. What is the minimum value of x₁⁴ + … + x_3⁴? (fraction if needed)

Hintx⁴ is convex on all of ℝ.
Answer48
Method: Jensen

Σ xᵢ⁴ ≥ n(Σ xᵢ/n)⁴ = 3·(2)⁴ = 48.

Method: Tangent line

x⁴ ≥ 4m³x − 3m⁴ with m = 2 (it is (x − m)²(x² + 2mx + 3m²) ≥ 0); summing gives 48.

Source: Technique from Pham Kim Hung ch. 4, 6 and Cîrtoaje ch. 3 (original problem)

Problem p10. 5 non-negative integers add up to 17. What is the smallest possible value of the sum of their squares?

HintConvexity says to keep the numbers as equal as possible.
Answer59
Method: Balance

Use 3 copies of 3 and 2 copies of 4: 3·9 + 2·16 = 59.

Method: Smoothing

If two numbers differ by 2 or more, moving them one step closer lowers the sum of squares, so the minimum has all numbers within 1 of each other: 59.

Source: Technique from Pham Kim Hung ch. 4, 6 and Cîrtoaje ch. 3 (original problem)

Problem p20. Reals x₁, …, x_5 lie in [0, 1] and have sum 5/2. What is the maximum of x₁² + … + x_5²? (fraction if needed)

Hintx² is convex, so the maximum is at an extreme point: all but one variable are 0 or 1.
Answer9/4
Method: Convexity

A convex function is largest at extreme points: 2 ones, one number equal to 1/2, and zeros give 2 + (1/2)² = 9/4.

Method: Pushing apart

Moving two interior numbers apart (keeping their sum) increases the sum of squares, so at most one variable stays strictly inside (0, 1): value 9/4.

Source: Technique from Pham Kim Hung ch. 4, 6 and Cîrtoaje ch. 3 (original problem)

Problem p23. Positive integers add up to 10. What is the largest possible value of their product?

HintUse as many 3s as possible, but avoid a leftover 1.
Answer36
Method: Balancing

Parts equal to 3 are best (4 = 2 + 2 ties, 5 splits as 2 + 3 to give more): the optimum is 3^3 with 4 in place of 3 + 1, product 36.

Method: Dynamic programming

Let P(n) be the best product; P(n) = max over j of j·P(n − j), P(1) = 1 ... P(10) = 36.

Source: Technique from Pham Kim Hung ch. 4, 6 and Cîrtoaje ch. 3 (original problem)
