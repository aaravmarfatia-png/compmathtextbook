1. Divisibility: the basic idea and its rules of calculation

We say that a divides b (written a | b) when b = a·k for some whole number k. So 6 | 42 because 42 = 6 × 7, and every integer divides 0 (0 = a × 0), but 0 divides only 0. 'Divisor', 'factor' and 'multiple' are three views of the same fact: if 6 | 42 then 6 is a divisor (or factor) of 42 and 42 is a multiple of 6. Number theory, the study of whole numbers, is mostly the study of this relation.

Three rules do most of the work. (1) Transitivity: if a | b and b | c then a | c. (2) Linear combinations: if a | b and a | c then a | (bx + cy) for all integers x and y. In particular a divides b + c and b − c. (3) Size: if a | b and b ≠ 0 then |a| ≤ |b|, so a non-zero number has only finitely many divisors. Rule (2) is the most useful: it lets you say what a number must divide without knowing the number. For example, if n divides both 3m + 5 and m + 2 then n divides (3m + 5) − 3(m + 2) = −1, so n = ±1.

Worked example 1. Show n³ − n is divisible by 6 for every integer n. Factor it as (n − 1)n(n + 1), a product of three consecutive integers. Among any three consecutive integers one is a multiple of 3, and at least one is even, so the product is divisible by 2 and by 3. Since 2 and 3 share no factor, it is divisible by 6. (Spot check: n = 5 gives 120 = 6 × 20, and n = −3 gives −24.)

Worked example 2. Divisibility rules can be derived, not just memorised. A number is divisible by 9 exactly when its digit sum is, because 10 leaves remainder 1 when divided by 9, hence so does every power of 10. For 123456 the digit sum is 21 and 21 = 2 × 9 + 3, so 123456 leaves remainder 3 when divided by 9. The rule for 11 uses the alternating sum, since 10 ≡ −1 (mod 11), and 7, 11 and 13 can be tested together because 1001 = 7 × 11 × 13.

Try it

If n divides both 4m + 9 and m + 2, what values can n take?

Answer. n divides (4m + 9) − 4(m + 2) = 1, so n = 1 or −1.

2. Primes and prime factorisation

A prime is a whole number greater than 1 whose only positive divisors are 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … A number greater than 1 that is not prime is composite. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from the order of the factors. So 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5, and no other product of primes gives 360. Uniqueness is what makes factorisations a reliable tool, and it fails in some other number systems.

Counting divisors. A divisor of 360 uses 0, 1, 2 or 3 factors of 2, 0, 1 or 2 factors of 3, and 0 or 1 factor of 5, independently. So the number of divisors is 4 × 3 × 2 = 24. In general if n = p₁^a × p₂^b × … then it has (a + 1)(b + 1)… divisors. The sum of divisors multiplies too: (1 + 2 + 4 + 8)(1 + 3 + 9)(1 + 5) = 15 × 13 × 6 = 1170 for n = 360. A number has an odd number of divisors exactly when every exponent is even, that is, when it is a perfect square: divisors pair up as d and n/d except when d = √n.

Primality testing. To decide whether n is prime you only need to test primes up to √n, because if n = ab with a ≤ b then a ≤ √n. For 221, √221 ≈ 14.9, so test 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13: it is divisible by 13, and 221 = 13 × 17. There are infinitely many primes (Euclid: if p₁, …, p_k were all of them, p₁⋯p_k + 1 leaves remainder 1 on division by each, so it has a prime factor not on the list).

Worked example. How many positive divisors of 2024 are even? 2024 = 2³ × 11 × 23, so a divisor is even if it uses at least one factor of 2: choices for the power of 2 are 1, 2, 3 (three choices), and for 11 and 23, two choices each, giving 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 even divisors out of the 16 divisors in all.

Try it

Find the number of divisors of 720 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5, and decide how many of them are perfect squares.

Answer. Number of divisors: 5 × 3 × 2 = 30. A square divisor needs even exponents: for 2 choose 0, 2, 4 (three choices), for 3 choose 0, 2 (two choices), for 5 choose 0 only (one choice): 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 square divisors.

3. The greatest common divisor and Euclid's algorithm

The greatest common divisor gcd(a, b) is the largest number dividing both. The least common multiple lcm(a, b) is the smallest positive number that both divide. They are linked by gcd(a, b) × lcm(a, b) = a × b. If you know the prime factorisations, the gcd takes the smaller exponent of each prime and the lcm the larger: 48 = 2⁴·3 and 180 = 2²·3²·5 give gcd = 2²·3 = 12 and lcm = 2⁴·3²·5 = 720, and indeed 12 × 720 = 8640 = 48 × 180. Two numbers are coprime when their gcd is 1.

Euclid's algorithm. Factoring large numbers is hard, but the gcd is easy, using the observation gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b) (any common divisor of a and b divides a − qb and conversely). Repeat until the remainder is 0; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd. Example for 252 and 198: 252 = 1 × 198 + 54; 198 = 3 × 54 + 36; 54 = 1 × 36 + 18; 36 = 2 × 18 + 0. So gcd(252, 198) = 18 and lcm = 252 × 198 / 18 = 2772. The numbers shrink so fast that even 100-digit numbers need only a few hundred steps.

Bézout's identity. For any integers a and b there are integers x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b). Work back up the algorithm: 18 = 54 − 36 = 54 − (198 − 3 × 54) = 4 × 54 − 198 = 4(252 − 198) − 198 = 4 × 252 − 5 × 198. Check: 1008 − 990 = 18 ✓. Consequences: if gcd(a, b) = 1 then ax + by = 1 can be solved, which is the key to dividing in modular arithmetic; and if a number divides a product bc and is coprime to b, it must divide c.

Worked example (gcd of special numbers). gcd(1001, 91): 1001 = 11 × 91 + 0, so gcd = 91. And gcd(2¹⁰ − 1, 2¹⁵ − 1) = 2⁵ − 1 = 31, an instance of the general fact gcd(2^m − 1, 2^n − 1) = 2^gcd(m,n) − 1, which you can verify by running Euclid's algorithm on the exponents.

Try it

Find gcd(4620, 1155) and gcd(8, 15), and write 1 as 8x + 15y.

Answer. 4620 = 4 × 1155, so gcd(4620, 1155) = 1155. For 8 and 15: 15 = 1 × 8 + 7; 8 = 1 × 7 + 1; so 1 = 8 − 7 = 8 − (15 − 8) = 2 × 8 − 15, that is x = 2, y = −1.

4. Congruences: arithmetic with remainders

Two integers are congruent modulo m, written a ≡ b (mod m), when m divides a − b, i.e. when they leave the same remainder after division by m. Think of a clock: on a 12-hour clock 15 o'clock and 3 o'clock are the same, so 15 ≡ 3 (mod 12). Congruences can be added, subtracted and multiplied just like equations: if a ≡ b and c ≡ d (mod m) then a + c ≡ b + d, a − c ≡ b − d and ac ≡ bd (mod m). In particular a ≡ b implies aᵏ ≡ bᵏ.

This is a huge saving: you can reduce numbers before computing. Worked example 1. Last digit of 7¹⁰⁰: work mod 10. 7² = 49 ≡ 9, 7⁴ ≡ 81 ≡ 1, so 7¹⁰⁰ = (7⁴)²⁵ ≡ 1²⁵ = 1 (mod 10); the last digit is 1. For the last two digits work mod 100: 7⁴ = 2401 ≡ 1, so 7¹⁰⁰ ≡ 1 (mod 100) and the last two digits are 01.

Worked example 2 (cycles). Powers of 2 mod 7 run 2, 4, 1, 2, 4, 1, … with period 3. Since 100 = 3 × 33 + 1, 2¹⁰⁰ ≡ 2¹ = 2 (mod 7). Any time you take a high power, look for the cycle. Worked example 3 (a tidy proof). A square leaves remainder 0 or 1 when divided by 4 (even numbers give 0, odd numbers give (2k + 1)² = 4k² + 4k + 1 ≡ 1), so a sum of two squares can never be 3 mod 4; hence 2023 = 4 × 505 + 3 is not a sum of two squares.

What you cannot do. You can reduce a base, but not the exponent naively: 2⁵ is not congruent to 2² mod 7 just because 5 ≡ 2 mod 3. And you cannot always divide: 2 × 3 ≡ 2 × 6 (mod 6) but 3 ≢ 6 (mod 6) here, so cancelling the 2 failed. Division is allowed only when the number you divide by is coprime to the modulus; that is the next section.

Try it

What is the remainder when 3²⁰²⁴ is divided by 5?

Answer. 3⁴ = 81 ≡ 1 (mod 5), and 2024 = 4 × 506, so 3²⁰²⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 5). The remainder is 1.

5. Inverses and linear congruences

If gcd(a, m) = 1, Bézout gives ax + my = 1, so ax ≡ 1 (mod m): x is the inverse of a modulo m, and we can 'divide by a'. For a = 7 and m = 11, 7 × 8 = 56 = 5 × 11 + 1, so 8 is the inverse of 7 modulo 11. To solve 7x ≡ 3 (mod 11) multiply both sides by 8: x ≡ 24 ≡ 2 (mod 11). Check: 7 × 2 = 14 = 11 + 3 ✓.

A congruence ax ≡ b (mod m) has a solution exactly when gcd(a, m) divides b, and then it has exactly gcd(a, m) solutions modulo m. The equation 6x ≡ 4 (mod 10) has gcd(6, 10) = 2 dividing 4, so it has 2 solutions: x = 4 (6 × 4 = 24 ≡ 4) and x = 9 (6 × 9 = 54 ≡ 4). The equation 6x ≡ 3 (mod 10) has no solution since 2 does not divide 3.

The Chinese remainder theorem. If the moduli are coprime, several congruences can be combined into one. To solve x ≡ 2 (mod 3), x ≡ 3 (mod 5), x ≡ 2 (mod 7): combining the first and third gives x ≡ 2 (mod 21) (the numbers 2, 23, 44, …), and testing which of these leaves remainder 3 on division by 5 finds x = 23. The full solution is x ≡ 23 (mod 105). Historically this is an ancient Chinese puzzle about counting soldiers.

Try it

Solve 5x ≡ 7 (mod 12).

Answer. 5 × 5 = 25 ≡ 1 (mod 12), so 5 is its own inverse modulo 12; multiply both sides by 5: x ≡ 35 ≡ 11 (mod 12). Check: 5 × 11 = 55 = 4 × 12 + 7 ✓.

6. Longer examples

Problem 1. Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 1 when divided by 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, and is divisible by 7. Solution. Let N − 1 be divisible by lcm(2, 3, 4, 5, 6) = 60, so N = 60k + 1. We need 60k + 1 ≡ 0 (mod 7). Since 60 ≡ 4 (mod 7) this says 4k ≡ −1 ≡ 6 (mod 7); the inverse of 4 mod 7 is 2 (4 × 2 = 8 ≡ 1), so k ≡ 12 ≡ 5 (mod 7). The smallest k is 5, giving N = 301. Check: 301 = 7 × 43, and 301 − 1 = 300 = 60 × 5 ✓.

Problem 2. Prove that n⁵ − n is divisible by 30 for every integer n. Solution. Factor: n⁵ − n = n(n − 1)(n + 1)(n² + 1). The first three factors are consecutive, so the product is divisible by 2 and 3. To show divisibility by 5 check the five residues of n modulo 5: if n ≡ 0, ±1 then one of n, n − 1, n + 1 is divisible by 5; if n ≡ ±2 then n² + 1 ≡ 5 ≡ 0. Since 2, 3, 5 are coprime, 30 divides n⁵ − n.

Problem 3. A number has digit sum 20; is it possible for it to be a perfect square? Squares modulo 9 are 0, 1, 4 or 7; a number is congruent mod 9 to its digit sum, and 20 ≡ 2 (mod 9), which is not in the list. So it cannot be a perfect square.

The three problems show the pattern for number theory: translate to remainders, reduce the size of the numbers, and use an exhaustive check over the few possible remainders when a proof is needed.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Forgetting that 1 is neither prime nor composite. (2) Dividing a congruence by a number that shares a factor with the modulus. (3) Reducing exponents mod m instead of working with cycles. (4) Mixing up gcd and lcm when doing word problems about 'when will two events coincide' (lcm) versus 'largest equal groups' (gcd). (5) Taking remainders of negative numbers: −7 mod 5 is 3, not −2 (though −2 ≡ 3).

Checks. After finding gcd by Euclid, check that it divides both original numbers. After solving a congruence, substitute. After a divisibility proof, try n = 0, 1, 2, −1 as sanity checks.

How to practise. Make a table of squares and cubes mod 7, 8, 9, 10: it solves many problems by itself. Do one Euclid/Bézout computation by hand each day for a week. The Unlimited practice section has families on Euclid's algorithm and remainders.

Problem 1.1.1. Find gcd(110, 490).

HintUse Euclid's algorithm: divide, then repeat with the remainder.
Answer10
Method: Euclid's algorithm

490 = 110 · 4 + 50. 110 = 50 · 2 + 10. 50 = 10 · 5 + 0. The last non-zero remainder is 10.

Method: Prime factorization

110 = 2 · 5 · 11 and 490 = 2 · 5 · 7². The common primes with the smaller exponents are 2 and 5, so the gcd is 2 · 5 = 10.

Method: List the divisors

Divisors of 110: 1, 2, 5, 10, 11, 22, 55, 110. Check which divide 490: 1, 2, 5, 10. The biggest is 10.

Problem 1.1.4. (Duke) What is the sum of all integers n such that n² + 2n + 2 divides n³ + 4n² + 4n − 14? (Be careful with signs: the answer is negative.)

HintDivide the polynomials: the quotient is n + 2, and the remainder is −2n − 18.
Answer-11
Method: Division, then bound

n³ + 4n² + 4n − 14 = (n² + 2n + 2)(n + 2) + (−2n − 18). So n² + 2n + 2 must divide −2n − 18. Either the remainder is 0 (n = −9) or |−2n − 18| ≥ n² + 2n + 2, which only holds for −4 ≤ n ≤ 4. Method: Brute-force check

Computing the divisibility for every integer from −1000 to 1000 gives exactly those seven values. Their sum is −9 − 4 − 2 − 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = −11. (The text version of the PDF loses minus signs, so I verified the sign this way.)

HintEvery amount is 6x + 9y.
Answer3

Every measurable amount has the form 6x + 9y, which is a multiple of gcd(6, 9) = 3. And 3 = 9 − 6 is measurable. So the smallest positive amount is 3.

Problem 1.3.3. (AIME 1987) How many ordered triples (a, b, c) of positive integers satisfy lcm(a, b) = 1000, lcm(b, c) = 2000, lcm(c, a) = 2000?

Hint1000 = 2³ · 5³ and 2000 = 2⁴ · 5³. Handle the exponents of 2 and of 5 separately.
Answer70
Method: Primes separately

Write a = 2^a₂ · 5^a₅ and so on. Powers of 2: lcm(b, c) = 2⁴ and lcm(c, a) = 2⁴ while lcm(a, b) = 2³, so a and b are at most 2³ and the 2⁴ must come from c: c₂ = 4. We also need max(a₂, b₂) = 3. Pairs (a₂, b₂) from 0..3 with max 3: 4 + 4 − 1 = 7. Powers of 5: all three lcms have 5³, so among a₅, b₅, c₅ (each 0 to 3) at least two equal 3. Exactly two equal 3: 3 choices of which one is smaller, times 3 values (0, 1, 2) = 9; all three equal 3: 1. Total 10. The primes are independent, so 7 · 10 = 70.

Method: Computer check

Looping over divisors of 2000 and checking the three lcm conditions gives 70.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Example 1.3.3 (AIME 1987)

Problem 1.24. (HMMT) Compute gcd(2002 + 2, 2002² + 2, 2002³ + 2, …).

Hintgcd(2004, 2002² + 2): use 2002 ≡ −2 (mod 2004).
Answer6
Method: Euclid, then mod 6

The first term is 2004. 2002² + 2 = 4008006 = 2004 · 2000 + 6, so gcd(2004, 2002² + 2) = gcd(2004, 6) = 6, since 2004 = 6 · 334. The gcd of all the terms divides this, so it is at most 6 (it divides 6). Every term is divisible by 6: 2002 leaves remainder 4 when divided by 6, and 4ᵏ always leaves remainder 4, so 2002ᵏ + 2 leaves 4 + 2 = 6, i.e. remainder 0. Hence the gcd is exactly 6.

Method: Computer check

The gcd of the first 30 terms is 6.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Exercise 1.24 (HMMT)

Problem 1.25. Calculate gcd(n! + 1, (n + 1)!) for n = 5.

Hint(n + 1)! = (n + 1) · n!.
Answer1
Method: Euclid for n = 5

gcd(121, 720): 720 = 121 · 5 + 115; 121 = 115 + 6; 115 = 6 · 19 + 1. So 1.

Method: Prime factors

For n = 5: 121 = 11², so the only possible common prime is 11. But 720 = 2⁴ · 3² · 5 has no factor 11. So the gcd is 1. (In general, a prime p dividing n! + 1 cannot divide n!, so it can only divide (n + 1)! through the factor n + 1; and then p ≤ n + 1 would divide n! unless p = n + 1, which is the case to examine.)

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Exercise 1.25 (evaluated at n = 5)

Problem 2.3.3. (AIME 1983) Let aₙ = 6ⁿ + 8ⁿ. What is the remainder when a₈₃ is divided by 49?

HintUse 6⁸⁴ ≡ 1 and 8⁸⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 49).
Answer35
Method: Euler's theorem

φ(49) = 42, so 6⁸⁴ ≡ 1 and 8⁸⁴ ≡ 1 (mod 49). Then 6⁸³ + 8⁸³ ≡ 6⁻¹ + 8⁻¹ = (6 + 8)/(6·8) = 14/48. With 48 ≡ −1 (mod 49) this is −14 ≡ 35 (mod 49).

Method: Binomial trick

6 = 7 − 1 and 8 = 7 + 1. In the binomial expansion of (7 − 1)⁸³, every term with 7² or higher is a multiple of 49, so only two terms matter: (−1)⁸³ + 83 · 7 · (−1)⁸² = −1 + 581. Likewise (7 + 1)⁸³ leaves 1 + 83 · 7 = 1 + 581. Adding: 580 + 582 = 1162 ≡ 1162 − 49 · 23 = 35 (mod 49).

Method: Computer check

(6⁸³ + 8⁸³) mod 49 = 35.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Example 2.3.3 (AIME 1983)

Problem 2.3.5. (HMMT 2009) Find the last two digits of 1032¹⁰³². Give a two-digit number.

HintFind the remainder mod 4 and mod 25.
Answer76
Method: Chinese remainder theorem

1032 is divisible by 4, so 1032¹⁰³² ≡ 0 (mod 4). 1032 ≡ 7 (mod 25) and 7² = 49 ≡ −1, so 7¹⁰³² = (7²)⁵¹⁶ ≡ (−1)⁵¹⁶ = 1 (mod 25). The number ≡ 0 mod 4 and ≡ 1 mod 25 is 76 (mod 100).

Method: Computer check

pow(1032, 1032, 100) = 76.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Example 2.3.5 (HMMT 2009)

Problem 2.5.2. (AIME 2001) How many positive integer multiples of 1001 can be written as 10ʲ − 10ⁱ with 0 ≤ i < j ≤ 99?

Hint1001 | 10ʲ⁻ⁱ − 1 exactly when 6 divides j − i.
Answer784
Method: Order of 10 mod 1001

10³ ≡ −1 and 10⁶ ≡ 1 (mod 1001), and the order is 6. So 1001 divides 10ʲ − 10ⁱ exactly when 6 | j − i. Split 0…99 by remainder mod 6: residues 0 to 3 have 17 numbers each, residues 4 and 5 have 16 each. Choose two numbers from each class: 4 · C(17,2) + 2 · C(16,2) = 4 · 136 + 2 · 120 = 784.

Method: Computer check

Counting pairs 0 ≤ i < j ≤ 99 with 1001 | 10ʲ − 10ⁱ gives 784.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Example 2.5.2 (AIME 2001)

Problem lcm. Find lcm(36, 60). (Use gcd · lcm = product.)

Hintgcd(36, 60) = 12.
Answer180
Method: gcd × lcm

gcd(36, 60) = 12, so lcm = 36 · 60/12 = 180.

Method: Prime factors

36 = 2² · 3² and 60 = 2² · 3 · 5. Take the larger exponent of each prime: 2² · 3² · 5 = 180.

Source: Stevens, Olympiad Number Theory Through Challenging Problems, Corollary 1.3.1
