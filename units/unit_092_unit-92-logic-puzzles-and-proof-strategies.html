Reading

1. Direct proof, contrapositive and contradiction

A direct proof starts from the hypotheses and reaches the conclusion. The contrapositive of 'if P then Q' is 'if not Q then not P', which is logically equivalent and sometimes easier. To prove 'if n² is even then n is even', prove its contrapositive: if n is odd, n = 2k + 1, then n² = 4k² + 4k + 1 is odd. A proof by contradiction assumes the opposite of what you want and derives an absurdity.

Worked example. There are infinitely many primes. Suppose there are only p₁, …, pₙ. The number N = p₁p₂⋯pₙ + 1 leaves remainder 1 when divided by each pᵢ, so no pᵢ divides it; but N > 1 has a prime factor, which is not on the list. Contradiction.

Counterexamples. To disprove 'for all', give one example. The statement 'n² + n + 41 is prime for every n' fails at n = 40 (the value is 41²).

2. Knights and knaves

On an island, knights always tell the truth and knaves always lie. Puzzle 1. A says 'We are both knaves.' If A were a knight, the statement would be true, making A a knave: contradiction. So A is a knave, the statement is false, and so B is a knight.

Puzzle 2. A says 'B is a knave' and B says 'A and I are of the same type.' If A is a knight, B is a knave and B's statement is false (they are different types) ✓. If A is a knave, B is a knight, but then B's statement 'same type' would be false for a knight: contradiction. So A is a knight and B a knave.

Technique. Assume one person's type, follow the consequences, and eliminate cases. Make a table of cases when there are three or more people.

3. Cases, WLOG and pigeonhole

Proof by cases. Split into exhaustive cases: for any integer n, n² mod 3 ∈ {0, 1} (n ≡ 0, 1, 2 mod 3 gives 0, 1, 1). Without loss of generality (WLOG) reduces symmetric cases: if a statement is symmetric in x and y, assume x ≤ y.

Worked example. Prove that among any three integers, two have a sum divisible by 2: two of them have the same parity, so their sum is even.

Worked example 2. Prove that the product of two consecutive integers is even: one of the two is even.

Writing a proof. State what is given and what is to be shown; define notation; give each step with a reason; end by saying what has been shown.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Proving the converse instead of the statement. Testing examples and calling it a proof. Circular arguments. Dividing by zero in an algebraic proof.

Checks. Look for the step where each hypothesis is used; if one is never used, ask why.

Practice. Rewrite each claim as 'if …, then …' before trying to prove it.

Worked examples with Try-its: Knights and knaves

Knights always tell the truth and knaves always lie. Example. A says: 'We are both knaves.' If A were a knight the statement would be true, making A a knave, a contradiction. So A is a knave; the statement is then false, so they are not both knaves, so B is a knight. A brute-force check over all four assignments of knight/knave gives the single consistent case (A knave, B knight).

Try it

A says: 'At least one of us is a knave.' What are A and B?

Answer. A knight (the statement is true and B is a knave).

Worked examples with Try-its: Proof strategies

Contrapositive: 'if P then Q' is equivalent to 'if not Q then not P'. Contradiction: assume the opposite and derive an impossibility, as in the proof that √2 is irrational. Cases: split into exhaustive cases (even/odd, positive/negative). Induction: prove a base case and the step. Write the negation of a statement carefully: the negation of 'every box is red' is 'some box is not red'.

Try it

State the negation of: 'every student passed and some student got an A'.

Answer. Some student failed, or no student got an A.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. There are three boxes labelled Apples, Oranges and Mixed, and every label is wrong. You may draw one fruit from one box. How can you fix all labels? Solution. Draw from the box labelled Mixed. If you get an apple, that box is Apples; the box labelled Oranges cannot be Oranges or Apples, so it is Mixed, and the remaining one is Oranges.

Reflection. Using the assumption that every label is wrong cuts the possibilities from six to two.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Three speakers. A says 'B is a liar'; B says 'C is a liar'; C says 'A and B are both liars'. Try all 8 assignments of truth-teller or liar: only (A liar, B truthful, C liar) is consistent. Check: A's claim is false (B tells the truth), B's claim is true (C lies), and C's claim is false (B is not a liar).

Try it

What is the contrapositive of 'if n² is even then n is even'?

Answer. If n is odd then n² is odd.

Try it

Negate: 'every prime greater than 2 is odd'.

Answer. Some prime greater than 2 is even.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Prove that √2 is irrational. Solution. Suppose √2 = p/q in lowest terms. Then p² = 2q², so p² is even and hence p is even, p = 2m. Then 4m² = 2q², so q² = 2m² and q is even too, contradicting lowest terms.

Reflection. A proof by contradiction assumes the opposite and reaches an impossibility.

Practice problems (25)

Problem p1. On an island A says 'We are both knaves' (A and B are each a knight or a knave). How many of A and B are knights?

HintTest A as a knight.
Answer1
Method: Contradiction

If A were a knight the statement would be true, so A would be a knave, which is a contradiction. So A is a knave and the statement is false, meaning at least one is a knight: B. So exactly 1 knight.

Method: Table

Of the four assignments only (A knave, B knight) makes A's statement false as a knave's must be.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 2 (methods of proof)

Problem p2. A says 'exactly one of the three of us is a liar', B says 'exactly two of us are liars' and C says 'all three of us are liars'. Each is a truth-teller or a liar. How many are liars?

HintAt most one statement can be true.
Answer2
Method: Cases

The statements contradict each other, so at most one is true. C cannot be true (then C would be a liar), so if one is true it is A or B. If A is true there is 1 liar, but then two are truthful, contradiction. If B is true there are 2 liars, consistent. So 2 liars.

Method: Enumeration

Checking all 8 assignments, only one is consistent, with exactly 2 liars.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 2 (methods of proof)

Problem p3. Among 9 coins one is heavier. What is the fewest weighings on a balance that always finds it?

HintEach weighing has 3 outcomes.
Answer2
Method: Thirds

Weigh 3 against 3: either the heavy coin is on the heavier pan or among the 3 left aside. Then weigh 1 against 1 among 3. Two weighings suffice.

Method: Information

One weighing gives at most 3 outcomes, so at most 3 coins can be settled, but 9 = 3² needs 2.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 2 (methods of proof)

Problem p5. In the Monty Hall game you always switch. What is your probability of winning the car, as a fraction?

HintEnumerate car position and pick.
Answer2/3
Method: Enumeration

Switching wins exactly when your first pick was wrong, which has probability 2/3.

Method: Complement

Staying wins 1/3 of the time, so switching wins the rest: 2/3.

HintDraw from the 'Mixed' box.
Answer1

Problem p9. Prove-by-contradiction check: if the sum of two integers is odd, how many of them are odd?

HintParity.
Answer1
Method: Cases

If both were odd or both even the sum would be even, so exactly one is odd.

Method: Contradiction

Assume 0 or 2 are odd: the sum is even, contradicting oddness.

Problem p10. Five statements say: 'Exactly 1 of these statements is false', 'Exactly 2 are false', …, 'Exactly 5 are false'. How many of the five are false?

HintAt most one statement can be true.
Answer4
Method: Contradiction

Two different statements cannot both be true, so at most one is true. Then 4 or 5 are false; if 5 were false, the statement 'exactly 5 are false' would be true. So exactly 4 are false.

Method: Check

The true statement is 'Exactly 4 are false', consistent.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 2 (methods of proof)

Problem p11. There are 100 doors, all closed. For k = 1 to 100, person k toggles every kth door. How many doors are open at the end?

HintA door is toggled once per divisor.
Answer10
Method: Divisors

Door n is open when it has an odd number of divisors, i.e. when n is a square: 1, 4, …, 100, so 10.

Method: Simulation

Squares have an unpaired divisor √n.

Problem p14. Among the 4 true/false assignments to p and q, in how many is 'if p then q' false?

HintAn implication fails only when the premise is true and the conclusion false.
Answer1
Method: Table

Only p true and q false makes it false: 1.

Method: Equivalent

'p → q' is the same as 'not p or q', which fails only if p is true and q false.

Problem p16. In a group of 100 people, 60 speak English, 50 speak French and 20 speak neither. How many speak both?

HintInclusion-exclusion.
Answer30
Method: Union

80 speak at least one, and 60 + 50 − both = 80 gives both = 30.

Method: Venn

English only 30, French only 20, both 30, neither 20: total 100.

Problem p17. In how many of the 4 assignments to p and q is '(p and q) → p' true?

HintIt is a tautology.
Answer4
Method: Table

If p and q are true, p is true; otherwise the premise is false, so the implication is true. All 4.

Method: Meaning

A statement true under every assignment is a tautology.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 2 (methods of proof)

Problem x18. 77 objects are placed into 10 boxes. What is the largest number n for which some box must contain at least n objects?

Answer8

Problem x20. Two fair dice are rolled. Find the probability that the sum is 10.

HintCount the favourable outcomes out of 36.
Answer1/12
Method: Count

The pairs are (4, 6), (5, 5), (6, 4): 3 outcomes out of 36, so the probability is 1/12.

Method: Symmetry

The sums s and 14 − s are equally likely, so sum 10 has the same count as sum 4, namely 3 outcomes, giving 1/12.
