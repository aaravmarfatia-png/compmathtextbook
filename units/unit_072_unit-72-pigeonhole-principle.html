Reading

1. The pigeonhole principle and its generalisation

If n + 1 objects go into n boxes, some box has at least two. More generally, if N objects are put in k boxes, some box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. 100 people in 12 months: some month has at least ⌈100/12⌉ = 9 birthdays.

Worked example 1. Any 5 integers contain two whose difference is divisible by 4 (4 boxes: remainders 0–3).

Worked example 2. Choose 11 numbers from {1, …, 20}. Two of them are consecutive: pair the numbers as (1, 2), (3, 4), …, (19, 20), ten boxes; 11 numbers give two in one box.

Worked example 3. In any set of n integers some non-empty block of consecutive ones has sum divisible by n. Take the prefix sums S₁, …, Sₙ. If one is divisible by n, done; otherwise the n sums lie in n − 1 non-zero remainders, so two are equal, and their difference is a block sum divisible by n.

2. Designing the boxes

The art is choosing boxes so that two objects in one box have the property you want. Common choices: remainders, intervals, pairs, small squares in a square, residue classes of exponents.

Worked example 1. Among any 5 points in an equilateral triangle of side 1, two are within distance 1/2. Cut the triangle into four small equilateral triangles of side 1/2; two points fall in one of them.

Worked example 2. From {1, …, 2n}, any n + 1 chosen numbers include two that are coprime (consecutive numbers are coprime). Pair (1, 2), (3, 4), …: n boxes.

Worked example 3. From {1, …, 2n}, any n + 1 numbers include two where one divides the other. Write each as 2ᵏ × odd; there are n odd numbers below 2n; two share the same odd part.

3. The Erdős–Szekeres theorem

Any sequence of n² + 1 distinct numbers has an increasing subsequence of length n + 1 or a decreasing one of length n + 1. For n = 2, any 5 distinct numbers contain a monotone subsequence of length 3: for example 2, 1, 4, 3, 5 has 2, 4, 5 increasing. Proof: for each term write (i, d) = (longest increasing subsequence ending there, longest decreasing one ending there). If all i, d ≤ n there are only n² pairs, but there are n² + 1 terms, so two terms have the same pair, but two terms cannot have the same pair (if a < b with a earlier, then i(b) > i(a); if a > b, then d(b) > d(a)). Contradiction.

The 4-element case. With 4 numbers the claim fails: 2, 1, 4, 3 has no monotone subsequence of length 3. So n² + 1 is the exact threshold when n = 2.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Using ⌊N/k⌋ instead of ⌈N/k⌉. Not proving each object falls into some box. Boxes that are too many. Concluding which box rather than that some box is crowded.

Checks. Make sure you have exactly more objects than boxes.

Practice. Write 'objects = …, boxes = …' for each argument.

Worked examples with Try-its: The principle

If more than k·m objects are placed into m boxes, some box holds more than k objects. The simplest case: n + 1 objects in n boxes means two share a box. The general form: some box contains at least ⌈N/m⌉ objects. The skill lies in choosing the boxes. Example 1. Among any 13 people, two were born in the same month (12 boxes). Example 2. Among any n + 1 integers, two have a difference divisible by n, because there are only n remainders mod n.

Example 3. Choose 6 numbers from 1 to 10. Two must sum to 11. The boxes are the five pairs {1,10}, {2,9}, {3,8}, {4,7}, {5,6}; six numbers cannot avoid putting two in one pair (and numbers in one pair sum to 11). A search over all 210 six-element subsets confirms this.

Try it

How many socks must you take from a drawer of 3 colours in the dark to be sure of a matching pair?

Answer. 4 (3 boxes, so 4 socks).

Worked examples with Try-its: Cleverer boxes

Divisibility. Write each number as 2ᵏ times an odd number. From 1 to 20 there are only 10 odd parts, so among any 11 chosen numbers two have the same odd part, and then the smaller divides the larger. Geometry. Place 5 points in a unit square. Split the square into 4 squares of side 1/2; two points share a small square, so they are at most √2/2 ≈ 0.707 apart. Lattice points. Among any 5 lattice points two have the same parity pattern of (x, y) (4 patterns), so their midpoint is also a lattice point.

Try it

How many integers guarantee that two have a difference divisible by 4?

Answer. 5: two share a remainder mod 4, so their difference is divisible by 4 (4 integers would not be enough: 0, 1, 2, 3).

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Prove that among any 7 integers, two have a sum or difference divisible by 10.

Solution. Sort remainders mod 10 into the 6 boxes {0}, {1, 9}, {2, 8}, {3, 7}, {4, 6}, {5}. Seven numbers force two into one box. If the remainders are equal the difference is divisible by 10. If they are a pair such as 3 and 7 the sum is.

Reflection. The boxes were designed so that being in the same box implies the property.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Pairs. Choose 5 numbers from 1 to 8; two must sum to 9, because the pairs {1, 8}, {2, 7}, {3, 6}, {4, 5} are the boxes. Four numbers can avoid this (for instance 1, 2, 3, 4), so 5 is the least number that works (confirmed by checking every choice). Birthdays. Among 367 people two share a birthday, since there are at most 366 birthdays.

Try it

How many socks must be taken from 5 colours to guarantee 3 of the same colour?

Answer. 2 · 5 + 1 = 11.

Try it

Among any 4 integers, must two have a difference divisible by 3?

Answer. Yes: there are only 3 remainders, so two share one.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Five points are placed in an equilateral triangle of side 1. Show two of them are at most 1/2 apart. Solution. Joining the midpoints of the sides cuts the triangle into 4 equilateral triangles of side 1/2. Five points in 4 pieces put two in one piece, and any two points of a triangle of side 1/2 are at most 1/2 apart.

Reflection. Choose boxes small enough that the diameter of each box gives the bound.

Practice problems (25)

Problem p1. What is the smallest number of people that guarantees two share a birth month?

Hint12 boxes.
Answer13
Method: Boxes

12 months, so 13 people force a repeat, while 12 people can fill each month once.

Method: Worst case

The largest set with no repeat has 12 people, so 13 is the answer.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p2. A drawer has many black and many white socks. What is the smallest number you must take to guarantee a matching pair?

HintTwo colours.
Answer3
Method: Boxes

Two colours, so 3 socks must repeat a colour.

Method: Worst case

Two socks can be different (one of each), so 3 is the least.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p3. Numbers are chosen from 1, 2, …, 20. What is the smallest count that guarantees two chosen numbers add to 21?

HintPair x with 21 − x.
Answer11
Method: Pairs

The ten pairs (1,20), (2,19), …, (10,11) are boxes; 11 numbers force two from one pair, and these sum to 21.

Method: Sharpness

Choosing 1 through 10 gives ten numbers with no pair summing to 21, so 10 is not enough.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p4. What is the smallest n so that any n integers contain two whose difference is divisible by 4?

HintBoxes are the 4 residues mod 4.
Answer5
Method: Residues

There are 4 residue classes, so 5 integers share a class, and two in the same class have a difference divisible by 4.

Method: Sharpness

0, 1, 2, 3 have no two differing by a multiple of 4.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p5. How many cards from a standard 52-card deck must you take to guarantee 4 of the same suit?

HintAvoid 4 of a suit as long as possible.
Answer13
Method: Worst case

You can hold 3 of each of the 4 suits (12 cards) without 4 of one suit. The 13th card forces it.

Method: Pigeonhole

With 13 cards in 4 suits, some suit has at least ⌈13/4⌉ = 4.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p6. How many numbers must be chosen from 1, 2, …, 100 to guarantee that one divides another?

HintBoxes: numbers with the same odd part.
Answer51
Method: Odd part

Every number is 2ᵏ · m with m odd. There are 50 odd values of m, so 51 chosen numbers share an odd part, and then one divides the other.

Method: Sharpness

The numbers 51, …, 100 have no divisibility pair, so 50 is not enough.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p7. What is the smallest n so that any n integers contain two whose sum or difference is divisible by 10?

HintBoxes: residue classes {0}, {5}, {1,9}, {2,8}, {3,7}, {4,6}.
Answer7
Method: Boxes

The six boxes are {0}, {5}, {1,9}, {2,8}, {3,7}, {4,6}. With 7 integers two share a box, and then either the difference or the sum is a multiple of 10.

Method: Sharpness

The residues 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 contain no repeated residue and no pair summing to 10, so 6 integers are not enough.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p8. An equilateral triangle of side 3 is cut into smaller equilateral triangles of side 1. How many small triangles are there?

HintArea scales as the square of the side.
Answer9
Method: Area

The area ratio is 3² = 9, so there are 9 small triangles. Ten points in the big triangle then force two in one small triangle, within distance 1.

Method: Rows

The rows contain 1, 3, 5 small triangles: 1 + 3 + 5 = 9.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p9. Among any five points in a 2 × 2 square, two are within distance √2. How many unit squares are used as boxes?

HintCut the square into 1 × 1 squares.
Answer4
Method: Boxes

The four unit squares are the boxes. Two of five points share one, and any two points in a unit square are within √2 (the diagonal).

Method: Count

2 · 2 = 4 boxes.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p13. What is the smallest number of points in a square of side 2 that guarantees two are at distance at most √2?

HintBoxes of side 1 have diagonal √2.
Answer5
Method: Boxes

Four boxes, so 5 points force two in one box, at distance at most √2.

Method: Check

The four centres of the boxes are pairwise at distance at least 1, and the corners (0,0), (2,0), (0,2), (2,2) are 2 apart, so 4 points can avoid it.

Problem p14. From 1, 2, …, 30, what is the least number of integers you must pick to be sure two have a difference of 5?

HintGroup by remainder pattern.
Answer16
Method: Chains

Split into 5 chains by remainder mod 5, each of 6 numbers (like 1, 6, 11, 16, 21, 26). In a chain, at most 3 numbers can be picked with no two adjacent, so at most 15 numbers avoid a difference of 5.

Method: Result

So 16 numbers are forced to contain two differing by 5, while 15 can avoid it.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

Problem p15. Among any 7 integers, there are two whose difference is divisible by what largest guaranteed number?

HintUse remainders mod 6.
Answer6
Method: Pigeonhole

Among 7 integers, two share a remainder mod 6, so their difference is a multiple of 6.

Method: Sharpness

The 6 integers 0 to 5 have no difference divisible by 6, so 7 is minimal.

Problem x18. 50 objects are placed into 8 boxes. What is the largest number n for which some box must contain at least n objects?

HintPigeonhole principle.
Answer7
Method: Pigeonhole

⌈50/8⌉ = 7.

Method: Contradiction

If every box had at most 6 objects, there would be at most 8 · 6 = 48 < 50 objects in all.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 3 and Engel ch. 4

HintC(n, k).
