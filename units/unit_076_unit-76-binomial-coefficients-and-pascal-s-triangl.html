1. Identities of binomial coefficients

C(n, k) counts the k-element subsets of an n-set. Some identities, each with a counting proof: C(n, k) = C(n, n − k) (choose what to leave out); k·C(n, k) = n·C(n − 1, k − 1) (choose a committee and its chair in two ways); Σₖ C(n, k) = 2ⁿ (all subsets); Σₖ (−1)ᵏ C(n, k) = 0 for n ≥ 1; and the hockey stick Σᵢ₌ₖⁿ C(i, k) = C(n + 1, k + 1).

Worked example 1. Σ k·C(10, k) = 10 × 2⁹ = 5,120, by k·C(n, k) = n·C(n − 1, k − 1) summed.

Worked example 2. C(10, 5) = 252 = C(9, 4) + C(9, 5) = 126 + 126 ✓.

Worked example 3. Σ C(n, k)² = C(2n, n) (Vandermonde with m = n = r): for n = 3: 1 + 9 + 9 + 1 = 20 = C(6, 3) ✓.

2. Binomial coefficients and primes

For a prime p and 0 < k < p, p divides C(p, k), since C(p, k) = p!/(k!(p − k)!) has a p in the numerator but not in the denominator. Therefore (x + y)ᵖ ≡ xᵖ + yᵖ (mod p): the middle terms vanish. This gives another proof of Fermat's little theorem: (1 + 1 + … + 1)ᵖ ≡ 1 + … + 1, i.e. aᵖ ≡ a.

Lucas's theorem (parity). C(n, k) is odd exactly when every binary digit 1 of k is also a 1 in n. For n = 7 = 111₂, every k from 0 to 7 works: the row 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1 is all odd. For n = 8 = 1000₂ only k = 0 and 8 qualify: 1, 8, 28, 56, 70, 56, 28, 8, 1.

Counting the odd entries. Row n has 2^s odd entries, where s is the number of 1s in the binary form of n. For n = 6 = 110₂, s = 2: row 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1 has 4 odd entries ✓.

3. Counting with binomials

Paths on a grid. The number of paths from (0, 0) to (a, b) going only right or up is C(a + b, a). Subsets with a sum condition and distributions use stars and bars: C(n + k − 1, k − 1).

Worked example. A committee of 4 is chosen from 6 men and 5 women with at least one of each. Total C(11, 4) = 330; all men C(6, 4) = 15; all women C(5, 4) = 5; so 330 − 20 = 310.

Worked example 2. What is the coefficient of x⁵ in (1 + x)¹⁰(1 + x)⁵? The product is (1 + x)¹⁵, so the coefficient is C(15, 5) = 3,003. By Vandermonde it is Σ C(10, i)C(5, 5 − i).

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Using C(n, k) with k > n. Forgetting symmetry. Mis-indexing the hockey stick. Mixing combinations with permutations.

Checks. Evaluate small n by Pascal's triangle.

Practice. Give each identity a committee-counting story.

Worked examples with Try-its: Pascal's triangle

Each entry is the sum of the two above it: row 5 is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. The entry C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!) counts the ways to choose k objects from n, so C(10, 3) = 120. Row n adds up to 2ⁿ (choose any subset) and the alternating sum is 0. The Binomial Theorem says (x + y)ⁿ = ΣC(n, k)xⁿ⁻ᵏyᵏ; the coefficient of x³ in (1 + x)⁸ is C(8, 3) = 56, and the coefficient of x² in (2x + 3)⁴ is C(4, 2) · 2² · 3² = 216.

Try it

What is the coefficient of x² in (1 + 2x)⁵?

Answer. C(5, 2) · 4 = 40.

Worked examples with Try-its: Identities

Hockey stick. C(2, 2) + C(3, 2) + … + C(6, 2) = 1 + 3 + 6 + 10 + 15 = 35 = C(7, 3). Vandermonde. Choosing 3 people from 5 women and 4 men: Σ C(5, k)C(4, 3 − k) = 4 + 30 + 40 + 10 = 84 = C(9, 3). Parity. Row 7 of Pascal's triangle is entirely odd (8 odd entries), because 7 = 111 in binary; in general the number of odd entries in row n is 2 raised to the number of 1s in the binary form of n.

Try it

Evaluate C(5, 0) + C(5, 1) + … + C(5, 5).

Answer. 32.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. How many ways can a committee of 3 be chosen from 5 women and 4 men if it must include at least one man? Solution. Total C(9, 3) = 84 minus the all-women committees C(5, 3) = 10, giving 74.

Reflection. Complementary counting is usually the cleanest for 'at least one'.

More worked examples: Worked examples

C(6, 3) = 20; the coefficient of x⁴ in (1 + x)⁷ is C(7, 4) = 35; and C(n, 2) = 45 gives n(n − 1) = 90, so n = 10. In (x + 2)⁵ the coefficient of x² is C(5, 2) · 2³ = 80. The row sum Σ C(6, k) = 64.

Try it

What is the coefficient of x³ in (1 − x)⁶?

Answer. −C(6, 3) = −20.

Try it

How many subsets of {1, …, 6} have an even number of elements?

Answer. 32 (half of 64).

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Find the middle term of (a + b)⁸. Solution. The middle term is the fifth, C(8, 4)a⁴b⁴ = 70a⁴b⁴.

Check. The row 1, 8, 28, 56, 70, 56, 28, 8, 1 has 70 in the centre.
