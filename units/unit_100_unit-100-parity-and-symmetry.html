Reading

1. Parity: even and odd

Parity means whether an integer is even or odd. The rules: even ± even = even, odd ± odd = even, even ± odd = odd, even × anything = even, odd × odd = odd. The sum of k odd numbers has the parity of k. Because parity survives addition and multiplication, it is a powerful invariant: a quantity that a process never changes.

Worked example 1. The numbers 1, 2, …, 10 are written down (sum 55, which is odd). Repeatedly erase two numbers a and b and write |a − b|. Since a + b and |a − b| have the same parity, the parity of the total never changes, so after nine steps the single remaining number is odd. It cannot be 0.

Worked example 2. Can 1, 2, …, 2023 be split into two groups of equal sum? The total is 2023 × 2024/2 = 2023 × 1012, which is even, so parity does not forbid it (and it can be done). Compare 1, …, 2022: the total 2022 × 2023/2 = 1011 × 2023 is odd, so equal sums are impossible.

2. Colouring and pairing arguments

Many parity arguments hide behind a colouring. Colour a chessboard in the usual way. A domino always covers one black and one white square. Remove two opposite corners of an 8 × 8 board: they have the same colour, so 32 black and 30 white remain (or the reverse), and the 62 squares cannot be tiled by 31 dominoes.

Pairing. If objects come in pairs with each pair contributing an even amount, the total is even. A graph always has an even number of vertices of odd degree, because the sum of degrees equals twice the number of edges.

Worked example. At a party of 15 people, can each person shake hands with exactly 3 others? The degree sum would be 45, which is odd, but it must equal twice the number of handshakes. So it is impossible.

3. Symmetry

A configuration is symmetric when a transformation (reflection, rotation, swapping variables) maps it to itself. Symmetry can cut a problem in half. A function with f(−x) = f(x) is even; one with f(−x) = −f(x) is odd. The integral or sum of an odd function over a symmetric range is 0.

Symmetric polynomials. If a polynomial is unchanged when x is replaced by 1/x after dividing by a power of x (a palindromic polynomial) then roots come in pairs r, 1/r (Unit 128).

Worked example. Counting palindromic numbers. A 3-digit palindrome has the form aba with a from 1 to 9 and b from 0 to 9: 9 × 10 = 90 of them. A 4-digit palindrome abba: also 90. The number of 5-digit palindromes is 9 × 10 × 10 = 900.

Using symmetry in a sum. For x + y = 10 the product xy is largest when the two numbers are equal (x = y = 5, product 25), since by symmetry the extremum must be at the symmetric point.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Concluding that something is possible just because parity does not forbid it. Parity only proves impossibility. Assuming a symmetric answer is the extremum without proof.

Checks. After finding an invariant, test it on a tiny example.

Practice. When stuck on 'is it possible?', compute some quantity mod 2 before and after one step.

Worked examples with Try-its: Parity

Even and odd numbers behave like 0 and 1 mod 2: odd + odd = even, odd × odd = odd. The sum 1 + 2 + … + n is odd when n ≡ 1 or 2 (mod 4). Lamps. 100 lamps start off; on pass k you flip every k-th lamp, for k = 1, …, 100. Lamp n is flipped once for each divisor of n, so it ends on exactly when n has an odd number of divisors, which happens only for perfect squares: 10 lamps (1, 4, 9, …, 100).

Try it

Can you cover a 5 × 5 board with 1 × 2 dominoes?

Answer. No: 25 is odd.

Worked examples with Try-its: Symmetry

Pairing terms exploits symmetry: 1 + 2 + … + 100 = 50 · 101, by pairing k with 101 − k. A symmetric expression in x and y can often be rewritten in x + y and xy. In games, a mirror strategy uses the symmetry of the starting position.

Try it

Evaluate 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + … − 100.

Answer. −50.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Is it possible to arrange the numbers 1 to 9 in a row so that the sum of any two adjacent numbers is even? Solution. Adjacent numbers must have the same parity, so all would be the same parity along the row, but there are 5 odd and 4 even numbers: impossible.

Reflection. A parity argument often rules a configuration out in one line.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Splitting a set. The numbers 1 to 10 cannot be split into two groups with equal sums, because the total 55 is odd. The sum 1 + 2 + … + n is even exactly when n ≡ 0 or 3 (mod 4): n = 3, 4, 7, 8, 11, 12, … Symmetry. The sum of cos θ over the six 6th roots of unity pairs each root with its conjugate and equals 0.

Try it

Can 1 to 7 be split into two groups of equal sum?

Answer. The total is 28, so each group needs 14: Yes: {7, 5, 2} and {1, 3, 4, 6} both sum to 14.

Try it

Is it possible for a knight to move from a1 to a2 in exactly 6 moves?

Answer. No: each knight move changes the square colour, so after an even number of moves the knight is on the same colour; a1 and a2 are different colours.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. There are 7 glasses, all upright. In one move you flip exactly 4 glasses. Can you turn all of them upside down? Solution. Each move changes the number of upside-down glasses by an even number (flipping a upright glasses and 4 − a upside-down ones changes the count by a − (4 − a) = 2a − 4, which is even). Starting with 0 upside-down, the number is always even, but 7 is odd: impossible.

Reflection. Parity of the count is the invariant.

Problem p1. For how many n from 1 to 20 can the set {1, 2, …, n} be split into two groups with equal sums?

HintThe total n(n + 1)/2 must be even.
Answer10
Method: Parity

n(n + 1)/2 is even exactly when n ≡ 0 or 3 (mod 4): n = 3, 4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 16, 19, 20, which is 10 values. For these n a split exists.

Method: Check

For n = 4 take {1, 4} and {2, 3}; for n = 3 take {3} and {1, 2}.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 14 (Further Strategies)

Problem p2. A knight starts on a1 of an 8 × 8 board. What is the fewest moves needed to reach a2?

HintEach move changes the square's colour.
Answer3
Method: Parity

a1 and a2 have different colours, so the number of moves is odd; one move is impossible, so at least 3.

Method: Path

For example a1 → c2 → b4 → a2 uses 3 moves.

Problem p3. Seven odd integers are added. Is the sum odd (answer 1) or even (answer 0)?

Answer1
Method: Parity

Seven odd terms give an odd sum: 1.

Method: Pairs

Six of them pair up to even sums and one odd term remains.

Problem p4. If x + 1/x = 3, find x² + 1/x².

HintSquare both sides.
Answer7
Method: Square

x² + 2 + 1/x² = 9, so x² + 1/x² = 7.

Method: Direct

x = (3 ± √5)/2 gives x² + 1/x² = 7 since the two roots are reciprocals.

Problem p7. Find the sum of all positive integers up to 100 that are coprime to 100.

HintPair k with 100 − k.
Answer2000
Method: Pairing

k and 100 − k are both coprime to 100, so the numbers pair up with sum 100 each. There are φ(100) = 40 of them, so the total is 20 · 100 = 2000.

Method: Check

The average of such numbers is 50, so 40 · 50 = 2000.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 14 (Further Strategies)

Problem p8. At a party of 15 people each person shakes hands with exactly k others. For how many k from 1 to 14 is this possible?

HintThe degree sum 15k must be even.
Answer7
Method: Parity

15k is even only when k is even, so k = 2, 4, …, 14: 7 values. Each of these is achievable with a suitable regular graph.

Method: Reason

An odd number of people cannot all have an odd degree.

Problem p9. Each of the 4 vertices of a square is coloured black or white. How many colourings are different up to rotation?

HintCount necklaces.
Answer6
Method: List

By the number of black vertices: 0, 1, 2 (adjacent), 2 (opposite), 3, 4 gives 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 6.

Method: Burnside

(16 + 2 + 4 + 2)/4 = 6, averaging the fixed colourings over the four rotations.

Problem p10. Can a 5 × 5 board be tiled with 1 × 2 dominoes? Answer 1 for yes and 0 for no.

HintCount the squares.
Answer0
Method: Parity

The board has 25 squares, an odd number, but dominoes cover 2 each. So no: 0.

Method: Colouring

A chessboard colouring gives 13 and 12 squares, while every domino covers one of each colour.

Problem p14. How many subsets of {1, 2, …, 10} have an even sum?

HintToggle element 1.
Answer512
Method: Pairing

Toggling 1 in or out flips the parity of the sum, pairing up all 2¹⁰ subsets, so half of them: 512.

Method: Count

Both parities are equally likely: 1024/2 = 512.

Answer8

Method: Step by step

Problem x19. 25 objects are placed into 6 boxes. What is the largest number n for which some box must contain at least n objects?

HintPigeonhole principle.
Answer5
Method: Pigeonhole

⌈25/6⌉ = 5.

Method: Contradiction

If every box had at most 4 objects, there would be at most 6 · 4 = 24 < 25 objects in all.
