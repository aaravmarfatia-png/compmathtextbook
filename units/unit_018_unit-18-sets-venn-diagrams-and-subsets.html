Reading

1. Sets and notation

A set is a collection of distinct objects, called its elements. We write the elements in braces: A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. The statement '3 ∈ A' says 3 belongs to A, and '9 ∉ A' says it does not. Order and repetition do not matter: {1, 2, 3} and {3, 2, 1, 1} are the same set. The number of elements is written |A| (here 6). The set with no elements is the empty set ∅, and its size is 0.

Describing sets. We can list them or describe a rule: {n : n is a positive multiple of 3 below 20} = {3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18}. A set B is a subset of A, written B ⊆ A, if every element of B is also in A. Every set is a subset of itself and ∅ is a subset of every set.

The operations. The union A ∪ B contains everything in A or B (or both); the intersection A ∩ B contains what is in both; the difference A − B contains what is in A but not B; the complement of A (inside a fixed universal set U) contains everything of U outside A. With A = {1,…,6} and B = {4,…,8}: A ∪ B = {1,…,8}, A ∩ B = {4, 5, 6}, A − B = {1, 2, 3} and B − A = {7, 8}.

The size formula. When we count |A ∪ B| by adding |A| + |B| the elements of A ∩ B are counted twice, so |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. Check: 6 + 5 − 3 = 8 ✓. De Morgan's laws. The complement of A ∪ B equals (complement of A) ∩ (complement of B), and the complement of A ∩ B equals (complement of A) ∪ (complement of B). In words: 'not (A or B)' is 'not A and not B'. With U = {1,…,10}, the elements outside A ∪ B are 9 and 10, which are indeed outside both sets.

Try it

If A = {2, 4, 6, 8, 10} and B = {3, 6, 9}, find A ∪ B, A ∩ B and A − B.

Answer. A ∪ B = {2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 10}; A ∩ B = {6}; A − B = {2, 4, 8, 10}.

2. Venn diagrams with two sets

A Venn diagram draws each set as a circle inside a rectangle for the universe. The overlap of two circles is the intersection; the region outside both is 'neither'. The golden rule: fill in the innermost region first (the overlap), then work outwards, subtracting as you go.

Worked example 1. In a class of 40, 25 play music, 18 play sport and 7 do neither. How many do both? The 7 who do neither are outside the circles, so 40 − 7 = 33 are in at least one. Then |M ∪ S| = |M| + |S| − |M ∩ S| gives 33 = 25 + 18 − x, so x = 10. Fill in the diagram: both 10, music only 25 − 10 = 15, sport only 18 − 10 = 8, neither 7; the total 10 + 15 + 8 + 7 = 40 ✓.

Worked example 2 (only one). How many students do exactly one of the two activities? Music only plus sport only = 15 + 8 = 23. The common mistake is to say 25 + 18 = 43, which counts the 10 who do both twice and is bigger than the class.

Worked example 3 (a missing total). In a group of 50 people, 30 speak French, 20 speak German and 8 speak both. How many speak neither? The union has 30 + 20 − 8 = 42 people, so 50 − 42 = 8 speak neither.

Try it

Of 60 students, 35 like tea, 28 like coffee and 12 like neither. How many like both?

Answer. Union: 60 − 12 = 48. 35 + 28 − x = 48, so x = 15.

3. Three sets and inclusion–exclusion

For three sets the diagram has 7 regions plus the outside. Adding |A| + |B| + |C| counts the pairwise overlaps twice and the triple overlap three times; subtracting the three pairwise intersections removes the triple overlap three times too many, so we add it back: |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|.

Worked example 1 (divisibility). How many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2, 3 or 5? |A| = 50, |B| = 33, |C| = 20; pairs: multiples of 6: 16, of 10: 10, of 15: 6; triple: multiples of 30: 3. So 50 + 33 + 20 − 16 − 10 − 6 + 3 = 74. The remaining 100 − 74 = 26 integers are divisible by none of 2, 3, 5 (a computer count agrees).

Worked example 2 (a survey). In a group of 60, 30 like A, 28 like B, 25 like C; 10 like both A and B, 8 both A and C, 9 both B and C, and 4 like all three. Then everybody likes at least one, since 30 + 28 + 25 − 10 − 8 − 9 + 4 = 60. The number liking exactly two: pairs minus three times the triple: (10 + 8 + 9) − 3 × 4 = 15. The number liking only A: 30 − 10 − 8 + 4 = 16.

Strategy. Fill the Venn diagram from the centre out: first the triple overlap, then each pairwise region (the pairwise total minus the centre), then the single-set regions (the set total minus all the regions already inside it). The final check is that all eight region counts add up to the universe.

Try it

How many integers from 1 to 30 are divisible by neither 2 nor 3?

Answer. Divisible by 2: 15; by 3: 10; by 6: 5. Union: 15 + 10 − 5 = 20. Neither: 30 − 20 = 10.

4. Subsets and counting them

A set with n elements has 2ⁿ subsets: for each element, choose in or out. For {1,2,3,4,5} that is 32 subsets, of which 31 are non-empty. The number of subsets of size k is the combination C(n, k); for instance there are C(5, 2) = 10 two-element subsets.

Subsets with conditions. Subsets of {1,…,6} that contain 1 but not 2: those two elements are decided, the other four are free: 2⁴ = 16. Subsets that do not contain both 1 and 2: the subsets containing both number 2⁴ = 16 and the total is 2⁶ = 64, so the answer is 64 − 16 = 48.

Pairs of nested subsets. How many ordered pairs (A, B) of subsets of {1, 2, 3} have A ⊆ B? Each element has three possible fates: in both, in B only, or in neither. So 3³ = 27 pairs (confirmed by listing all 64 pairs).

Sum of the subset sums. Each element of {1, 2, 3, 4} lies in exactly 2³ = 8 of the 16 subsets, so the total of all subset sums is 8 × (1 + 2 + 3 + 4) = 80. The same idea works for any set: every element appears in half of all subsets.

Subsets with even sum. Of the 2¹⁰ = 1024 subsets of {1, …, 10}, exactly half have even sum: pair each subset with the one that differs only in the element 1; they have sums of opposite parity. So 512 subsets have even sum, as brute force confirms.

Try it

How many subsets of {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7} contain exactly three elements, including the element 7?

Answer. Choose the other 2 elements from the remaining 6: C(6, 2) = 15.

5. Subsets with no two adjacent elements

A classic problem: how many subsets of {1, 2, …, n} contain no two consecutive numbers? Let f(n) be the answer. For n = 1 the subsets are ∅ and {1}: f(1) = 2. For n = 2 they are ∅, {1}, {2}: f(2) = 3. In general split by whether n is in the subset: if n is not in it, the subset is any good subset of {1,…,n−1}, giving f(n − 1); if n is in it, then n − 1 is not, and the rest is a good subset of {1,…,n−2}, giving f(n − 2). Hence f(n) = f(n − 1) + f(n − 2), which is the Fibonacci recurrence.

The sequence begins f(1) = 2, f(2) = 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55. A listing by computer for n up to 8 confirms 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55. So {1, …, 8} has 55 such subsets. Counting only those with exactly k elements gives C(n − k + 1, k): for n = 8 and k = 3, C(6, 3) = 20.

Why the lesson matters. Many 'no two adjacent' problems — seating people, choosing non-attacking positions on a line, tiling a strip — reduce to the same recurrence. See Unit 57 for sequences and recurrences.

Try it

How many subsets of {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} have no two consecutive elements?

Answer. f(5) = 13, using f(3) = 5, f(4) = 8.

6. A longer problem

Problem. In a school of 120 students, 70 study French, 60 study Spanish and 40 study German. 30 study French and Spanish, 20 French and German, 15 Spanish and German, and 8 study all three. (a) How many study at least one language? (b) How many study none? (c) How many study exactly one? (d) How many study exactly two?

Solution. (a) 70 + 60 + 40 − 30 − 20 − 15 + 8 = 113. (b) 120 − 113 = 7 study none. (c) Only French: 70 − 30 − 20 + 8 = 28; only Spanish: 60 − 30 − 15 + 8 = 23; only German: 40 − 20 − 15 + 8 = 13. Exactly one: 28 + 23 + 13 = 64. (d) Exactly two: (30 + 20 + 15) − 3 × 8 = 41. Check: 64 + 41 + 8 (all three) = 113 ✓.

Reflection. The consistency check — the region counts add to the inclusion–exclusion total — is the key habit. If a region comes out negative, the data are contradictory.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Counting the overlap twice (or forgetting to subtract it). (2) Forgetting the 'neither' region. (3) Reading 'French and Spanish: 30' as the number who do only those two. (4) Using 2ⁿ for subsets of a given size. (5) Forgetting the empty set. (6) Mixing up A − B and B − A.

Checks. Add all regions of the diagram and compare with the universe. Make sure each region is non-negative. Test a tiny case where you can list the sets.

How to practise. Draw a Venn diagram for every problem, even easy ones, and fill it in from the centre. In Unlimited practice, rotate the set and counting families together.

Problem s1. A = {2, 4, 6, 8} and B = {2, 4, 5, 7}. How many elements does A ∪ B have?

HintUnion: everything in either, no repeats.
Answer6
Method: List

A ∪ B = {2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}: six elements.

Method: Formula

#A + #B − #(A ∩ B) = 4 + 4 − 2 = 6.
