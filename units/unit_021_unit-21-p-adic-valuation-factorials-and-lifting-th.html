Reading

1. Valuations: counting prime factors

For a prime p and a non-zero integer n, the p-adic valuation vₚ(n) is the exponent of the largest power of p dividing n. So v₂(40) = 3 because 40 = 2³ · 5, v₃(40) = 0, and v₅(40) = 1. It converts divisibility into inequalities: p^k divides n exactly when vₚ(n) ≥ k.

Three basic rules. (1) vₚ(ab) = vₚ(a) + vₚ(b): products add valuations. (2) vₚ(a + b) ≥ min(vₚ(a), vₚ(b)), with equality when the two valuations are different. (3) If a and b are both divisible by p^k then so is a ± b. Rule (2) is the engine behind many proofs: if you add a number divisible by a high power of 3 to one divisible by exactly one factor of 3, the sum has exactly one factor of 3. Compare gcd and lcm: vₚ(gcd(a, b)) = min and vₚ(lcm(a, b)) = max. For a = 2⁵ · 3² · 7 and b = 2³ · 3⁴ · 5, the gcd is 2³ · 3² = 72 and the lcm is 2⁵ · 3⁴ · 5 · 7.

Using valuations to solve problems. Equations among integers become comparisons of exponents: compare v₂ on both sides, then v₃, and so on. This is the prime-by-prime thinking of Unit 9, but now with a name and a strategy.

Try it

Find v₂, v₃ and v₅ of 360 and of 1800.

Answer. 360 = 2³ · 3² · 5: (3, 2, 1). 1800 = 2³ · 3² · 5²: (3, 2, 2).

2. Legendre's formula: valuations of factorials

How many factors of 5 does 100! contain? Count multiples of 5 below 100: 20 numbers, each giving at least one factor; of those 4 are multiples of 25 and give a second factor. So v₅(100!) = 20 + 4 = 24. This is Legendre's formula: vₚ(n!) = ⌊n/p⌋ + ⌊n/p²⌋ + ⌊n/p³⌋ + … (the sum stops when p^k exceeds n). Similarly v₂(100!) = 50 + 25 + 12 + 6 + 3 + 1 = 97, and v₃(30!) = 10 + 3 + 1 = 14. All three values agree with direct computation.

Trailing zeros. Each trailing zero in n! needs a factor 10 = 2 · 5, and factors of 2 are more plentiful than factors of 5, so the number of trailing zeros equals v₅(n!). For 100! it is 24; for 1000! it is 200 + 40 + 8 + 1 = 249; for 2023! it is ⌊2023/5⌋ + ⌊2023/25⌋ + ⌊2023/125⌋ + ⌊2023/625⌋ = 404 + 80 + 16 + 3 = 503.

Skipped values. The number of trailing zeros jumps by more than 1 whenever n is a multiple of 25. The value 5 is never attained: 24! has 4 zeros and 25! has 6. In fact 6 is attained for n = 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, and a computer search confirms that no n has exactly 5 zeros.

The digit-sum form. If sₚ(n) is the sum of the base-p digits of n, then vₚ(n!) = (n − sₚ(n))/(p − 1). For n = 100 in base 2 (1100100₂, digit sum 3): v₂(100!) = 100 − 3 = 97 ✓.

Try it

How many zeros does 150! end with?

Answer. ⌊150/5⌋ + ⌊150/25⌋ + ⌊150/125⌋ = 30 + 6 + 1 = 37.

3. Binomial coefficients and Kummer

Valuations of C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n − k)!) follow from Legendre's formula by subtracting. For p = 2: v₂(C(100, 50)) = v₂(100!) − 2 v₂(50!) = 97 − 2 × 47 = 3. Kummer's theorem says that vₚ(C(n, k)) equals the number of carries when adding k and n − k in base p. For C(10, 5) = 252 = 4 · 63 we have v₂ = 2, and indeed adding 5 + 5 in binary (101 + 101) produces two carries: the units digit 1 + 1 = 10 carries once, the middle digit 0 + 0 + 1 = 1 does not carry, and the leading digit 1 + 1 = 10 carries again.

Why it is useful. It tells you at once that C(2ⁿ, k) is even for 0 < k < 2ⁿ, that C(p, k) is divisible by p for a prime p and 0 < k < p, and that C(n, k) is odd only when the binary digits of k are 'contained' in those of n. For instance C(9, 3) has v₃ = 1 (9 = 100₃ and adding 3 + 6 = 010₃ + 020₃ in base 3 produces one carry).

Try it

What is v₂(C(8, 4)) and how can you see it from Kummer's theorem?

Answer. C(8, 4) = 70 = 2 · 35, so v₂ = 1. Adding 4 + 4 in binary (100 + 100) carries once.

4. Lifting the exponent

The Lifting the Exponent lemma (LTE) computes vₚ(xⁿ − yⁿ). For an odd prime p dividing x − y, and p not dividing x or y: vₚ(xⁿ − yⁿ) = vₚ(x − y) + vₚ(n). Example. v₃(7³ − 1) = v₃(6) + v₃(3) = 1 + 1 = 2 (indeed 342 = 2 · 3² · 19). v₃(7⁹ − 1) = 1 + 2 = 3. v₅(6⁵ − 1) = v₅(5) + v₅(5) = 2 and v₅(11²⁵ − 1) = 1 + 2 = 3 (all verified by direct computation).

The sum version. For odd n and p dividing x + y: vₚ(xⁿ + yⁿ) = vₚ(x + y) + vₚ(n). For example v₃(2⁹ + 1) = v₃(3) + v₃(9) = 3, and v₃(2²⁷ + 1) = 1 + 3 = 4.

The prime 2. For even n and odd x, y: v₂(xⁿ − yⁿ) = v₂(x − y) + v₂(x + y) + v₂(n) − 1. For 3⁴ − 1 = 80: v₂(2) + v₂(4) + v₂(4) − 1 = 1 + 2 + 2 − 1 = 4 ✓ (80 = 16 · 5). For 5⁶ − 1 = 15 624: 2 + 1 + 1 − 1 = 3 ✓ (15 624 = 8 · 1953).

Warning. The hypotheses matter: p must divide x − y and must not divide x or y. A very common error is to apply the lemma when p does not divide the difference. Test the lemma on a small case before trusting it.

Try it

Find v₃(10⁹ − 1).

Answer. 3 divides 10 − 1 = 9 and v₃(9) = 2; v₃(9) = 2 again for the exponent n = 9. So v₃(10⁹ − 1) = 2 + 2 = 4, in agreement with 999 999 999 = 3⁴ · 37 · 333 667.

5. Using valuations in proofs

The smallest exponent. What is the smallest n with 3³ dividing 7ⁿ − 1? By LTE (since 3 | 7 − 1 and 3 does not divide 7), v₃(7ⁿ − 1) = 1 + v₃(n), so we need v₃(n) ≥ 2 and n = 9 is the smallest; a search confirms n = 9. In general 3^k divides 7ⁿ − 1 exactly when 3^(k − 1) divides n.

Is n! ever a perfect square? For n > 1 there is a prime p between n/2 and n (Bertrand's postulate), and then vₚ(n!) = 1 is odd, so n! is not a square. A computer check confirms that no n! with 2 ≤ n < 60 is a square. A perfect square has even valuation at every prime, so a single odd valuation suffices to prove that a number is not a square.

Equal valuations. To show that 2ⁿ + 1 is not a perfect cube for large n, or that two expressions cannot be equal, compare their valuations at a convenient prime. Strategy: pick a prime p, compute vₚ of each side, and show the values are different.

Divisibility of 2ⁿ + 1 by 3. By the sum form (x = 2, y = 1, x + y = 3 and n odd) 3 divides 2ⁿ + 1 for every odd n, while for even n we have 2ⁿ ≡ 1 mod 3 so 2ⁿ + 1 ≡ 2: no. Both facts match a computer check for n up to 20.

Try it

What is the smallest n such that 2¹⁰ divides n!?

Answer. v₂(n!) ≥ 10. v₂(11!) = 5 + 2 + 1 = 8 and v₂(12!) = 6 + 3 + 1 = 10, so n = 12.

6. A longer problem

Problem. Let N = 100!. (a) How many trailing zeros has N? (b) What is the largest power of 12 dividing N? (c) What is the largest k such that 6ᵏ divides N?

Solution. (a) 24 (from v₅ = 24 and v₂ = 97). (b) 12 = 2² · 3, so 12ᵏ divides N if 2k ≤ 97 and k ≤ v₃(100!). v₃(100!) = 33 + 11 + 3 + 1 = 48. The binding constraint is 2k ≤ 97, k ≤ 48; so k = 48. (c) 6ᵏ = 2ᵏ 3ᵏ needs k ≤ 97 and k ≤ 48, so k = 48.

Reflection. The general method: factor the base, compute the valuation of N at each prime of the base with Legendre's formula, and take the minimum of the ratios ⌊vₚ(N)/e⌋ over the primes p with exponent e in the base.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Stopping Legendre's sum too early (forgetting ⌊n/p²⌋ and higher terms). (2) Using LTE when p does not divide x − y, or when p divides x or y. (3) Forgetting the −1 and the extra term in the p = 2 case. (4) Counting only multiples of 5 for trailing zeros and forgetting multiples of 25 and 125. (5) Assuming vₚ(a + b) = min when the valuations are equal (then it can be larger).

Checks. Test every formula on a small case you can factor by hand. For trailing zeros, check that the answer jumps by 2 at multiples of 25.

How to practise. Make a table of vₚ(n!) for p = 2, 3, 5, 7 and n up to 30 and verify it by factoring. In Unlimited practice, drill the valuation and trailing-zero families until the floor sums are automatic.
