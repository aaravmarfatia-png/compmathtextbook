Aigner & Ziegler, Proofs from THE BOOK, ch. 1 (primes), 2 (Bertrand), 4 (sums of two squares), 12 (Euler's formula), 30 (Cayley); Gelca & Andreescu 6.1.4 and 5.2.5 (Wilson).

Reading

1. Classics: Euclid, Bertrand, Wilson, Fermat

Euclid. If p₁, …, pₖ are primes, N = p₁⋯pₖ + 1 has a prime factor outside the list: 2·3·5 + 1 = 31 is prime; 2·3·5·7·11·13 + 1 = 30031 = 59 · 509. Wilson. (p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p), so (p − 3)! ≡ (p − 1)/2: for p = 7, 4! = 24 ≡ 3 ✓. Fermat. p ≡ 1 mod 4 is a sum of two squares: 13 = 2² + 3².

Try it

Smallest prime factor of 2·3·5·7 + 1?

Answer. 211 is prime, so 211.

Try it

Write 29 as a sum of two squares.

Answer. 25 + 4.

2. Graphs and Euler's formula

A connected plane graph has V − E + F = 2. A simple planar graph with V ≥ 3 has at most 3V − 6 edges. Cayley: nⁿ⁻² labelled trees; for n = 4 that is 16. Erdős–Szekeres: any (k − 1)² + 1 distinct numbers contain a monotone subsequence of length k; for k = 3 that is 5 numbers.

Try it

Faces of a connected planar graph with 6 vertices and 9 edges?

Answer. 5.

Try it

Labelled trees on 3 vertices?

Answer. 3.

3. A worked problem

Problem. What is the largest number of edges in a simple planar graph on 6 vertices? Solution. 3·6 − 6 = 12, achieved by the octahedron graph (6 vertices, 12 edges, 8 triangular faces).

Check. 6 − 12 + 8 = 2 ✓.

4. In the books

Aigner & Ziegler (4th ed.). Ch. 1 gives six proofs of the infinitude of primes (the first is Euclid's p₁⋯pₖ + 1 argument), ch. 2 Bertrand's postulate, ch. 4 representing numbers as sums of two squares (a prime p ≡ 1 mod 4 is a sum of two squares), ch. 12 three applications of Euler's formula (with Pick's theorem), ch. 25 pigeon-hole and double counting, ch. 30 Cayley's formula for the number of trees, ch. 36 Turán's theorem.

Erdős–Szekeres is not in this edition of the book; it appears in Putnam and Beyond and the IMO Compendium. Wilson's theorem is in Putnam and Beyond 5.2.5, Khurmi and 104 Number Theory Problems.

Honest note. The book gives full proofs; this unit only practises the statements with numbers, so it is a companion to reading the proofs, not a substitute.

Problem p1. Let N be the product of all primes up to 5, plus 1. What is the smallest prime factor of N?

HintEuclid's argument: N has a prime factor larger than 5, but which?
Answer31
Method: Factor

N = 31 = 31; its smallest prime factor is 31.

Method: Trial division

N is not divisible by any prime up to 5, so test the primes beyond 5 in order: 31 is the first divisor.

Source: Theorems from Aigner & Ziegler ch. 1, 2, 4, 12, 30 (original problem)

Problem p4. Find the smallest prime p with 100 < p < 200 (Bertrand's postulate says one exists).

HintCheck the numbers after 100 one by one.
Answer101
Method: Search

Test 101, 102, … for primality; the first prime is 101.

Method: Sieve

Sieve the numbers from 101 to 200 with the primes up to 14; the first survivor is 101.

Problem p7. How many labelled trees are there on 5 vertices?

HintCayley's formula: nⁿ⁻².
Answer125
Method: Cayley

5^3 = 125.

Method: Prüfer codes

Trees correspond to sequences of length 3 with entries from 5 labels: 5^3 = 125.

Problem p10. What is the smallest length L such that every sequence of L distinct numbers has an increasing or a decreasing subsequence of length 4?

HintErdős–Szekeres: (k − 1)² + 1.
Answer10
Method: Theorem

Every sequence of (k − 1)² + 1 numbers works, and a (k − 1) × (k − 1) block arrangement shows (k − 1)² does not: 10.

Method: Pigeonhole

Label each term by (longest increasing run ending there, longest decreasing run ending there); with at most 3·3 labels, 10 terms force a repeat, which is impossible, so L = 10.

Source: Theorems from Aigner & Ziegler ch. 1, 2, 4, 12, 30 (original problem)

Problem p13. A connected planar graph has 8 vertices and 12 edges. How many faces (including the outer one) does a plane drawing have?

HintEuler: V − E + F = 2.
Answer6
Method: Euler's formula

F = 2 − V + E = 6.

Method: Build up

A tree with 8 vertices has 1 face; each of the 5 extra edges adds one face, giving 6.

Source: Theorems from Aigner & Ziegler ch. 1, 2, 4, 12, 30 (original problem)

Problem p16. What is the largest possible number of edges in a simple planar graph with 8 vertices?

HintEvery face has at least 3 edges; combine with Euler's formula.
Answer18
Method: Face count

2E ≥ 3F and V − E + F = 2 give E ≤ 3V − 6 = 18.

Method: Triangulation

A triangulated planar graph achieves it: 3·8 − 6 = 18.

Problem p19. The prime 13 is the sum of two squares a² + b² with 0 < a < b. Find ab.

HintPrimes ≡ 1 (mod 4) are sums of two squares (Fermat–Euler).
Answer6
Method: Search

Test 13 − a² for a = 1, 2, …; it is a square only for a = 2 (giving b = 3), so ab = 6.

Method: Gaussian integers

13 = (2 + 3i)(2 − 3i), so 13 = 2² + 3² and ab = 6.

Source: Theorems from Aigner & Ziegler ch. 1, 2, 4, 12, 30 (original problem)

Problem p23. Find (7 − 3)! mod 7.

HintWilson: (p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p).
Answer3
Method: Wilson

(p − 1)! = (p − 1)(p − 2)(p − 3)! ≡ 2·(p − 3)! ≡ −1, so (p − 3)! ≡ (p − 1)/2 = 3.

Method: Direct

Multiply 1·2·…·4 reducing mod 7: 3.

Source: Theorems from Aigner & Ziegler ch. 1, 2, 4, 12, 30 (original problem)
