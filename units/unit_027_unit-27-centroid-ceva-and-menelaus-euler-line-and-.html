1. The medians and the centroid

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. The three medians of any triangle meet in a single point, the centroid G, and G divides each median in the ratio 2 : 1, counting from the vertex. In coordinates the centroid is the average of the vertices. For the triangle with A = (5, 12), B = (0, 0), C = (14, 0) (sides 13, 14, 15 and area 84) we get G = (19/3, 4), which I confirmed by intersecting two medians with exact fractions.

Physical meaning. G is the balance point: a triangular plate balances on a pin at G. It is also the average position of the three vertices, so the three triangles GAB, GBC and GCA all have the same area, one third of the whole: here 28, 28, 28 out of 84.

Median lengths. The median from A to side a has length mₐ with mₐ² = (2b² + 2c² − a²)/4. With a = 14, b = 15, c = 13 we get mₐ² = (450 + 338 − 196)/4 = 148, and a direct distance computation gives the same. The sum of the squares of the three medians is three quarters of the sum of the squares of the sides: 148 + mb² + mc² = (3/4)(196 + 225 + 169) = 442.5 (verified exactly).

Distances from G. The sum GA² + GB² + GC² equals (a² + b² + c²)/3, which is 590/3 here. This identity is used to show that G minimises the sum of squared distances to the vertices.

Try it

A triangle has vertices (0, 0), (6, 0), (0, 9). Find its centroid and the length of the median from the origin.

Answer. G = (2, 3). The midpoint of the opposite side is (3, 4.5), so the median from the origin has length √(9 + 20.25) = √29.25 ≈ 5.41, and the vertex-to-G distance is √13 ≈ 3.61, which is two thirds of it.

2. Ceva's theorem

Let D, E, F be points on the sides BC, CA, AB of triangle ABC (strictly inside the sides). The cevians AD, BE, CF are concurrent (meet in a single point) if and only if (BD/DC) · (CE/EA) · (AF/FB) = 1. The mnemonic: go round the triangle A → B → C and take the ratio at each side in the same direction.

Example 1 (medians). All three ratios are 1, the product is 1, so the medians are concurrent. Example 2 (angle bisectors). By the angle bisector theorem BD/DC = AB/AC = 13/15, CE/EA = BC/BA = 14/13, AF/FB = CA/CB = 15/14; the product is 1, so the bisectors meet, and the meeting point is the incentre (6, 4). Example 3 (altitudes). The foot of the altitude from A is (5, 0), so BD = 5 and DC = 9; the other feet give the remaining ratios and the three altitudes meet at (5, 15/4), which I found by intersecting two altitudes and checking that the third passes through the same point.

Example 4 (the Gergonne point). The incircle touches the sides at points whose tangent lengths from the vertices are 7, 6, 8 (Unit 26). So BD = 6, DC = 8, CE = 8, EA = 7, AF = 7, FB = 6 and the product (6/8)(8/7)(7/6) = 1: the lines joining the vertices to the points of contact are concurrent, at the Gergonne point.

Building concurrency. If BD/DC = 2/3 and CE/EA = 3/4, then AF/FB must be 2 for the cevians to be concurrent. I constructed the three points with exact arithmetic and confirmed that the three lines are concurrent; with AF/FB = 1 instead, the third line misses the common point.

Try it

In triangle ABC, D lies on BC with BD/DC = 1/2, E lies on CA with CE/EA = 4/3. If the cevians AD, BE, CF are concurrent, find AF/FB.

Answer. AF/FB = 1/((1/2)(4/3)) = 3/2.

3. Menelaus' theorem

Ceva deals with three cevians through a point. Menelaus deals with a straight line crossing the three side lines (possibly extended). If a line meets lines BC, CA, AB at X, Y, Z, then using signed ratios, (BX/XC) · (CY/YA) · (AZ/ZB) = −1. In unsigned lengths the product is 1, with either one or three of the points lying on extensions of sides, outside the triangle.

Example. Take Y on CA with CY/YA = 1/2 and Z on AB with AZ/ZB = 3. The line YZ meets BC at X with BX/XC = −2/3 (since (BX/XC)(1/2)(3) = −1). A computation with the actual coordinates gives X = (−28, 0): BX = −28 and XC = 42 so BX/XC = −2/3 ✓. The negative sign says that X lies outside the segment BC, on the extension beyond B.

Uses. Menelaus proves that three points are collinear (the product of ratios is −1) in the same way that Ceva proves concurrency. It is also a useful tool for computing ratios along a transversal: area problems with a line cutting the three sides often reduce to one application.

Duality. Ceva: product = +1 and lines concurrent. Menelaus: product = −1 and points collinear. A common mistake is to confuse the two. Check the sign convention by testing a simple case: the three midpoints of the sides have ratios 1, 1, 1 (product 1), so they correspond to Ceva-type concurrency of the medians; they are not collinear.

Try it

A line meets BC at X, CA at Y and AB at Z with unsigned ratios BX/XC = 2, CY/YA = 3/4. Find AZ/ZB.

Answer. Unsigned product 1: AZ/ZB = 1/(2 · 3/4) = 2/3.

4. The Euler line

The circumcentre O, centroid G and orthocentre H of any triangle lie on one line, the Euler line, with G between O and H and OG : GH = 1 : 2. For the 13-14-15 triangle: O = (7, 33/8), G = (19/3, 4), H = (5, 15/4). Check: G − O = (−2/3, −1/8) and H − G = (−4/3, −1/4), exactly twice as large and in the same direction. So collinearity and the 1 : 2 ratio both hold.

The vector identity. With the circumcentre as origin, H = A + B + C (as vectors) and G = (A + B + C)/3, so H = 3G. That is a short proof that O, G, H are collinear with OH = 3 · OG.

Special triangles. In an equilateral triangle O = G = H. In an isosceles triangle the Euler line is the axis of symmetry. In a right triangle H is the right-angle vertex and O is the midpoint of the hypotenuse, so the Euler line is the median to the hypotenuse.

Using it. If you know two of O, G, H you can find the third: H = 3G − 2O. In our triangle 3G − 2O = (19, 12) − (14, 33/4) = (5, 15/4) ✓.

Try it

A triangle has circumcentre (0, 0) and centroid (1, 2). Where is its orthocentre?

Answer. H = 3G − 2O = (3, 6).

5. The nine-point circle

A single circle passes through nine special points of a triangle: the three midpoints of the sides, the three feet of the altitudes, and the three midpoints of the segments joining the orthocentre to the vertices. It is the nine-point circle. Its centre N is the midpoint of OH and its radius is R/2.

Verification. For our triangle, N = ((7 + 5)/2, (33/8 + 15/4)/2) = (6, 63/16) and R/2 = 65/16. I computed the squared distances from N to all nine points using exact fractions — midpoints of BC, CA, AB; feet (5, 0) and the other two feet; midpoints of AH, BH, CH — and every one equals (65/16)². So the nine points really are concyclic.

Why the radius is R/2. The nine-point circle is the image of the circumcircle under the homothety with centre G and ratio −1/2, which sends A to the midpoint of BC, and sends H to O's image N. This is also why N lies on the Euler line: it is the midpoint of OH.

Applications. The nine-point circle is tangent to the incircle and to the three excircles (Feuerbach's theorem). In olympiad problems it often explains why certain feet and midpoints are concyclic without any angle chasing.

Try it

A triangle has circumradius 10. What is the radius of its nine-point circle, and what is the distance from its centre to the circumcentre if the orthocentre is 12 from the circumcentre?

Answer. Radius 5. N is the midpoint of OH, so ON = 12/2 = 6.

6. A longer problem

Problem. In triangle ABC with AB = 13, BC = 14, CA = 15 (coordinates as above): (a) Show that the bisectors, medians and altitudes are all concurrent and find the coordinates of each meeting point. (b) Show that the Gergonne point lies inside the triangle and find its coordinates.

Solution. (a) Centroid (19/3, 4), incentre (6, 4), orthocentre (5, 15/4): all are found by intersecting two cevians and checking the third. Ceva gives concurrency for each family, since the ratio products are 1: medians (1, 1, 1), bisectors (13/15 · 14/13 · 15/14 = 1), altitudes with BD = 5, DC = 9, etc. (b) The Gergonne point is the intersection of AD, BE, with D, E the touch points; solving with exact fractions gives (414/73, 288/73), and the third line CF passes through it. Because all three cevians are inside the triangle for an acute triangle, the point is interior.

Reflection. The same triangle supplied eight remarkable points (G, I, O, H, N, Gergonne, and so on) and each confirmed a theorem. Whenever you meet a new theorem in geometry, test it on a concrete triangle with integer coordinates like this one.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Writing the Ceva ratios in inconsistent directions (use the cyclic order A → B → C throughout). (2) Using Ceva for lines that are not cevians. (3) Forgetting the minus sign convention in Menelaus. (4) Believing that G, I, O, H all lie on one line (only O, G, H, N do). (5) Misplacing the 2 : 1 division of the median.

Checks. Test every claimed concurrency numerically with coordinates. Check that ratio products come out to exactly 1. Check that the centroid lies inside the triangle.

How to practise. Put a triangle on coordinates with integer vertices and compute the centres. In Unlimited practice, rotate the centroid, Ceva and Menelaus families.

Problem p7. In triangle ABC, AB = 12, AC = 18 and BC = 20. The bisector of angle A meets BC at D. Find BD.

Answer8

Triangles ABD and ACD have the same height from A, so their areas are in the ratio BD : DC. Also D is equally far from AB and AC (it lies on the bisector), so the areas are in the ratio AB : AC. Hence BD/DC = 12/18 and BD = 8.
