1. Colouring as a tool

A colouring proof paints the cells of a board (or points of a set) in colours chosen to expose a hidden counting fact. The most famous example is the chessboard with two opposite corners removed: it has 62 squares, which is even, so one might hope to tile it with 31 dominoes. But opposite corners have the same colour, so the board now has 30 squares of one colour and 32 of the other, while every domino covers exactly one square of each colour. Hence no tiling exists. A computer search over all tilings of the full 6 × 6 board with the same two corners removed also finds none, while the intact 6 × 6 board can be tiled.

The recipe. (1) Notice a count that every piece must respect (a domino covers one black and one white). (2) Choose a colouring that makes this count visible. (3) Show that the region has the wrong totals. The colouring can have more than two colours: for 1 × 3 strips, colour cell (r, c) by (r + c) mod 3, because each strip covers one cell of each colour. On an 8 × 8 board the three colours have 21, 22 and 21 cells, which are unequal, so the full board cannot be tiled by 1 × 3 strips (and 64 is not a multiple of 3 anyway).

Example (a quick impossibility). A 3 × 3 board cannot be tiled by dominoes since it has 9 (odd) cells. But 3 × 3 with the centre removed has 8 cells and can be tiled (a computer finds a tiling: the ring of 8 cells can be paired off going round). Colouring only gives a necessary condition for tiling; it never proves that a tiling exists.

Try it

Can a 5 × 5 board be tiled by dominoes? Can a 4 × 4 board with two opposite corners removed?

Answer. 5 × 5 has 25 cells, odd: no. 4 × 4 with two opposite corners removed has 14 cells, 6 of one colour and 8 of the other, so no.

2. Tilings that do exist

To prove a tiling exists you must construct it. The simplest case is a rectangle with an even number of cells: lay dominoes in rows. How many tilings are there? A 2 × n strip has 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 tilings for n = 1, …, 5 (Fibonacci numbers; the last column holds one vertical domino or two horizontal ones). A 3 × 4 rectangle has 11 tilings and a 4 × 4 has 36 (both counted by a computer search).

Deficient boards with trominoes. A 2ⁿ × 2ⁿ board with one cell removed can always be tiled by L-shaped trominoes (three cells in an L). The count works: 4ⁿ − 1 is divisible by 3. The proof is by induction: split the board into four quarters, one of which contains the missing cell; place a single L-tromino at the centre covering one cell from each of the other three quarters; now every quarter has exactly one missing cell, so apply the induction hypothesis four times. I checked the 4 × 4 case with several different missing cells by exhaustive search.

The lesson. A construction by induction splits the problem into smaller copies of itself. When a proof by colouring shows only that a counting condition holds, look for an induction or a direct pattern.

Try it

Why is the 4 × 4 board with any one cell removed tileable by L-trominoes in principle, even before you find a tiling?

Answer. The board has 15 cells, a multiple of 3, and the induction above applies with n = 2.

3. The pigeonhole principle

If you put more than n objects into n boxes, some box contains at least two objects. This is the pigeonhole principle. Among any 13 people, two share a birth month; among 367 people, two share a birthday. The stronger form: if N objects go into k boxes, some box has at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects.

Example 1 (remainders). Among any 5 integers, two differ by a multiple of 4: there are only 4 possible remainders modulo 4. Example 2 (consecutive). Choose any 7 numbers from 1, …, 12. Two of them are consecutive: the pairs {1, 2}, {3, 4}, …, {11, 12} are 6 boxes, and 7 numbers must put two in one box (an exhaustive search over all choices confirms this, and shows that 6 numbers can avoid consecutive pairs, e.g. 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11).

Example 3 (one divides another). Choose any 7 numbers from 1, …, 12. Two of them are such that one divides the other. Write every number as 2ᵏ times an odd number m; there are 6 odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, so 7 numbers share an odd part, and the smaller divides the larger. (A search over all choices confirms.)

Example 4 (a block with a given sum). Among any n integers a₁, …, aₙ some consecutive block has sum divisible by n. Look at the n + 1 partial sums 0, a₁, a₁ + a₂, …; two of them are congruent modulo n; their difference is the sum of a block.

Try it

Prove that in any group of 6 people there are two with the same number of friends within the group, if friendship is mutual and nobody is their own friend.

Answer. Each person has between 0 and 5 friends, but a person with 0 friends and a person with 5 friends cannot both exist (the second would be friends with everyone, including the first). So only 5 values are available for 6 people: two people share a value.

4. Geometric pigeonholes

Points in a square. Place 5 points in a unit square. Divide the square into 4 quarter-squares of side 1/2. Two points lie in one quarter, so their distance is at most √2/2 ≈ 0.707, the diagonal of a quarter. In a 3 × 3 square with 10 points, divide it into nine unit squares: two points lie within √2 of each other.

Choosing boxes. The art of pigeonhole geometry is to choose the boxes so that two objects in the same box have the property you want. Typical boxes: sub-squares, equilateral triangles of half the side, angular sectors, or intervals of residues. The boxes must be chosen before you look at the points and must cover the whole region.

Colourings of the plane. Colour every point of the plane red or blue. Then two points at distance 1 have the same colour: take an equilateral triangle of side 1; two of its three vertices have the same colour. It is a pigeonhole on three vertices and two colours.

Try it

Prove that among any 5 points in an equilateral triangle of side 1 there are two at distance at most 1/2.

Answer. Cut the triangle into 4 equilateral triangles of side 1/2 (joining midpoints). Two of the 5 points lie in the same small triangle, so their distance is at most 1/2.

5. Monotone subsequences

The Erdős–Szekeres theorem. Any sequence of n² + 1 distinct numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length n + 1 or a decreasing subsequence of length n + 1. For n = 3: any 10 distinct numbers in a row contain an increasing run of 4 or a decreasing run of 4 (selected non-adjacent items allowed). I checked 300 random sequences of 10 distinct numbers and every one had such a subsequence.

Proof idea. For each term aᵢ let Lᵢ be the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at aᵢ and Dᵢ be the same for decreasing. If aᵢ < aⱼ with i < j then Lⱼ > Lᵢ; if aᵢ > aⱼ then Dⱼ > Dᵢ. So the pairs (Lᵢ, Dᵢ) are all different. If both L and D were at most n, there would be only n² possible pairs for n² + 1 terms, a contradiction.

Sharpness. Nine numbers need not have such a subsequence: 3, 2, 1, 6, 5, 4, 9, 8, 7 has longest increasing and decreasing runs of length 3.

Try it

Why does the sequence 3, 2, 1, 6, 5, 4, 9, 8, 7 have no monotone subsequence of length 4?

Answer. Any increasing subsequence uses at most one number from each block of three (the blocks are decreasing), so its length is at most 3. Any decreasing subsequence stays within one block (blocks increase in size from left to right), so its length is also at most 3.

6. A longer problem

Problem. (a) Show that among any 7 integers, two have a sum or a difference divisible by 10. (b) Show that any 5 points in a 2 × 2 square include two at distance at most √2. (c) Show that in a 6 × 6 board with two opposite corners removed, no domino tiling exists.

Solution. (a) Look at the remainders mod 10 as boxes: {0}, {1, 9}, {2, 8}, {3, 7}, {4, 6}, {5}: six boxes for 7 numbers; two share a box, so their sum is divisible by 10 (when the remainders are r and 10 − r), or their difference is (when the remainders are equal). (b) Cut the square into four unit squares: two points share one, and the diagonal of a unit square is √2. (c) Colour the board like a chessboard: the corners have the same colour, leaving 16 cells of one colour and 18 of the other, while each domino covers one of each colour. A computer search over all tilings of this board finds none.

Reflection. Part (a) is a pure pigeonhole; part (c) is a pure colouring. Notice that in both cases the boxes or colours were chosen by looking at what the pieces or operations preserve.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Using a colouring to claim that a tiling exists. (2) Counting a pigeonhole with the wrong number of boxes. (3) Choosing boxes that do not cover every case. (4) Using 'more than n objects' where n boxes exist but some boxes may be empty (that is fine; the principle still holds). (5) Forgetting that the principle gives existence only; it does not tell which two objects share a box.

Checks. Count boxes and objects twice. For a colouring, count the cells of each colour. Test the claim on a small board.

How to practise. For every pigeonhole problem write 'boxes: … objects: …' before anything else. In Unlimited practice, rotate through the colouring, tiling and pigeonhole families.

Problem p1. Two opposite corners are cut off an 8×8 chessboard. Can the remaining 62 squares be covered exactly by 31 dominoes (each covers two neighbouring squares)? Answer yes or no.

HintColour the board like a chessboard. What colour are opposite corners?
Answerno
Method: Two-colouring

Every domino covers one black and one white square. Opposite corners have the same colour, so after removing them there are 30 squares of one colour and 32 of the other. 31 dominoes would need 31 of each: impossible.

Method: Computer search

An exhaustive search for a domino tiling of the 62 squares finds none.

Problem p2. After cutting off two opposite corners of an 8×8 board, how many more squares has the majority colour than the minority colour?

HintOpposite corners share a colour.
Answer2
Method: Count

The board has 32 black and 32 white. Both removed corners are the same colour, say white: 30 white and 32 black, a difference of 2.

Method: Reason with corners

Corner (1,1) and corner (8,8) have 1 + 1 = 2 and 8 + 8 = 16, both even: the same colour. Removing two of one colour gives a difference of 2.

Problem p6. Can a 10×10 board be covered by 25 straight tetrominoes (1×4 pieces)? Answer yes or no.

HintColour square (r, c), counted from 0, with (r + c) mod 4.
Answerno

A straight piece, lying either way, covers four consecutive values of r + c, so exactly one square of each colour. 25 pieces would cover 25 squares of each colour. Problem p8. For a, b from 1 to 8, count the pairs (a, b) such that an a×b rectangle can be tiled with 1×3 strips.

HintTileable exactly when 3 divides a or 3 divides b.
Answer28
Method: The rule

An a×b rectangle can be covered by 1×n strips exactly when n divides a or n divides b. Pairs where neither a nor b is a multiple of 3: 6·6 = 36 (a, b ∈ {1,2,4,5,7,8}), so 64 − 36 = 28 pairs work.

Method: Computer check

Tiling every case by exhaustive search gives the same 28.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 2 (Coloring Proofs) problem 8

Problem p9. A beetle sits on each square of a 9×9 board. At a signal each beetle moves diagonally to a neighbouring square. What is the smallest possible number of squares that end up empty?

HintMatch squares to the squares they can land on.
Answer9
Method: Counting by colour

Colour columns alternately. Beetles in odd columns (5 columns × 9 = 45 beetles) move into even columns and beetles in even columns (36) move into odd columns. Squares in even columns: 36. So the 45 beetles land on at most 36 squares, leaving at least 9 empty. This is reachable.

Method: Matching by computer

Treat each square as a target and find a largest set of targets that can be given distinct beetles. The largest matching has 72 targets, so 81 − 72 = 9 squares are empty.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 2 (Coloring Proofs) problem 12

Problem p10. What is the smallest n such that every red/blue colouring of a 3×n grid of points has a rectangle (sides along the grid) whose four corners are all the same colour?

HintEach column is a pattern of 3 colours; two columns can clash.
Answer7
Method: Pigeonhole

Each column has 3 points, so at least 2 of them share a colour. There are 3 pairs of rows, and 2 colours, so 6 possible (pair, colour) labels. With 7 columns, two columns get the same label, giving a monochromatic rectangle. For n = 6 a colouring with no such rectangle exists.

Method: Computer check

Trying all 8⁶ and 8⁷ column patterns confirms a bad-free colouring for 6 columns but none for 7.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 2 (Coloring Proofs) problem 13 (generalisation)
