Reading

1. The two basic rules: add or multiply

Counting means finding how many ways something can happen without listing every case. Two rules do almost all the work. The addition rule: if a task can be done in one of two exclusive ways, with m possibilities for the first and n for the second, the total is m + n. A lunch of one main course chosen from 4 meat dishes or 3 vegetarian dishes has 4 + 3 = 7 options. The multiplication rule: if a task is done in successive steps, with m ways for the first step and n ways for the second whatever the first was, then there are m × n ways altogether. A lunch with one of 4 starters, one of 6 mains and one of 3 desserts has 4 × 6 × 3 = 72 possible meals.

The slogan is: 'or' means add, 'and then' means multiply. Why does multiplication work? Draw a tree: the first step has m branches, each of which splits into n branches, giving m × n leaves. The rule is only valid when the number of options at the second step does not depend on the first choice (or if it depends, the dependence must be the same for every first choice).

Worked example 1 (codes). A bike lock has 4 dials with digits 0–9. The number of codes is 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10 000. If digits cannot repeat there are 10 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 5040 codes. Worked example 2 (licence plates). Three letters followed by two digits: 26³ × 10² = 17 576 × 100 = 1 757 600 plates. Worked example 3 (outfits). With 5 shirts, 4 pairs of trousers and 3 hats the number of outfits using a shirt and trousers and a hat is 60; if you may also choose not to wear a hat there are 5 × 4 × 4 = 80.

Counting subsets. Each of the n elements of a set is either in a subset or not: 2 choices each, so there are 2ⁿ subsets, including the empty set and the whole set. For 10 elements that is 1024 subsets.

Try it

How many 4-letter 'words' (any letters) can be made from A, B, C, D, E if no letter may repeat, and if repeats are allowed?

Answer. No repeats: 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 = 120. With repeats: 5⁴ = 625.

2. Counting numbers and digits

Digits are the classic training ground. For a three-digit number the first digit cannot be 0: 9 choices (1–9), then 10 choices for each of the others, so 9 × 10 × 10 = 900 three-digit numbers (check: 100 to 999 is 900 numbers). Even three-digit numbers: the last digit is 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, so 9 × 10 × 5 = 450 (half of all, as you would expect). Divisible by 5: last digit 0 or 5: 9 × 10 × 2 = 180.

No repeated digits. The first digit has 9 choices, the second any of the 9 remaining digits (including 0, now allowed), the third 8: 9 × 9 × 8 = 648, which a computer count of 100 through 999 confirms. For four-digit numbers with all digits different: 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 4536, and there are 9000 four-digit numbers in all, so 4464 have a repeated digit.

Restrictions first. When a position has a restriction, fill it first. How many four-digit numbers with all digits different are even? Case last digit 0: the first three digits can be chosen from the remaining 9 digits in 9 × 8 × 7 = 504 ways. Case last digit is 2, 4, 6 or 8 (4 choices): the first digit cannot be 0 or the chosen last digit, 8 choices; the second 8 (anything not used, 0 now allowed); the third 7: 4 × 8 × 8 × 7 = 1792. Total 504 + 1792 = 2296.

The general principle. Choose the most restricted position first, then the next, and so on; each time count what is still available. If this leads to messy cases, switch to counting the complement (section 5).

Try it

How many 3-digit numbers are divisible by 5 and have all digits different?

Answer. Last digit 0: first two digits from 9 × 8 = 72 ways. Last digit 5: first digit not 0 or 5: 8 choices, second digit not used: 8 choices: 64. Total 72 + 64 = 136.

3. Factorials and arrangements

In how many ways can 5 different books be arranged on a shelf? The first place has 5 choices, the second 4, and so on: 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. This product has a name, 5 factorial, written 5!. In general n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1, with the convention 0! = 1. Factorials grow very fast: 10! = 3 628 800.

Arranging some of the objects. The number of ways to arrange k objects chosen from n different ones in a row is n × (n − 1) × … × (n − k + 1) = n!/(n − k)!. For example the number of ways to award gold, silver and bronze among 8 runners is 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 = 8!/5!. Such arrangements where order matters are called permutations.

Repeated objects. The 6 letters of BANANA can be arranged in 6! = 720 orders if we treat the three As as different and the two Ns as different. But swapping the As among themselves does not change the word, so each distinct word has been counted 3! × 2! = 12 times. The number of distinct words is 720/12 = 60 (confirmed by listing them by computer). Likewise MISSISSIPPI has 11!/(4! 4! 2!) = 34 650 arrangements (four Is, four Ss, two Ps).

Circular arrangements. Seating n people around a round table: rotations of the same seating count as the same, so fix one person's seat and arrange the rest: (n − 1)!. For 5 people: 4! = 24.

Try it

In how many ways can the letters of LETTER be arranged?

Answer. 6 letters with E twice and T twice: 6!/(2! 2!) = 720/4 = 180.

4. Arrangements with conditions

Two people together. In how many ways can 5 people stand in a row if two of them (A and B) must be adjacent? Glue A and B into a single block: there are 4 objects to arrange, 4! = 24 ways, and inside the block A and B can swap: ×2. Total 48. Check by computer: of the 120 arrangements exactly 48 have A and B adjacent. The number where they are not adjacent is 120 − 48 = 72.

People who must not be somewhere. How many arrangements of 5 people have person 1 not first and person 5 not last? Use inclusion–exclusion on the 'bad' events: total 120; person 1 first: 24; person 5 last: 24; both: 3! = 6. Good arrangements = 120 − 24 − 24 + 6 = 78. A check by listing all 120 arrangements gives exactly 78.

Alternating or separated groups. 3 boys and 2 girls stand in a row with no two girls adjacent. First arrange the boys: 3! = 6 ways, creating 4 gaps (before, between, after); choose 2 of the 4 gaps for the girls, in order: 4 × 3 = 12. Total 6 × 12 = 72 (compare the 48 arrangements where the girls are adjacent: 72 + 48 = 120 ✓).

Derangements. The number of arrangements of 4 objects with nobody in their original place is 9 (found by listing or by inclusion–exclusion): 24 − 4 × 6 + 6 × 2 − 4 × 1 + 1 = 9.

Try it

How many ways can 6 people sit in a row if A and B must not sit next to each other?

Answer. Total 720. A and B adjacent: 2 × 5! = 240. Not adjacent: 720 − 240 = 480.

5. Complementary counting

Sometimes it is easier to count what you do not want and subtract from the total. If a condition like 'at least one' is awkward, its opposite 'none' is usually a single simple case. Worked example 1. How many 3-letter strings from the 26-letter alphabet contain at least one vowel (A, E, I, O, U)? All strings: 26³ = 17 576. Strings with no vowel: 21³ = 9261. So 17 576 − 9261 = 8315 contain at least one vowel.

Worked example 2. How many 4-digit numbers have at least one repeated digit? All 4-digit numbers: 9000. Those with all digits different: 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 4536. Hence 9000 − 4536 = 4464 have a repeat. Worked example 3. A password has 4 digits (10 000 possible). How many contain at least one 7? 10 000 − 9⁴ = 10 000 − 6561 = 3439.

When to use it. Phrases like 'at least', 'not all', 'at most' often signal complement counting. Check the arithmetic by a small case: for 2 digits, at least one 7: 100 − 81 = 19 (07, 17, …, 97 is 10 numbers ending… direct count: 7 as tens digit: 10 numbers; as units digit: 10; both counted 77 twice: 19 ✓).

Try it

How many 5-letter strings using only A and B contain at least one A?

Answer. All 2⁵ = 32; with no A: 1 (BBBBB). So 31.

6. Counting divisors

Prime factorisation (Unit 9) turns divisor questions into multiplication-rule questions. A divisor of 360 = 2³ · 3² · 5 is of the form 2^a 3^b 5^c with a from 0 to 3, b from 0 to 2, c from 0 to 1, independently, so there are 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 divisors. Conditions on divisors. Divisors that are multiples of 6 must contain at least one 2 and one 3: a ∈ {1, 2, 3}, b ∈ {1, 2}, c any: 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 (confirmed by counting). Divisors that are perfect squares have even exponents: a ∈ {0, 2}, b ∈ {0, 2}, c = 0: 2 × 2 × 1 = 4 (1, 4, 9, 36).

Number of ways to write n as a product of two factors. Since divisors pair up as d and n/d, 360 has 24/2 = 12 unordered pairs (no square issue since 360 is not a perfect square). For 36 = 2² · 3² there are 9 divisors, and 4 unordered pairs plus the square pair 6 × 6 gives (9 + 1)/2 = 5 ways.

Worked example. How many positive integers divide 7! = 5040 = 2⁴ · 3² · 5 · 7? (4 + 1)(2 + 1)(1 + 1)(1 + 1) = 5 × 3 × 2 × 2 = 60 divisors.

Try it

How many positive divisors does 2 · 3² · 5³ have, and how many of them are odd?

Answer. (1 + 1)(2 + 1)(3 + 1) = 24 divisors. Odd ones omit the factor 2: 3 × 4 = 12.

7. A longer problem

Problem. A committee room has a row of 7 seats. (a) In how many ways can 7 different people sit? (b) If two people refuse to sit next to each other? (c) If a particular person must be at one of the two ends? (d) How many seatings have the tallest and shortest people at the two ends?

Solution. (a) 7! = 5040. (b) Count the complement: with the two together, 2 × 6! = 1440; not together: 5040 − 1440 = 3600. (c) Choose the end (2 ways), then arrange the other 6 in 6! = 720 ways: 2 × 720 = 1440. (d) The tallest and shortest take the ends in 2 ways, the other five fill the middle in 5! = 120 ways: 240.

Reflection. Each part used a different tool: the multiplication rule, complement, 'choose the restricted element first', and gluing. A good habit is to estimate the answer's size first: part (c) should be around 2/7 of all seatings, 5040 × 2/7 = 1440 ✓.

8. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Adding where you should multiply, or the reverse. (2) Forgetting that the first digit of a number cannot be 0. (3) Counting arrangements with identical objects as if they were different. (4) Double counting when cases overlap (use inclusion–exclusion). (5) Treating a circular arrangement like a row. (6) Forgetting the empty set or whole set when counting subsets.

Checks. Test the method on a tiny case that you can list: if the problem has 5 objects, solve the version with 3. Estimate the size of the answer. Count the same thing a second way.

How to practise. For each problem decide which steps are 'and then' and which are 'or'. In Unlimited practice, work on the counting families (digits, arrangements, divisors) until you can set up the product in under a minute.

Problem c1. How many integers are there from 45 to 317 inclusive?

HintSubtract and add 1.
Answer273
Method: Subtract and add 1

317 − 45 + 1 = 273. (Between them, exclusive, there are 271; adding both endpoints gives 273.)

Method: Shift the range

Subtract 44 from every number: 45 … 317 becomes 1 … 273, which has 273 numbers.

Problem c2. In how many ways can six coins come up when flipped?

HintEach coin has 2 outcomes independently.
Answer64
Method: Multiplication principle

2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 = 2⁶ = 64.

Method: Doubling

1 coin: 2 outcomes. Adding a coin doubles the list (put H or T on the end of each): 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.

Source: Rusczyk, The Art of Problem Solving Vol. 1, Chapter 25, Section 25.2

Problem c3. Of the 64 outcomes of flipping six coins, how many have at least one head?

HintCount the opposite: no heads at all.
Answer63
Method: Count the opposite

Exactly one outcome has no heads (all tails). 64 − 1 = 63.

Method: Cases

Number of heads 1, 2, …, 6 contribute 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1 outcomes: 6 + 15 + 20 + 15 + 6 + 1 = 63. (Much more work, but it confirms the first answer.)

Source: Rusczyk, The Art of Problem Solving Vol. 1, Chapter 25, Section 25.7 idea (counting the wrong thing)

Problem c9. How many odd numbers with middle digit 5 and no repeated digit are there from 40000 to 69999 inclusive?

HintHandle the most restricted digit (the last, odd) first.
Answer336
Method: Casework on the first digit

The first digit is 4, 5 or 6, but 5 is already used as the middle digit, so 4 or 6: 2 ways. The last digit is odd and not 5: 1, 3, 7, 9: 4 ways. The remaining two digits (second and fourth) come from the 10 − 3 = 7 unused digits: 7 · 6 = 42. Total 2 · 4 · 42 = 336.

Method: Computer check

Counting by loop gives 336.

Problem c18. With how many zeros does 103! end?

HintCount the factors of 5.
Answer24

Each trailing zero needs a pair 2 · 5, and 5s are the scarce ones. Multiples of 5 up to 103: 20; multiples of 25: 4 (25, 50, 75, 100) each give one extra 5. 20 + 4 = 24.

Computing 103! and counting trailing zeros gives 24.

Problem c21. In how many ways can 6 different keys be arranged on a keychain?

HintCircle, then divide by 2 for flipping.
Answer60
Method: Circle then flip

On a circle 5! = 120. A keychain can be flipped over, so each arrangement is counted twice: 120/2 = 60.

Method: From the line

6! = 720 in a line; 6 rotations and 2 flips make 12 equivalent ones each: 720/12 = 60.

Problem c25. Antoinette gives one sweet each to two children who insist on different colours. She has 3 different red, 4 different brown and 5 different tan sweets. In how many ways can she do it? (The children are different people.)

HintCasework on which two colours.
Answer94
Method: Casework

Red–brown 3 · 4 = 12; red–tan 3 · 5 = 15; brown–tan 4 · 5 = 20. Each colour pair can go to (child 1, child 2) in two orders: 2(12 + 15 + 20) = 94.

Method: Subtract

Total ordered choices 12 · 11 = 132 (any sweet to child 1, then any other to child 2). Same colour: 3·2 + 4·3 + 5·4 = 38. 132 − 38 = 94.
