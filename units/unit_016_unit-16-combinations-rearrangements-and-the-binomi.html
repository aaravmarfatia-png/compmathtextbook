1. Choosing without order: combinations

In Unit 15 order mattered: choosing a captain, a vice-captain and a treasurer from 8 people gave 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 outcomes. But if you only want to choose a team of 3 from 8 people, the order of choosing does not matter, and each team has been counted 3! = 6 times (once for each ordering of its members). So the number of teams is 336 / 6 = 56. This number is written C(8, 3), or 'eight choose three', or the binomial coefficient with 8 above 3. The general formula is C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!).

Always cancel before multiplying. C(52, 5) is the number of poker hands. Writing 52!/(5! 47!) = (52 × 51 × 50 × 49 × 48)/(5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 2 598 960 is much easier than computing 52!. Cancel factors step by step: 50/5 = 10, 48/4 = 12, 51/3 = 17 and 52/2 = 26, which uses up the whole denominator 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 and leaves 10 × 12 × 17 × 26 × 49 = 2 598 960.

Symmetry. Choosing which 3 of 8 people to include is the same as choosing which 5 to leave out, so C(8, 3) = C(8, 5) = 56. In general C(n, k) = C(n, n − k). This is a big time-saver: C(100, 98) is simply C(100, 2) = 4950.

Worked example 1 (committee with a condition). A club has 6 women and 5 men. A committee of 4 must contain at least 2 women. Count by cases: 2 women and 2 men: C(6,2) × C(5,2) = 15 × 10 = 150; 3 women and 1 man: 20 × 5 = 100; 4 women: 15. Total 150 + 100 + 15 = 265. (A check by complement: C(11, 4) = 330 committees in all, minus 0 women: C(5,4) = 5, minus exactly 1 woman: 6 × C(5,3) = 60. 330 − 5 − 60 = 265 ✓.)

Worked example 2 (splitting into pairs). In how many ways can 10 people be divided into two unlabelled groups of 5? Choosing the first group gives C(10, 5) = 252, but the two groups can be named in either order, so each split is counted twice: 252 / 2 = 126. For splitting 8 people into 4 pairs it is C(8,2) × C(6,2) × C(4,2) × C(2,2) / 4! = 28 × 15 × 6 × 1 / 24 = 105.

Try it

A class has 7 boys and 5 girls. How many ways to choose a team of 3 boys and 2 girls?

Answer. C(7,3) × C(5,2) = 35 × 10 = 350.

2. Permutations, combinations and when to use which

The test. Ask: if I swap two of the chosen things, do I get a different outcome? If yes (president and treasurer, a 3-digit code, finishing positions), order matters and you use arrangements n!/(n − k)!. If no (a committee, a hand of cards, a subset), order does not matter and you use C(n, k). Equivalent: C(n, k) = arrangements / k!.

Mixed problems. Choose first, then arrange. How many 3-letter 'words' (all letters distinct) can be made from the 5 letters A, B, C, D, E which include the letter A? Choose which 2 other letters join A: C(4, 2) = 6; arrange the three letters in 3! = 6 ways: 36. Check by complement: all arrangements of 3 distinct letters from 5 is 60, those without A: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24, and 60 − 24 = 36 ✓.

Words from letters with repeats. The number of 5-letter arrangements of the letters AABBC is 5!/(2! 2!) = 30. An equivalent way is to choose positions: choose 2 of 5 for the As (10 ways), then 2 of the remaining 3 for the Bs (3 ways), and C takes the last place: 10 × 3 = 30 ✓. This shows that the repeated-letter formula is really a product of combinations.

Lattice paths. A walker moves from the bottom-left corner of a grid to the top-right corner, going only right (R) or up (U). To go 5 right and 3 up requires 8 steps, of which any 3 may be the 'up' steps, so the number of paths is C(8, 3) = 56 (and a listing by computer of all 3-subsets of 8 positions confirms 56). If the path must pass through a given point, multiply the number of paths to the point by the number from the point.

Try it

How many paths from (0,0) to (4,3) go only right or up and pass through (2,1)?

Answer. To (2,1): C(3,1) = 3. From (2,1) to (4,3): 2 right and 2 up, C(4,2) = 6. Total 3 × 6 = 18.

3. Stars and bars

How many ways can 10 identical sweets be shared among 4 children if a child may get none? This is the same as counting the solutions in non-negative integers of x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + x₄ = 10. Picture 10 'stars' in a row and 3 'bars' dividing them into 4 groups. A sequence of 13 symbols is determined by which 3 positions hold the bars: C(13, 3) = 286. A direct count of all quadruples confirms 286.

The formula. The number of non-negative integer solutions of x₁ + … + x_r = n is C(n + r − 1, r − 1). If each variable must be at least 1 (everyone gets at least one sweet), first give each child one: now 6 sweets remain for 4 children, C(9, 3) = 84. In general the positive-solution count is C(n − 1, r − 1).

Variants. If x₁ must be at least 3, substitute y₁ = x₁ − 3 ≥ 0 and the equation becomes y₁ + x₂ + x₃ + x₄ = 7, with C(10, 3) = 120 solutions. If there is an upper bound, count without the bound and subtract the violations: for x₁ ≤ 4 in the original problem, subtract those with x₁ ≥ 5: y = x₁ − 5 gives sum 5, C(8, 3) = 56, so 286 − 56 = 230.

Warning. Stars and bars only applies to identical objects going into different boxes. If the sweets are all different, each sweet has 4 choices: 4¹⁰.

Try it

How many non-negative integer solutions has a + b + c = 8?

Answer. C(8 + 2, 2) = C(10, 2) = 45.

4. Pascal's triangle and identities

Write 1 at the top, then build each row by adding the two numbers above. The rows are 1; 1 1; 1 2 1; 1 3 3 1; … and row n lists C(n, 0), C(n, 1), …, C(n, n). Row 5 is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 and row 6 is 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1. The rule is Pascal's identity: C(n, k) = C(n − 1, k − 1) + C(n − 1, k).

Why Pascal's identity holds. To choose k people from n, single out one person, Anna. Either Anna is in the team (choose the other k − 1 from the n − 1 remaining: C(n − 1, k − 1) ways) or she is not (choose all k from the other n − 1). Adding the two cases gives the identity. For example C(7,2) + C(7,3) = 21 + 35 = 56 = C(8,3).

The sum of a row. Every element is either in or out of a subset, so there are 2ⁿ subsets in all; counting by size gives C(n,0) + C(n,1) + … + C(n,n) = 2ⁿ. For n = 10 the row sums to 1024. The alternating sum C(n,0) − C(n,1) + C(n,2) − … equals 0 for n ≥ 1 (verified for n = 8).

The hockey-stick identity. C(2,2) + C(3,2) + C(4,2) + … + C(n,2) = C(n + 1, 3). With n = 5 we get 1 + 3 + 6 + 10 = 20 = C(6,3).

Try it

Use Pascal's identity to find C(9,4) if C(8,3) = 56 and C(8,4) = 70.

Answer. C(9,4) = C(8,3) + C(8,4) = 56 + 70 = 126.

5. The binomial theorem

Expanding (x + y)ⁿ means choosing, from each of the n brackets, either x or y. The term x^(n−k) y^k arises from choosing y in exactly k of the n brackets, which can be done in C(n, k) ways. Hence (x + y)ⁿ = Σ C(n, k) x^(n−k) y^k. For example (x + 2)⁴ = x⁴ + 4·2 x³ + 6·4 x² + 4·8 x + 16 = x⁴ + 8x³ + 24x² + 32x + 16 (computer algebra agrees).

Signs. For (x − 3)⁵ think of y = −3: the coefficients are 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 times powers of −3: x⁵ − 15x⁴ + 90x³ − 270x² + 405x − 243. Signs alternate. Single coefficient. The coefficient of x³ in (2x − 1)⁶: the term with x³ has k = 3 factors of 2x and 3 factors of −1: C(6,3) · 2³ · (−1)³ = 20 · 8 · (−1) = −160.

The constant term. In (x + 1/x)⁶ the general term is C(6, k) x^(6−k) x^(−k) = C(6, k) x^(6−2k); it is constant when 6 − 2k = 0, k = 3, giving C(6, 3) = 20. In (x² + 1/x)⁹ the term k has x^(2(9−k)) x^(−k) = x^(18 − 3k); constant when k = 6 and the coefficient is C(9, 6) = 84.

Evaluation tricks. Put x = y = 1 to get the sum of coefficients; put x = 1, y = −1 for the alternating sum. Use the theorem for mental arithmetic: 11⁴ = (10 + 1)⁴ = 10⁴ + 4·10³ + 6·10² + 4·10 + 1 = 14 641, and 101³ = 1 030 301. Also Σ C(8, k) 3ᵏ = (1 + 3)⁸ = 4⁸ = 65 536.

Try it

Find the coefficient of x⁴ in (1 + x)¹⁰, and the coefficient of x² in (x − 2)⁵.

Answer. C(10,4) = 210. For (x − 2)⁵ the x² term has k = 3 factors of −2: C(5,3) · (−2)³ = 10 · (−8) = −80.

6. A longer problem

Problem. A school must choose a committee of 4 from 6 teachers and 5 students. (a) How many committees in all? (b) With at least one student? (c) If teacher Mr Lee and student Ana refuse to serve together? (d) If the committee must contain a chair chosen from its members (any member)?

Solution. (a) C(11, 4) = 330. (b) Complement: no students means all four teachers, C(6,4) = 15, so 330 − 15 = 315. (c) Count the committees containing both and subtract: with both chosen, 2 more from the other 9: C(9,2) = 36, so 330 − 36 = 294. (d) Choose the committee then the chair: 330 × 4 = 1320; or choose the chair first (11 ways) then the other 3 from 10: 11 × 120 = 1320 ✓. The agreement of two routes is the best check available.

Reflection. Notice that parts (b) and (c) both used complement counting and that part (d) is a gentle reminder that 'choosing in stages' can reorder the work without changing the answer. When two routes disagree, one of them double counts or misses a case.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Using permutations when order does not matter, or the reverse. (2) Forgetting to divide by 2 when two groups are interchangeable. (3) Using stars and bars when the objects are different. (4) In the binomial theorem, forgetting that coefficients such as 2ˣ or −1 are also raised to powers. (5) Dropping the sign of a negative term. (6) Computing C(n, k) with huge factorials instead of cancelling.

Checks. Make a small version and list the possibilities. Compute a count two different ways. Check that a binomial expansion gives the correct total at x = 1.

How to practise. Say aloud, for each problem, 'order matters / does not matter' and 'objects identical / different'. Then drill the combination and binomial families in Unlimited practice until the choice of formula is automatic.

Hint5·4·3 ordered, divide by 3!.
Answer10
Method: Overcount then divide

Choosing 3 to eat is the same as choosing 2 to leave: 5 · 4/2 = 10.

Answer1

Problem d7. Points are chosen on a circle so that the number of triangles with vertices among them equals the number of pentagons. How many points are there? (Mandelbrot 93)

HintC(n, 3) = C(n, 5), and C(n, k) = C(n, n − k).
Answer8
Method: Symmetry

C(n, 3) = C(n, 5) with C(n, k) = C(n, n − k) suggests n − 3 = 5, so n = 8. (Check: C(8, 3) = 56 = C(8, 5).)

Method: Algebra (the book's)

n!/(3!(n − 3)!) = n!/(5!(n − 5)!) gives (n − 3)(n − 4) = 5!/3! = 20, so n² − 7n − 8 = 0, n = 8 (or −1, rejected).

Problem d16. What is the constant term in the expansion of (x² + 1/x)⁶?

HintThe k-th term has x to the power 12 − 3k.
Answer15
Method: General term

Choosing k of the brackets to give 1/x: C(6, k) x^(2(6 − k)) · x^(−k) = C(6, k) x^(12 − 3k). Constant when k = 4: C(6, 4) = 15.

Method: Total degree reasoning

Each x² contributes +2, each 1/x contributes −1. With a of the first and 6 − a of the second: 2a − (6 − a) = 0, a = 2, so choose which 2 brackets give x²: C(6, 2) = 15.
