Reading

1. Graphs: vertices, edges and degrees

A graph consists of vertices (points) and edges (segments joining pairs of vertices). The degree of a vertex is the number of edges meeting it. Counting each edge from both ends gives the handshake lemma: the sum of all degrees is twice the number of edges. Consequently the number of vertices of odd degree is always even. At a party of 5 people, it is impossible for everyone to know exactly 3 others: the degrees would sum to 15, which is odd.

Complete graphs. With n vertices and every pair joined there are n(n − 1)/2 edges: 10 for n = 5, 15 for n = 6. A graph with n vertices can have at most n(n − 1)/2 edges, so there are 2^{n(n−1)/2} labelled graphs on n vertices (8 for n = 3, 64 for n = 4).

Paths, cycles, trees. A path visits vertices without repeating; a cycle returns to its start; a tree is a connected graph with no cycle. A tree with n vertices has exactly n − 1 edges, and any connected graph with n vertices needs at least n − 1 edges. By Cayley's formula there are nⁿ⁻² labelled trees on n vertices: 16 for n = 4, 125 for n = 5.

Try it

A graph has 8 vertices, each of degree 3. How many edges does it have?

Answer. 8 · 3/2 = 12.

2. Extremal questions: how many edges can we have?

An extremal graph problem asks for the maximum number of edges in a graph with a forbidden structure. Mantel's theorem: a graph on n vertices with no triangle has at most ⌊n²/4⌋ edges, achieved by the complete bipartite graph with parts as equal as possible. For n = 5 the maximum is 6 and for n = 4 it is 4; exhaustive search over all graphs confirms both. For n = 6 the answer is 9 (K₃,₃), for n = 10 it is 25.

Why it works (sketch). Take a vertex v of largest degree d. Its neighbours are pairwise non-adjacent (otherwise a triangle), so each edge has at least one end among the n − d vertices outside the neighbourhood, and each of those has degree at most d. Counting gives at most d(n − d) ≤ n²/4 edges.

Forbidden cycles. A graph with no cycle (a forest) on n vertices has at most n − 1 edges, and a graph with n vertices and n or more edges must contain a cycle. This is a very useful pigeonhole-type fact: if 10 towns are joined by 10 roads, some roads form a loop.

Try it

What is the maximum number of edges in a triangle-free graph on 8 vertices?

Answer. ⌊64/4⌋ = 16.

3. Colouring and greedy algorithms

A proper colouring gives adjacent vertices different colours. The least number of colours is the chromatic number. The 5-cycle needs 3, the complete graph on 4 vertices needs 4, and even cycles need 2. A graph is 2-colourable (bipartite) exactly when it contains no odd cycle.

The order matters. The greedy algorithm colours vertices one by one with the lowest colour not used by earlier neighbours. On the 'crown' graph (a 6-cycle's cousin: complete bipartite K₃,₃ with a perfect matching removed) the order a₀, b₀, a₁, b₁, a₂, b₂ forces 3 colours, whereas the order a₀, a₁, a₂, b₀, b₁, b₂ uses just 2 — both outcomes were computed. So greedy never uses more than (maximum degree + 1) colours, but it can use more than the chromatic number.

Using it. If every person in a club conflicts with at most 4 others, 5 time slots always suffice, whatever the pattern of conflicts. In extremal arguments, colour in a clever order (for example, removing a vertex of smallest degree last).

Try it

A graph has maximum degree 3. How many colours does greedy colouring need at most?

Answer. 4.

4. Euler trails and planarity

An Euler trail uses every edge exactly once. A connected graph has an Euler circuit if every vertex has even degree, and an Euler trail if exactly two vertices have odd degree. The Königsberg bridges graph has degrees 3, 3, 3, 5 (four odd vertices), so no walk crosses each bridge once.

Planar graphs. A graph is planar if it can be drawn without crossing edges. A simple planar graph with n ≥ 3 vertices has at most 3n − 6 edges. K₅ has 10 edges but 3 · 5 − 6 = 9, so K₅ is not planar. (Euler's formula V − E + F = 2, treated in Unit 98, is the reason.)

Try it

Does a graph with degrees 2, 2, 2, 4 on four vertices have an Euler circuit?

Answer. All degrees are even and (if connected) yes. The sum is 10 so it has 5 edges.

5. A longer problem

Problem. Ten people are at a conference and each is friends with at least 6 others in the group (friendship is mutual). Show that three of them are mutual friends.

Solution. The number of friendships is at least 10 · 6/2 = 30 by the handshake lemma. By Mantel's theorem, a triangle-free graph on 10 vertices has at most ⌊100/4⌋ = 25 edges. Since 30 > 25, a triangle must exist.

Reflection. The bound is sharp: if everyone had only 5 friends, the two-group graph K₅,₅ (everyone friends with exactly the five people in the other group) has 25 edges and no triangle, so 'at least 5' would not be enough. A single extra friend each pushes the count over the limit.

6. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Forgetting that edges are counted twice in the degree sum. (2) Confusing 'path' with 'trail'. (3) Believing greedy colouring is optimal. (4) Applying Mantel to graphs that may contain triangles.

Checks. Make the sum of degrees even. Test a claim on small graphs. Draw the extremal example.

How to practise. Draw a graph for every word problem. In Unlimited practice, work the counting and extremal-graph families.

Problem p1. What is the largest number of adjacent swaps ever needed to sort 8 different numbers into increasing order?

HintEach adjacent swap removes at most one inversion.
Answer28
Method: Count inversions

An inversion is a pair that is out of order. An adjacent swap changes the number of inversions by exactly 1, and sorting must reach 0. The reverse order has all C(8,2) = 28 pairs inverted, so 28 swaps are needed, and bubble sort never needs more.

Method: Small cases

For n = 3 the worst case 3 2 1 needs 3 swaps, for n = 4 it needs 6: the pattern is C(n,2).

Problem p2. A graph's vertices each have at most 5 neighbours. Colour the vertices greedily one at a time so adjacent vertices differ. What is the smallest number of colours that is always enough and cannot be reduced in general?

HintGreedy needs one more colour than the maximum degree; K₆ shows this is tight.
Answer6
Method: Greedy argument

When you colour a vertex it has at most 5 already-coloured neighbours, so some one of 6 colours is free. And the complete graph on 6 vertices (each has 5 neighbours) needs all 6 colours.

Method: Tightness

In K₆ every vertex touches every other, so all six must be different colours; 5 colours are not enough in general.

Problem p3. In a round robin 7 teams each play every other team once, no ties. What is the smallest possible value of the largest number of wins by any team?

HintThere are 21 games.
Answer3
Method: Pigeonhole

There are C(7,2) = 21 games, so 21 wins are spread over 7 teams, average 3. Some team has at least 3 wins, and giving every team exactly 3 wins is possible (each beats the next three around a circle).

Method: Construction

Arrange the teams in a circle; each team beats the next 3 teams clockwise. Each team then also loses to the previous 3, so everyone has exactly 3 wins.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (algorithms, induction, extremal arguments)

Problem p4. On 10 vertices, what is the smallest minimum degree that guarantees a graph is connected?

HintTwo disjoint complete graphs K₅ show 4 is not enough.
Answer5
Method: Argument

Two disjoint K₅ have 10 vertices and minimum degree 4 but are disconnected. If the minimum degree is at least 5, two vertices u, v in different components would have disjoint neighbour sets of size ≥ 5 each plus themselves, needing at least 12 vertices. Only 10 exist, so the graph is connected.

Method: Small cases

For 6 vertices (checked by computer over all graphs) the largest minimum degree of a disconnected graph is 2 = n/2 − 1, so the needed value is n/2 = 3; for 10 vertices that gives 5.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (algorithms, induction, extremal arguments)

Problem p5. What is the largest number of edges a graph on 8 vertices can have with no triangle?

HintSplit the vertices into two groups and join across (Mantel).
Answer16
Method: Construction and bound

Take two groups of 4 and join every vertex to all in the other group: 16 edges, no triangle (any triangle would need two vertices in one group joined). And no more is possible: adjacent vertices u, v share no neighbour, so d(u) + d(v) ≤ 8; summing over all edges gives Σd² ≤ 8e, while Σd² ≥ (2e)²/8, so e ≤ 16.

Method: Computer check

For 6 vertices the maximum is 9 and for 7 it is 12 (checked over all graphs); both equal ⌊n²/4⌋, so for 8 the value is 16.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (algorithms, induction, extremal arguments)
