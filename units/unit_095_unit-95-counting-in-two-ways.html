Unit 95: Unit 95: Counting in two ways

Book: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 1 and 9; AoPS Volume 2 ch. 17 (25 problems)
Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, and AoPS Volume 2, chapter 17 (clever correspondences and double counting).

Reading

1. The idea of double counting

Count the same set in two different ways; the two answers must be equal. That is the whole principle, and it proves surprising identities.

Worked example 1: handshakes. In a group of n people, count pairs of people: C(n, 2). Count by degrees: each person shakes d hands, and the sum of degrees counts each handshake twice. So Σd = 2E, the handshake lemma.

Worked example 2. Prove C(n, 2) + C(n + 1, 2) = n². Use a picture: an n × n grid of squares has n² squares. The squares on or above the diagonal number n(n + 1)/2 = C(n + 1, 2) and those strictly below number n(n − 1)/2 = C(n, 2). So C(n + 1, 2) + C(n, 2) = n². For n = 5: 15 + 10 = 25 ✓.

Worked example 3. Σ_{k=0}^{n} k C(n, k) = n 2ⁿ⁻¹. Count pairs (S, x) where x is an element of the subset S of an n-set. Count by S: Σ |S| = Σ k C(n, k). Count by x: n choices for x, then 2ⁿ⁻¹ subsets containing x. For n = 3: 3 + 6 + 3 = 12 = 3 × 4 ✓.

2. Counting pairs in tables

Make a table with rows and columns, mark an entry for each incidence (person–club, number–divisor), and count the marks by rows and then by columns.

Worked example 1: divisors. Σ_{k=1}^{n} ⌊n/k⌋ counts the pairs (k, m) with km ≤ n; counting by m gives the same thing, so it equals the total number of divisors of the numbers 1, …, n (Unit 82).

Worked example 2: committees. Each of 10 students belongs to exactly 3 clubs, and each club has exactly 5 members. How many clubs are there? Count the (student, club) memberships: 10 × 3 = 30 = (number of clubs) × 5, so there are 6 clubs.

Worked example 3: an impossibility. Is it possible to have 7 people where each knows exactly 3 others? The pairs of acquaintances would number 7 × 3/2 = 10.5, not an integer. Impossible.

3. Further identities by double counting

Vandermonde: C(m + n, r) = Σ C(m, i)C(n, r − i), counting committees of r from m men and n women. Σ C(n, k)² = C(2n, n): choose n people from 2n people (n men and n women) by choosing k men and n − k women: C(n, k)C(n, n − k) = C(n, k)².

Worked example. Prove that Σ_{k=1}^{n} k·k! = (n + 1)! − 1 by telescoping, since k·k! = (k + 1)! − k!. For n = 3 gives 1 + 4 + 18 = 23 = 24 − 1 ✓.

Tournaments. In a round-robin with n players, the total number of wins equals C(n, 2), and the sum of the squares of the scores is related to the number of cyclic triples; the number of non-transitive triples is C(n, 3) − Σ C(wᵢ, 2).

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Counting ordered pairs in one count and unordered in the other. Forgetting to divide by 2 when each pair is counted twice. Using different sets in the two counts.

Checks. Test the final identity for n = 2 or 3.

Practice. Define the set of pairs before you count.

Worked examples with Try-its: Double counting

Count the same set in two different ways and equate. Identity. C(4, 0)² + C(4, 1)² + … + C(4, 4)² = 1 + 16 + 36 + 16 + 1 = 70 = C(8, 4): choose 4 people from 4 men and 4 women, grouping by the number of men k (C(4, k) · C(4, 4 − k) = C(4, k)²). Identity. Σ k·C(5, k) = 5 · 2⁴ = 80: count (committee, chair) pairs by committee size or by chair first.

Try it

Show that k·C(n, k) = n·C(n − 1, k − 1) by counting committees with a chair.

Answer. Left: choose k members, then a chair among them. Right: choose the chair (n ways), then the other k − 1 members from n − 1.

Worked examples with Try-its: Degrees and handshakes

In a graph the sum of degrees is twice the number of edges, because each edge is counted at both ends. At a party of 9 people it is impossible for everyone to shake hands with exactly 3 others: 9 · 3 = 27 is odd. A table with r rows and c columns of numbers has the same total whether you add rows first or columns first, which is the basis of many double-counting proofs.

Try it

Can a graph have 7 vertices all of degree 3?

Answer. No: the degree sum 21 is odd.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. In a group of 6 people each pair are friends or strangers. Show there are at least 2 triangles whose three pairs are all the same (all friends or all strangers). Solution. Call an angle at a vertex 'bad' if its two edges differ in kind. A vertex with f friends and 5 − f strangers is the centre of f(5 − f) ≤ 6 bad angles, so there are at most 36 bad angles in all. Each triangle that is not all one kind contains exactly 2 bad angles, so at most 18 of the 20 triangles are mixed, leaving at least 2 uniform ones.

Reflection. Double counting converts a global statement into a local bound.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Committees with a chair pair. Σ C(4, k)C(k, 2) = 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 counts choosing a committee of any size from 4 people and then 2 of its members as co-chairs; counting the pair first gives C(4, 2) · 2² = 24 (the other 2 people are free to join or not). Edges. Counting handshakes among n people in two ways, as n(n − 1)/2 and as half the sum of degrees, shows the degree sum is n(n − 1) when everyone shakes hands with everyone.

Try it

Count the pairs (person, club) if 5 clubs each have 6 members and each person belongs to 3 clubs.

Answer. 5 · 6 = 30 pairs, so there are 30/3 = 10 people.

Try it

Prove Σ C(n, k) = 2ⁿ by counting subsets two ways.

Answer. Each of the n elements is in or out of a subset (2ⁿ), or group subsets by size k.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Show that C(n, 2) + C(n + 1, 2) = n². Solution. Count the ordered pairs (a, b) of numbers from 1 to n: n² in total. Pairs with a < b number C(n, 2), pairs with a > b number C(n, 2), and pairs with a = b number n, so n² = 2C(n, 2) + n. This is C(n, 2) + C(n + 1, 2) since C(n + 1, 2) = C(n, 2) + n.

Check. n = 4: 6 + 10 = 16 ✓.

Problem p1. At a party each of 10 people shakes hands with exactly 3 others. How many handshakes are there?

HintCount handshake ends.
Answer15
Method: Handshake

The degree sum is 30 and each handshake has 2 ends, so there are 15.

Method: Check

A 3-regular graph on 10 vertices exists (for example the Petersen graph), with 15 edges.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 1 and 9; AoPS Volume 2 ch. 17

Problem p2. How many pairs (i, j) with 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 10 have i + j even?

Hinti and j must have the same parity.
Answer20
Method: Parity

Five odd and five even numbers give C(5,2) + C(5,2) = 20.

Method: Complement

All pairs are 45 and the mixed-parity pairs are 5 · 5 = 25, so 20 are same-parity.

Problem p7. In how many ways can 5 non-attacking rooks be placed on a 5 × 5 board?

HintOne rook per row and per column.
Answer120
Method: Permutation

Each row has one rook in a distinct column: 5! = 120.

Method: Count by rows

5 choices in the first row, 4 in the next, and so on.

Problem p8. How many subsets of {1, 2, …, 8} have an even sum?

HintToggle element 1.
Answer128
Method: Pairing

Toggling the element 1 flips the parity of the sum and pairs the subsets, so half of all 256 have an even sum: 128.

Method: Counting odd elements

The sum is even when an even number of the 4 odd elements is chosen: 8 ways (even-size subsets of 4) times 16 even-element choices = 128.
