Reading

1. Letters stand for numbers

Algebra begins with a simple idea: instead of writing a particular number, write a letter that can stand for any number. The reason is power. The statement “3 × 4 = 4 × 3” is one fact about two numbers, but the statement “a × b = b × a” is a fact about every pair of numbers at once. Letters also let us talk about a number we do not know yet. If a bag holds x marbles and you add 5, you now have x + 5, even though you do not know x.

An expression is a recipe built from numbers, letters and operations, such as 3x + 7 or a² − 2ab. The pieces that are added or subtracted are called terms, and the number multiplying the letter in a term is its coefficient. In 5a + 3b − 2a + 7b the terms are 5a, 3b, −2a and 7b. Terms that have exactly the same letter part are called like terms, and like terms can be combined by adding their coefficients, because they count the same kind of thing. Here 5a − 2a = 3a and 3b + 7b = 10b, so the whole expression simplifies to 3a + 10b. You cannot combine 3a with 10b, any more than you can add 3 apples to 10 oranges and call the result 13 apples.

Substituting. To evaluate an expression you replace each letter by a number and calculate, respecting the order of operations. For x² − 3x + 1 with x = −2: x² is (−2)² = 4, then −3x is −3 × (−2) = +6, so the value is 4 + 6 + 1 = 11. Put brackets around a negative number when you substitute it, and this will prevent most sign errors.

Worked example: simplifying with brackets. Simplify 3(x + 4) − 2x. First use the distributive law: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12. Then combine like terms: 3x − 2x = x, leaving x + 12. A good habit is to check with a test value: if x = 5 the original is 3 × 9 − 10 = 17, and x + 12 is 17. If the two disagree for even one test value, something is wrong. This kind of check takes ten seconds and catches most slips.

Worked example: turning words into letters. “A number is doubled and then 7 is subtracted, and the result is 19. What is the number?” Let the number be x. Then 2x − 7 = 19. Add 7 to both sides: 2x = 26. Divide by 2: x = 13. Checking the original sentence: 13 doubled is 26, minus 7 is 19. ✓. Translating sentences into expressions is the most valuable skill in this chapter, and you will practise it constantly.

2. Negative numbers in depth

You met negative numbers in Unit 1. Here we look more carefully at why the rules work, because the reasons make the rules easy to remember. Every number has an opposite: the opposite of 5 is −5 and the opposite of −5 is 5. A number and its opposite add up to 0. The absolute value of a number, written |x|, is its distance from 0 on the number line, so |−7| = 7 and |7| = 7. The expression |3 − 8| = |−5| = 5 is the distance between 3 and 8.

Why a negative times a negative is positive. Look at a pattern. 3 × (−2) = −6, 2 × (−2) = −4, 1 × (−2) = −2, 0 × (−2) = 0. As the first number goes down by 1, the answer goes up by 2. Keep the pattern going: (−1) × (−2) must be 2, and (−2) × (−2) must be 4. This is not a trick. If we want the laws of arithmetic (especially the distributive law) to keep working, there is no other choice. For instance (−1) × (1 + (−1)) = (−1) × 0 = 0, and by the distributive law this equals (−1) × 1 + (−1) × (−1) = −1 + (−1) × (−1). For the total to be 0, (−1) × (−1) has to be +1.

Rules summary. Adding a negative number is the same as subtracting its opposite: 6 + (−4) = 6 − 4 = 2. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding: 6 − (−4) = 6 + 4 = 10. A product or quotient is positive if the two signs agree and negative if they differ, so (−12) ÷ 3 = −4 and (−12) ÷ (−3) = 4. A product of several numbers is negative exactly when an odd number of the factors are negative: (−1)(−2)(−3) = −6 but (−1)(−2)(−3)(−4) = 24.

Worked example. Compute −8 − (−3) × (−2) + 5. Multiplication first: (−3) × (−2) = 6. Now the expression is −8 − 6 + 5. Working left to right: −8 − 6 = −14, and −14 + 5 = −9. Notice that the minus sign in front of the bracket is subtraction, applied to the whole product 6. If you rushed and computed −8 − (−3) first, you would get −5 and then −5 × (−2) = 10, which is wrong because the multiplication has to be done first.

Dividing by zero is never allowed. Division asks “what number times the divisor gives the dividend?” For 6 ÷ 0 there is no number that, multiplied by 0, gives 6, and for 0 ÷ 0 every number works, so the question has no single answer. Whenever a fraction has a letter in its denominator, remember that the letter must not make the denominator zero.

3. Fractions: comparing and combining

A fraction a/b describes a parts of size 1/b. The top number is the numerator and the bottom number is the denominator, which must not be 0. Two fractions are equivalent if they describe the same amount, and you make one by multiplying or dividing the top and the bottom by the same non-zero number: 2/3 = 4/6 = 6/9. A fraction is in lowest terms when top and bottom have no common factor greater than 1. To reduce 48/36, divide both by their greatest common divisor 12 to get 4/3.

Comparing fractions. To decide whether 5/7 or 7/10 is bigger, write them over a common denominator, for example 70: 5/7 = 50/70 and 7/10 = 49/70, so 5/7 is bigger. A faster method is cross-multiplication: compare 5 × 10 = 50 with 7 × 7 = 49. The bigger cross product belongs to the bigger fraction, provided both denominators are positive.

Adding and subtracting. You can only add fractions that have the same denominator, because only then are you counting the same size of piece. So 2/3 + 3/4 = 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12, and 5/6 − 1/4 = 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12. The most common error in all of school mathematics is to add the tops and the bottoms: 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. A quick sanity check: 2/5 is less than 1/2, but adding something to 1/2 cannot make it smaller. The right answer is 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

Multiplying and dividing. To multiply, multiply the tops and the bottoms: (2/3) × (9/10) = 18/30 = 3/5. It is easier to cancel common factors first: the 9 and the 3 share a factor of 3, and 2 and 10 share a factor of 2, giving (1/1) × (3/5) = 3/5. To divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal (flip it): (3/4) ÷ (5/8) = (3/4) × (8/5) = 24/20 = 6/5. The reason is that dividing by 5/8 asks how many 5/8s fit into 3/4, and each whole 1 contains 8/5 of them.

Mixed numbers. A mixed number like 2 3/5 is a whole number plus a fraction. Convert to an improper fraction with 2 × 5 + 3 = 13, so 2 3/5 = 13/5. Do this before multiplying or dividing mixed numbers, since multiplying the whole and fraction parts separately gives the wrong answer.

Fractions with letters. The same rules apply to fractions with letters. For example x/2 + x/3 = 3x/6 + 2x/6 = 5x/6, and (a/b) × (b/c) = a/c as long as b is not zero. Later units on rational expressions rely on this, so be sure you can do each of these operations with plain numbers first.

4. Powers

A power is shorthand for repeated multiplication: aⁿ means n copies of a multiplied together. In 2⁵ the number 2 is the base and 5 is the exponent, and 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The word “squared” for the exponent 2 comes from the area of a square, and “cubed” for 3 comes from the volume of a cube. Powers of 2 grow quickly: 2¹⁰ = 1,024 and 3⁴ = 81. It helps to know the first few by heart: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024.

Signs and powers. A negative base raised to an even power is positive and to an odd power is negative: (−2)³ = −8 and (−2)⁴ = 16. Be careful with brackets. The expression −2⁴ means −(2⁴) = −16, because the power is done before the minus sign is applied, while (−2)⁴ = 16. So −3² = −9 but (−3)² = 9.

The three basic rules. Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, because a² × a³ = (a × a)(a × a × a) = a⁵. A power of a power multiplies the exponents: (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ, because (2³)² = 2³ × 2³ = 2⁶ = 64. And a power of a product distributes: (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ, so (2 × 3)² = 36 = 4 × 9. Notice that there is no rule for the power of a sum: (a + b)² is not a² + b². That is the same warning as Unit 1, and it is worth repeating because it causes more wrong answers than any other mistake.

Worked example. Simplify (x³ · x⁴)² ÷ x⁵. First x³ · x⁴ = x⁷. Then (x⁷)² = x¹⁴. Finally x¹⁴ ÷ x⁵ = x⁹, because dividing removes five of the fourteen factors. Writing the intermediate lines helps avoid slips.

Why the rules work. Every rule above is just counting factors. When you see an exponent rule you have forgotten, rebuild it from a small example such as a² × a³, and the correct statement appears.

5. Zero and negative exponents, and scientific notation

What should a⁰ mean? Look at the pattern 2⁴ = 16, 2³ = 8, 2² = 4, 2¹ = 2. Each step down divides by 2, so the next value is 2⁰ = 1, and the one after is 2⁻¹ = 1/2, then 2⁻² = 1/4. That is the definition: a⁰ = 1 for every non-zero a, and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. The first definition keeps the rule aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ working: a³ × a⁰ = a³ only if a⁰ = 1. The second is the same idea: a² × a⁻² = a⁰ = 1 only if a⁻² is the reciprocal of a².

The quotient rule. Dividing powers of the same base subtracts the exponents: aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ. For example 2⁵ ÷ 2⁸ = 2⁻³ = 1/8, which agrees with the cancelled form (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2)/(2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2) = 1/(2 × 2 × 2) = 1/8. For a fraction raised to a negative power, flip it: (3/2)⁻² = (2/3)² = 4/9.

Scientific notation. Very large and very small numbers are written as a number between 1 and 10 times a power of ten. So 4,500,000 = 4.5 × 10⁶ and 0.00032 = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴. Multiplying then follows the rules: (4.5 × 10⁶) × (2 × 10⁻²) = 9 × 10⁴ = 90,000. The exponent on the 10 counts how many places the decimal point moves, to the right if it is positive and to the left if it is negative.

Worked example. Simplify (2⁻³ · 4²) ÷ 8⁻¹ as a single power of 2. Write everything with base 2: 4² = (2²)² = 2⁴ and 8⁻¹ = (2³)⁻¹ = 2⁻³. The numerator is 2⁻³ × 2⁴ = 2¹, and dividing by 2⁻³ gives 2¹ ÷ 2⁻³ = 2⁴ = 16. A very common method for exponent problems is to convert all the bases to the same prime, and then you only have to add and subtract exponents.

6. Common mistakes and how to practise

Mistakes to watch for. Treating (a + b)² as a² + b². Adding numerators and denominators when adding fractions. Forgetting that −3² and (−3)² are different. Combining unlike terms such as 3a + 4b into 7ab. Dropping a negative sign when you substitute a negative number into an expression. Dividing only part of an expression when cancelling: in (x + 6)/6 you cannot cancel the 6s to get x + 1, only a factor of the whole top can cancel.

How to check. Substitute a small test value for each letter in both the original and your simplified form, and make sure both give the same number. For fraction work, estimate with decimals. For powers, test the rule on 2 or 3 to see if it holds with small numbers.

The practice set. Read the hint if you are stuck, then try again. After you reach an answer open all the methods and see which ones you could have used. In this unit the most useful methods are rewriting everything in one form (one base, one denominator), testing with numbers, and using a pattern to reconstruct a rule. When you can do the problems comfortably, try the Unlimited practice generator for this unit to see fresh numbers.

Problem 37. A small vessel and a big vessel together hold 5 liters. Two small and three big vessels together hold 13 liters. How many liters does a small vessel hold, and how many does a big one hold? Answer as: small, big.

HintDouble the first fact. Compare it with the second fact.
Answer2, 3
Method: Arithmetic reasoning

Small + big = 5, so two small + two big = 10. We are told two small + three big = 13. The difference between those two situations is one extra big vessel, and it accounts for 13 − 10 = 3 liters. So a big vessel holds 3 liters, and a small one holds 5 − 3 = 2 liters.

Method: Letters and elimination

Let x be the small vessel and y the big one. Then x + y = 5 and 2x + 3y = 13. Multiply the first equation by 2: 2x + 2y = 10. Subtract it from the second: y = 3. Put y = 3 into x + y = 5: x = 2.

Method: Substitution

From x + y = 5 we get x = 5 − y. Put this into 2x + 3y = 13: 2(5 − y) + 3y = 13, so 10 − 2y + 3y = 13, so 10 + y = 13, y = 3, x = 2.

Method: Guess and check

The sizes must add to 5, so try pairs: (1, 4) gives 2 + 12 = 14, no. (2, 3) gives 4 + 9 = 13, yes. This only works because the numbers are small; it is a good way to confirm an answer you found another way.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 37

Problem 38. Choose any number. Add 3. Multiply by 2. Subtract your number. Subtract 4. Subtract your number again. What number do you always get?

HintCall your number x and follow it step by step.
Answer2
Method: Follow x with a letter

Start: x. Add 3: x + 3. Double: 2(x + 3) = 2x + 6. Subtract x: x + 6. Subtract 4: x + 2. Subtract x again: 2. The letter disappears, so the answer is 2 whatever you pick.

Method: Try numbers

Pick 5: 5 + 3 = 8, × 2 = 16, − 5 = 11, − 4 = 7, − 5 = 2. Pick 10: 13, 26, 16, 12, 2. Pick 0: 3, 6, 6, 2, 2. It is always 2. A few examples suggest it; the letter method proves it.

Method: Count x's and plain numbers

Track two things separately: how many x's you hold, and the plain number. Start (1 x, 0). Add 3: (1x, 3). Double: (2x, 6). Subtract x: (1x, 6). Subtract 4: (1x, 2). Subtract x: (0x, 2). Zero x's, so only the 2 is left.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 38

Problem 39. Which is bigger, 1/3 or 2/7? Answer with the bigger fraction.

HintFind a common denominator, or a common numerator.
Answer1/3
Method: Common denominator

The smallest number both 3 and 7 divide is 21. 1/3 = 7/21 and 2/7 = 6/21. Since 7 > 6, 1/3 is bigger.

Method: Common numerator

Make both tops 2: 1/3 = 2/6. Compare 2/6 and 2/7. Same top, so the smaller bottom is the bigger fraction: 2/6 > 2/7. In real life: one bottle for three people is better than two bottles for seven people.

Method: Decimals

1/3 = 0.333… and 2/7 = 0.2857… So 1/3 is bigger.

Method: Cross-multiply

To compare a/b and c/d (positive), compare a·d and c·b. Here 1·7 = 7 and 2·3 = 6. Since 7 > 6, 1/3 > 2/7. (This works because it is the common-denominator method with the common denominator b·d hidden.)

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 39

Problem 40. Which is bigger, 10001/10002 or 100001/100002? Write the bigger fraction.

HintBoth are less than 1. How far below 1 is each?
Answer100001/100002
Method: Distance from 1

10001/10002 = 1 − 1/10002. 100001/100002 = 1 − 1/100002. Since 1/100002 is smaller than 1/10002, we subtract less from 1, so 100001/100002 is bigger.

Method: Cross-multiply

Compare 10001 × 100002 with 100001 × 10002. The first product is 1,000,120,002 and the second is 1,000,210,002. Cross-multiplying says a/b < c/d exactly when a·d < c·b (all positive), so 10001/10002 < 100001/100002. This works, but it needs big multiplications, which is why method 1 is nicer.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 40

Problem 41. Which is bigger, 12345/54321 or 12346/54322? Write the bigger fraction.

HintAdding 1 to the top and bottom of a fraction less than 1 moves it toward 1.
Answer12346/54322
Method: Distance from 1 (as in Problem 40)

The distances from 1 are (54321 − 12345)/54321 = 41976/54321 and (54322 − 12346)/54322 = 41976/54322. The tops are equal, and 54322 > 54321, so the second distance is smaller. The second fraction is closer to 1, and therefore bigger.

Method: Cross-multiplication

Compare 12345 × 54322 with 12346 × 54321. Expand: 12345 × (54321 + 1) = 12345 × 54321 + 12345, and (12345 + 1) × 54321 = 12345 × 54321 + 54321. Since 54321 > 12345, the second product is bigger, so 12346/54322 is bigger. Notice that we never multiplied the big numbers out; we only compared what was added.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 41

Problem 44. What is better: 5 percent of seven billion, or 7 percent of five billion? Answer: first, second, or equal.

HintWrite each as a product with 5, 7 and 100.
Answerequal
Method: Write both as products

5% of 7 billion = (5/100) × 7 billion = 5 × 7 × 109/100. 7% of 5 billion = (7/100) × 5 billion = 7 × 5 × 109/100. Multiplication order does not matter, so they are equal (both 350 million).

1% of 7 billion = 70 million, so 5% = 350 million. 1% of 5 billion = 50 million, so 7% = 350 million. Equal.

Problem 45. A string is 2/3 meter long. What fraction of the string must be cut off to leave a piece 1/2 meter long?

HintCompare 1/2 with 2/3: what fraction of 2/3 is 1/2?
Answer1/4
Method: Divide the lengths

The piece is (1/2) ÷ (2/3) = (1/2) × (3/2) = 3/4 of the string. So cut off 1 − 3/4 = 1/4 of the string.

Method: Common denominators

In sixths, the string is 4 sixths long (2/3 = 4/6) and the piece is 3 sixths (1/2 = 3/6). So the piece is 3 out of 4 equal parts, and you cut off 1 out of 4: one fourth.

Method: Think of the string as 4 equal parts

Fold the 2/3 m string into 4 equal parts. Each part is (2/3)/4 = 1/6 m. Three parts make 1/2 m. Cut off the remaining 1 part: that is one quarter of the string.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 45
