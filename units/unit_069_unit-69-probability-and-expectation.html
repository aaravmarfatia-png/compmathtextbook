1. Probability and expectation: a second look

Earlier units introduced probability as counting. Here we use linearity of expectation: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] for any random quantities, dependent or not. Example 1. In a random permutation of 4 objects the expected number of fixed points is 4 · (1/4) = 1, because each object has probability 1/4 of landing in its own place (a direct count over all 24 permutations gives total 24 fixed points, average 1). Example 2 (coupon collector). To collect all 4 different coupons, the expected number of draws is 4(1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4) = 25/3 ≈ 8.33, since the waiting time for the k-th new coupon is a geometric variable with mean 4/(5 − k).

Geometric waiting time. If an event has probability p on each trial, the expected number of trials to the first success is 1/p: 6 rolls on average to see a 6. At least one in 4 rolls: 1 − (5/6)⁴ = 671/1296 ≈ 0.518.

Try it

Expected number of heads in 10 fair tosses?

Answer. 10 · 1/2 = 5.

2. Indicator variables

Write the quantity you want as a sum of indicators (each 1 if some event happens and 0 otherwise); the expected value of an indicator is the probability of the event. Example. 8 people are seated in a row at random; how many adjacent pairs consist of a man and a woman if there are 4 men and 4 women? Each of the 7 adjacent pairs is mixed with probability 2 · (4/8)(4/7) = 16/28 = 4/7, so the expected number is 7 · 4/7 = 4.

Why it works. The indicators are dependent, but linearity of expectation does not care. This is the single most powerful trick in expected-value problems.

Try it

In a class of 30, what is the expected number of people who share a birthday with at least one other, if birthdays are uniform over 365 days? (Give the formula.)

Answer. 30(1 − (364/365)²⁹) ≈ 30 · 0.0765 ≈ 2.3 people.

3. A longer problem

Problem. A fair die is rolled until a 6 appears. (a) Expected number of rolls? (b) Probability the 6 appears within 3 rolls? (c) Expected number of 6s in 12 rolls?

Solution. (a) 6. (b) 1 − (5/6)³ = 91/216. (c) 12 · 1/6 = 2.

Reflection. Three different ideas (geometric waiting time, complement, indicators) all appear with one die.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Assuming indicators must be independent. (2) Computing E[XY] as E[X]E[Y] for dependent variables. (3) Forgetting that expectation is a weighted average, not the most likely value.

Checks. Compute the expectation by brute force for a small case.

How to practise. Always try indicator variables first. In Unlimited practice, rotate the expectation families.

More worked examples: Worked examples

One die. The expected value of a fair die is (1 + 2 + … + 6)/6 = 7/2. Maximum of two dice. Averaging max(a, b) over all 36 outcomes gives 161/36 ≈ 4.47. Sum of 7. Six of the 36 outcomes sum to 7, so the probability is 1/6. Linearity. The expected sum of two dice is 7/2 + 7/2 = 7 without listing outcomes.

Try it

What is the expected number of tails in 8 fair coin tosses?

Answer. 4.

Try it

Two dice are rolled. What is the probability that the sum is at least 10?

Answer. (3 + 2 + 1)/36 = 1/6.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. A bag has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Two are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that both are red and the expected number of red balls. Solution. P(both red) = (3/5)(2/4) = 3/10. The expected number of red balls is 2 · 3/5 = 6/5 by linearity (each draw is red with probability 3/5).

Check. The distribution is 0 red: 1/10, 1 red: 6/10, 2 red: 3/10, and 0 + 6/10 + 6/10 = 6/5 ✓.

Answer1/9
