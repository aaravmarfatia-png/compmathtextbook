Reading

1. Lagrange interpolation

A polynomial of degree at most n is determined by its values at n + 1 points. The polynomial of degree ≤ 2 through (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 7) is x² + x + 1 (found by the Lagrange formula, confirmed by computer algebra), and the cubic through (0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 8), (3, 27) is x³. Lagrange's formula: P(x) = Σ yᵢ Π_{j≠i} (x − xⱼ)/(xᵢ − xⱼ).

Finite differences. For the sequence 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 the first differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 and the second differences are constant (2), so the sequence is a quadratic polynomial in n. In general, if the k-th differences are constant, the sequence is a polynomial of degree k.

Try it

Find the quadratic through (1, 2), (2, 5), (3, 10).

Answer. Differences 3, 5 and 2: P(n) = n² + 1.

2. Remainders and identities

The remainder of P(x) on division by (x − 1)(x − 2) is a linear polynomial; if P(1) = 3 and P(2) = 5, then the remainder is 2x + 1 (the line through (1, 3), (2, 5)). A trick for values at consecutive integers. Suppose P has degree at most 3 and P(k) = 1/k for k = 1, 2, 3, 4. Then Q(x) = xP(x) − 1 has degree at most 4 and roots 1, 2, 3, 4, so Q(x) = c(x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3)(x − 4). Putting x = 0: Q(0) = −1 = 24c, so c = −1/24. Then P(5) = (1 + Q(5))/5 = (1 − 24/24)/5 = 0 (computer algebra agrees).

Identities. A polynomial identity valid for infinitely many x is valid for all x. Two polynomials of degree at most n that agree at n + 1 points are identical.

Try it

A cubic P satisfies P(0) = 0, P(1) = 1, P(2) = 8, P(3) = 27. Find P(4).

Answer. P(x) = x³, so 64.

3. A longer problem

Problem. A polynomial P of degree at most 3 satisfies P(k) = 1/k for k = 1, 2, 3, 4. Find P(5) and P(0).

Solution. Q(x) = xP(x) − 1 = −(1/24)(x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3)(x − 4), as in section 2. P(5) = 0. For P(0): P(x) = (1 + Q(x))/x, and 1 + Q(x) vanishes at x = 0 (since Q(0) = −1), so P(0) is the derivative of Q at 0: Q′(0) = −(1/24) · (−(2·3·4) − (1·3·4) − (1·2·4) − (1·2·3)) = (24 + 12 + 8 + 6)/24 = 50/24 = 25/12.

Reflection. Count unknowns and conditions before solving: a polynomial of degree at most 3 has 4 coefficients, which matches the four conditions.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Assuming a polynomial of the wrong degree. (2) Mixing up conditions and unknowns. (3) Forgetting the constant when using the Q(x) trick. (4) Evaluating the product at the wrong point.

Checks. Evaluate the final polynomial at the given points.

How to practise. Count degrees of freedom. In Unlimited practice, rotate the interpolation families.

More worked examples: Worked examples

A polynomial of degree at most n is determined by its values at n + 1 points. If P(0) = 1, P(1) = 3 and P(2) = 7, Lagrange interpolation gives P(x) = x² + x + 1. The finite differences of the values 1, 3, 7 are 2, 4 and then 2: a constant second difference of 2 signals a quadratic with leading coefficient 1. Another check: the polynomial through (1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9) is x².

Try it

Find the quadratic with values 0, 2, 6 at x = 0, 1, 2.

Answer. x² + x.

Try it

A polynomial of degree 3 takes the values 1, 2, 3, 4 at x = 1, 2, 3, 4. What is it?

Answer. P(x) = x.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. A polynomial P of degree 2 satisfies P(1) = 2, P(2) = 5 and P(3) = 10. Find P(4). Solution. The values 2, 5, 10 have differences 3, 5 and second difference 2, so P(4) = 10 + (5 + 2) = 17. Indeed P(x) = x² + 1.

Check. 4² + 1 = 17 ✓.

HintUse r + s and rs.
Answer29
