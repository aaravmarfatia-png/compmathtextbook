Reading

1. Counting terms before you multiply

When you multiply polynomials, one of the first questions is how many products you must write down. If one bracket has p terms and the other has q terms, the product has p × q terms before like terms are combined. So (a + b + c)(d + e) has 3 × 2 = 6 products, and (a + b + c + d)(e + f + g) has 12. After combining like terms there may be many fewer. Knowing both numbers is useful for checking your work and for solving counting questions.

Worked example 1. How many terms does (x + y + z)² have before combining? Three terms times three terms gives 9 products: x², xy, xz, yx, y², yz, zx, zy, z². After combining, xy and yx join, as do xz and zx and yz and zy, leaving 6 terms: x² + y² + z² + 2xy + 2xz + 2yz. This is the square of a three-term sum, and it is worth remembering: the squares of the terms plus twice every pairwise product.

Worked example 2. How many terms does (a + b)⁵ have after combining? The terms are a⁵, a⁴b, a³b², a²b³, ab⁴, b⁵, which is 6 terms. In general (a + b)ⁿ has n + 1 terms. For a product of different brackets such as (a + b)(c + d)(e + f), you get 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 products and no two of them can combine, since each product has a different set of letters.

Worked example 3. How many terms does (1 + x + x²)(1 + x² + x⁴) have after combining? There are 9 products, with exponents 0, 2, 4, then 1, 3, 5, then 2, 4, 6. The exponent list is 0, 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6. The distinct exponents are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, which is 7 terms. Collecting exponents in a list like this is much faster than expanding the product fully.

The idea behind it. Counting how many terms there are, and in particular how many different exponents appear, is the first step toward understanding the structure of an expansion without doing it all. You will use this when you only need one coefficient or when you need to check that a degree is correct.

2. Plugging in numbers to learn about a polynomial

A polynomial is a machine that turns each number x into a value. Often a clever choice of x reveals a fact about its coefficients without any expansion. Suppose p(x) = a₄x⁴ + a₃x³ + a₂x² + a₁x + a₀. Then p(0) = a₀, the constant term. And p(1) = a₄ + a₃ + a₂ + a₁ + a₀, the sum of all the coefficients. And p(−1) = a₄ − a₃ + a₂ − a₁ + a₀, the sum with alternating signs. Adding p(1) and p(−1) gives twice the sum of the even-position coefficients, and subtracting gives twice the odd-position sum.

Worked example 1. Find the sum of the coefficients of p(x) = (2x − 1)⁵(x + 3) without expanding. Put x = 1: (2 − 1)⁵ × (1 + 3) = 1 × 4 = 4. The sum of the coefficients is 4. If you expanded the polynomial you would have six terms to add, with large numbers, and a high chance of a slip.

Worked example 2. For the same p(x), find the sum of the coefficients with alternating signs. Put x = −1: (−2 − 1)⁵ × (−1 + 3) = (−3)⁵ × 2 = −243 × 2 = −486. So a₀ − a₁ + a₂ − … = −486, where the signs follow the powers of x: p(−1) is that signed sum.

Worked example 3: separating even and odd. Using p(1) = 4 and p(−1) = −486, the sum of the coefficients of the even powers of x is (4 − 486)/2 = −241, and the sum of the coefficients of the odd powers is (4 + 486)/2 = 245. These two sums add to 4 as required. This technique is remarkably powerful, because it extracts partial information from a huge expression with only two evaluations.

Worked example 4: finding an unknown. Suppose x³ + kx² − 5x + 6 has the factor (x − 2). Then putting x = 2 must give zero: 8 + 4k − 10 + 6 = 0, so 4k = −4 and k = −1. This is the first appearance of an idea that Unit 6 develops fully: a factor (x − r) means that r is a root.

When to use it. Whenever a problem asks for a sum of coefficients, an alternating sum, a value at a particular point, or a coefficient condition, ask yourself whether a substitution answers it directly. If it does, do not expand.

3. Factoring by taking out a common factor and by grouping

Factoring is the reverse of expanding: writing an expression as a product. Always start by looking for a common factor that appears in every term. In 6x³ − 9x² + 3x each term is divisible by 3x, so the expression equals 3x(2x² − 3x + 1) and the bracket factors further as 3x(2x − 1)(x − 1). Take out the common factor first, since it simplifies everything afterwards.

Grouping. When there are four terms and no factor shared by all of them, try splitting them into two pairs and taking a common factor from each pair, so that the same bracket appears in both. Because ax + ay + bx + by = a(x + y) + b(x + y) = (a + b)(x + y). For example x³ + x² + x + 1 = x²(x + 1) + 1(x + 1) = (x² + 1)(x + 1).

Worked example with signs. Factor 6xy + 9x − 4y − 6. Group the first two and last two terms: 3x(2y + 3) − 2(2y + 3). Careful with the minus: −4y − 6 = −2(2y + 3). Now the bracket (2y + 3) is common, so the result is (3x − 2)(2y + 3). To check, expand: 3x × 2y = 6xy, 3x × 3 = 9x, −2 × 2y = −4y and −2 × 3 = −6. ✓

When grouping fails. Sometimes the pairs you chose do not give a repeated bracket. Then try a different pairing. For ac + bd + ad + bc, grouping the first and second terms gives no match, but rearranging as ac + ad + bc + bd = a(c + d) + b(c + d) = (a + b)(c + d) works. If no grouping works, the expression may simply not factor.

Why this matters. Factoring turns an equation f(x) = 0 into a product of simpler pieces. Since a product is zero only if one of its factors is zero, each factor gives a solution. That idea underlies the quadratic and higher-degree units.

4. Splitting a term and recognising perfect squares

For a quadratic x² + bx + c with whole-number roots, look for two numbers with sum b and product c, and then x² + bx + c = (x + p)(x + q). For x² + 2x − 15, we need two numbers that multiply to −15 and add to 2: they are 5 and −3, so the factorisation is (x + 5)(x − 3). Try pairs of factors of the constant term and look at their sum. If the constant is negative, the two numbers have opposite signs.

Splitting the middle term. If the leading coefficient is not 1, split the middle term. To factor 6x² + 11x + 4, multiply the first and last coefficients: 6 × 4 = 24. Find two numbers with product 24 and sum 11: they are 3 and 8. Rewrite 11x as 3x + 8x: 6x² + 3x + 8x + 4. Group: 3x(2x + 1) + 4(2x + 1) = (3x + 4)(2x + 1). Check by expanding: 6x² + 3x + 8x + 4. ✓

Adding and subtracting a term. Some expressions are not squares as written but become differences of squares when you add and subtract the same term. For x⁴ + 4, add and subtract 4x²: x⁴ + 4x² + 4 − 4x² = (x² + 2)² − (2x)² = (x² + 2x + 2)(x² − 2x + 2). This is the Sophie Germain identity, and the trick of completing a square by adding and subtracting appears often in competitions.

Perfect squares. A trinomial a² ± 2ab + b² is a perfect square. To test x² − 10x + 25: the first term is x², the last is 5², and the middle is 2 × x × 5 = 10x, so the expression is (x − 5)². Perfect squares are important because a perfect square is never negative, a fact that solves many extremum questions.

Worked example: a number trick. Compute 999² − 1 without a calculator. It is a difference of squares: (999 − 1)(999 + 1) = 998 × 1,000 = 998,000. A difference of squares often converts a heavy calculation into a quick product.

5. Sums and differences of cubes, and strategy

Recall the cube factorisations: a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²) and a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²). Notice the pattern of signs: the bracket (a + b) goes with the sum of cubes and has −ab in the trinomial, while (a − b) goes with the difference and has +ab. A helpful memory aid is that the trinomial never has a sign that would let it factor further over real numbers.

Worked example 1. Factor 8x³ + 27. Here 8x³ = (2x)³ and 27 = 3³. The factorisation is (2x + 3)((2x)² − (2x)(3) + 3²) = (2x + 3)(4x² − 6x + 9). Check by expanding: 8x³ − 12x² + 18x + 12x² − 18x + 27 = 8x³ + 27. ✓

Worked example 2. Evaluate 1,001³ − 1,000³ without a calculator. The identity gives (1,001 − 1,000)(1,001² + 1,001 × 1,000 + 1,000²) = 1 × (1,002,001 + 1,001,000 + 1,000,000) = 3,003,001. Differences of cubes where the bases differ by 1 are especially simple.

Worked example 3. Factor x⁶ − 1. As a difference of squares, it is (x³ − 1)(x³ + 1). Each factor is a cube factorisation: (x − 1)(x² + x + 1)(x + 1)(x² − x + 1). Alternatively treat it as a difference of cubes, (x²)³ − 1, giving (x² − 1)(x⁴ + x² + 1). Both lead to the same complete factorisation, since x⁴ + x² + 1 = (x² + x + 1)(x² − x + 1).

A strategy for factoring. Work in this order: (1) take out any common factor; (2) count the terms: two terms suggest difference of squares or sum or difference of cubes, three terms suggest a trinomial pattern, and four terms suggest grouping; (3) factor again if possible; (4) check by expanding or by substituting a number.

6. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Forgetting to take out a common factor first. Making a sign error when factoring out a negative: −4y − 6 = −2(2y + 3), not −2(2y − 3). Assuming x² + y² factors like x² − y². Stopping too early and leaving a factor that can be split further, such as x² − 4. Expanding when a substitution would answer the question instantly.

Checks. After factoring, multiply the factors back together. Or substitute x = 1 or x = 2 into both forms. For plug-in problems, double-check the sign when you substitute a negative number, using brackets.

The practice set. The problems mix counting terms, substitutions and factoring. Try to decide before you start which of these ideas applies. After you solve a problem, read the methods and see whether a faster one exists. Then use the Unlimited practice generator for this unit.

Problem 94. Each of two polynomials has four terms. What is the largest possible number of terms in their product?

HintEach term of the first meets each term of the second.
Answer16
Method: Count the pairs

Each of the 4 terms of the first polynomial is multiplied by each of the 4 terms of the second: 4 × 4 = 16 products. After combining similar terms you can only get fewer, never more, so the maximum is 16.

Method: A grid

Draw a 4 × 4 table with the first polynomial's terms down the side and the second's across the top. Each cell is one product, and there are 16 cells.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 94

Problem 95. How many terms does (1 + x + x² + x³)(1 + x + x² + x³) have after similar terms are collected?

HintThe powers of x range from 0 up to 6.
Answer7
Method: Which powers appear

The smallest power is x⁰ (1 · 1) and the largest is x⁶ (x³ · x³). Every power from 0 to 6 occurs (for example x⁵ = x² · x³ = x³ · x²). That is 7 different powers, so 7 terms.

Method: Expand

The 16 products collect as 1 + 2x + 3x² + 4x³ + 3x⁴ + 2x⁵ + x⁶. That is 7 terms, and the coefficients add to 16 as they must (4 × 4).

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 95 (numbers chosen by me)

Problem 99. The numbers in the row of Pascal's triangle for (a + b)¹⁰ are the coefficients of the expansion. What do they add up to?

HintPut a = 1 and b = 1.
Answer1024
Method: Substitute a = b = 1

(1 + 1)¹⁰ = 2¹⁰ = 1024, and the right-hand side becomes the sum of the coefficients.

Method: Row sums double

The row sums are 1, 2, 4, 8, … because every number in a row is used twice in the next row. Row 10 (starting from row 0 = 1) adds to 2¹⁰ = 1024.

Problem 101. The polynomial (1 + 2x)²⁰⁰ is written in standard form. What is the sum of all its coefficients? Write a power if you like (e.g. 3^5).

HintTry x = 1.
Answer3^200
Method: Substitute x = 1

The sum of the coefficients is the value at x = 1, because every power of 1 is 1. So it is (1 + 2)²⁰⁰ = 3²⁰⁰.

Method: Test the idea on a small case

(1 + 2x)² = 1 + 4x + 4x², and 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 = 3². The same pattern gives 3²⁰⁰ for the exponent 200.

Problem 103. The polynomial (1 + x − y)³ is written in standard form. What is the sum of its coefficients?

HintPut x = 1 and y = 1.
Answer1
Method: Substitute

(1 + 1 − 1)³ = 1³ = 1.

Method: Think about the pieces

Each coefficient is the number you get from choosing terms 1, x or −y from each of the three brackets, so the coefficients already include the minus signs. Putting x = y = 1 is the only way to add them all, and it gives 1.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 103

Problem 104. (Continuing from 103.) What is the sum of the coefficients of the terms of (1 + x − y)³ that do not contain y?

HintPut y = 0 and x = 1.
Answer8
Method: Substitute y = 0, x = 1

Setting y = 0 deletes every term that contains y. Setting x = 1 turns the remaining coefficients into their sum: (1 + 1 − 0)³ = 8.

Method: Expand the y-free part

With y = 0 the polynomial is (1 + x)³ = 1 + 3x + 3x² + x³, whose coefficients add to 8.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 104

Problem 105. (Continuing from 103.) What is the sum of the coefficients of the terms of (1 + x − y)³ that contain x?

HintFind the total and subtract the terms with no x.
Answer1
Method: Total minus no-x terms

The total of all coefficients is 1 (problem 103). The terms with no x are found by setting x = 0 and y = 1: (1 + 0 − 1)³ = 0. So the terms containing x add to 1 − 0 = 1.

Method: Expand the no-x part

With x = 0 the polynomial is (1 − y)³ = 1 − 3y + 3y² − y³, whose coefficients add to 0. So the terms with x add to 1 − 0 = 1.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 105
