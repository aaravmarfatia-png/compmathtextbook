1. Triangle area: the many formulas

The area of a triangle is half base times height: K = ½bh. The height is the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to the line of the base. A triangle has three bases and three heights, and ½bh gives the same value for each: in the 13-14-15 triangle the altitudes onto the sides 14, 15 and 13 are 12, 11.2 and 12.92…, and ½ · 14 · 12 = 84 = ½ · 15 · 11.2.

Two sides and the angle between them. K = ½ab sin C. A triangle with sides 7 and 8 enclosing 60° has area ½ · 56 · (√3/2) = 14√3. Heron's formula. With semiperimeter s = (a + b + c)/2, K = √(s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)). For 13, 14, 15: s = 21, K = √(21 · 8 · 7 · 6) = √7056 = 84. It is the tool of choice when only the three sides are known. For 7, 15, 20: s = 21, K = √(21 · 14 · 6 · 1) = 42, confirmed numerically.

Using the radii. K = rs for the inradius r, and K = abc/(4R) for the circumradius R. For 13-14-15: r = 84/21 = 4 and R = 2730/336 = 65/8. For a right triangle r = (a + b − c)/2: for 5-12-13 that is 2, and for 6-8-10 also 2.

Coordinates. The shoelace formula gives the area of any polygon from its vertices: ½|Σ(xᵢyᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ₊₁yᵢ)|. For (0, 0), (14, 0), (5, 12) it returns 84.

Try it

Find the area of a triangle with sides 9, 10, 17.

Answer. s = 18, K = √(18 · 9 · 8 · 1) = √1296 = 36.

2. Special triangles and similar triangles

Equilateral triangle of side s: height (√3/2)s and area (√3/4)s². For s = 6 the area is 9√3 (checked with Heron). Right isosceles (45-45-90): sides in ratio 1 : 1 : √2. 30-60-90: sides 1 : √3 : 2. Memorising these saves a great deal of work, and so does knowing the common Pythagorean triples 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25.

Similar triangles. Triangles with equal angles have proportional sides, and areas in the ratio of the squares of the scale factor. If the sides of a triangle are doubled (3-4-5 becomes 6-8-10), its area quadruples: from 6 to 24, which Heron's formula confirms.

The midsegment theorem. The segment joining the midpoints of two sides is parallel to the third side and half its length; it cuts off a triangle similar to the whole with ratio 1/2 and area 1/4. The four triangles formed by the three midsegments have equal areas.

Same height, same base. Triangles with the same height have areas proportional to their bases; the angle bisector from A in the 13-14-15 triangle meets BC at D with BD : DC = 13 : 15, so triangle ABD has area (13/28) · 84 = 39.

Try it

A triangle has area 20. A similar triangle has sides 3 times as long. What is its area?

Answer. 20 · 9 = 180.

3. Parallelograms, trapezoids, rhombi and kites

Parallelogram: area = base × height (not base × side). With sides 6 and 8 and a 30° angle, the height onto the side 8 is 3 and the area is 24, as ab sin θ confirms. Rhombus: area = ½d₁d₂. With diagonals 10 and 24 the side is √(5² + 12²) = 13 and the area is 120. Kite (two pairs of adjacent equal sides, perpendicular diagonals): area = ½d₁d₂; the kite with vertices (0, 0), (4, 3), (0, 10), (−4, 3) has diagonals 10 and 8 and area 40, confirmed by shoelace.

Trapezoid (one pair of parallel sides b₁, b₂): area = ½(b₁ + b₂)h. With bases 10 and 4 and legs 5 and 5, drop perpendiculars: each overhangs by (10 − 4)/2 = 3, so the height is √(25 − 9) = 4 and the area is ½ · 14 · 4 = 28 (shoelace agrees). The midsegment of a trapezoid has length (b₁ + b₂)/2.

Strategy. For a quadrilateral that is not special, split it into two triangles by a diagonal. When a figure is given by coordinates, use the shoelace formula; when it has perpendicular diagonals, use ½d₁d₂.

Cyclic quadrilaterals. For a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle with semiperimeter s, Brahmagupta's formula gives K = √((s − a)(s − b)(s − c)(s − d)). For sides 25, 39, 52, 60, which form a cyclic quadrilateral with perpendicular right-angled halves, s = 88 and K = √(63 · 49 · 36 · 28) = 1764 (computed).

Try it

A rhombus has side 10 and one diagonal 12. Find its area.

Answer. Half the diagonals and the side form a right triangle with hypotenuse 10 and leg 6, so the other half-diagonal is 8 and the diagonal 16. Area = ½ · 12 · 16 = 96.

4. Triangle inequality and counting triangles

Three lengths form a triangle exactly when each is smaller than the sum of the other two: a + b > c (checking the largest side suffices). Sides 3, 4, 8 fail since 3 + 4 < 8. The degenerate case a + b = c is a flat triangle with zero area.

Counting integer triangles. With perimeter 12 the triangles with integer sides are (2, 5, 5), (3, 4, 5) and (4, 4, 4), found by listing a ≤ b ≤ c with a + b > c. For perimeter 30 a program finds 19 such triangles. A quick bound: the largest side must be less than half the perimeter.

Range of the third side. If two sides are 7 and 12, the third side x satisfies 5 < x < 19, so there are 13 integer values (6 through 18).

Try it

How many triangles with integer sides have perimeter 10?

Answer. a ≤ b ≤ c, c < 5: (2, 4, 4) and (3, 3, 4). So 2.

5. A longer problem

Problem. In triangle ABC with AB = 13, BC = 14, CA = 15, point D lies on BC with BD = 6. Find (a) the area of ABC, (b) the area of ABD, (c) the length AD.

Solution. (a) 84. (b) ABD and ABC share the vertex A, so their areas are proportional to BD and BC: (6/14) · 84 = 36. (c) The height from A to BC is 12 and the foot is at distance 5 from B (coordinates B = (0, 0), A = (5, 12)); D = (6, 0), so AD = √((6 − 5)² + 12²) = √145.

Reflection. Area ratios and coordinates complement each other: the first gives (b) in one line, the second gives (c). Cross-checking with a second method catches slips.

6. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Using a slanted side instead of the height. (2) Forgetting ½ in triangle area. (3) Using the wrong semiperimeter in Heron. (4) Scaling area by k instead of k². (5) Forgetting the triangle inequality when counting.

Checks. Compute the area in two ways when possible. Check that a trapezoid's height is less than its legs. Check Heron's output against ½bh with a known height.

How to practise. Make a one-page list of the formulas in this unit and apply each to a concrete figure. In Unlimited practice, rotate the area and similar-triangle families.

Problem p1. A right triangle has legs 9 and 12. Find the altitude to the hypotenuse as a fraction.

HintHypotenuse 15; area two ways.
Answer36/5
Method: Area two ways

The hypotenuse is √(81 + 144) = 15. Area = 9·12/2 = 54 = 15·h/2, so h = 108/15 = 36/5.

Method: Similar triangles

The altitude splits the triangle into two triangles similar to it: h/9 = 12/15, so h = 36/5.

Problem p8. Find the area of the quadrilateral with vertices (0,0), (8,0), (10,5), (2,6).

HintUse the shoelace formula or split into triangles.
Answer45
Method: Shoelace

Problem p9. How many right triangles with positive integer sides have hypotenuse at most 50? (Count similar triangles of different sizes separately; a triangle and its reflection are the same.)

HintCount (a, b, c) with a ≤ b.
Answer20
Method: Primitive triples and multiples

There are seven primitive triples with hypotenuse at most 50: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25), (20,21,29), (12,35,37), (9,40,41). Counting their multiples with hypotenuse at most 50: 3-4-5 gives 10 (hypotenuses 5, 10, …, 50), 5-12-13 gives 3, 8-15-17 gives 2, 7-24-25 gives 2, and the last three give 1 each. 10 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 20.

Method: Computer count

Checking all a ≤ b < c ≤ 50 with a² + b² = c² gives exactly 20.

Source: Rusczyk, Art of Problem Solving Vol. 1, ch. 11-12 (Triangles; Quadrilaterals) (Pythagorean triples)

Problem p11. The median to a side of length 10 in a triangle is 9 long and is perpendicular to a second median. Find the exact length of the third median.

HintUse m² = (2b² + 2c² − a²)/4 and the perpendicular-medians relation.
Answer3√13
Method: Medians formula

Let a = 10, with median m_a = 9 perpendicular to m_b. Then 81 = (2b² + 2c² − 100)/4 gives b² + c² = 212. Perpendicular medians from A and B mean a² + b² = 5c², so 100 + b² = 5c². Solving: c² = 52, b² = 160. The third median m_c² = (2·100 + 2·160 − 52)/4 = 117, so m_c = 3√13.

Method: Coordinates

Put B = (0,0), C = (10,0) and A = (−0.4, y) with AB² = 52 so A = (−0.4, √51.84) = (−0.4, 7.2). Then the median from A goes to (5,0): length √(5.4² + 7.2²) = 9. The median from B goes to the midpoint (4.8, 3.6) of AC, so it is perpendicular to the first (check: (5.4, −7.2)·(4.8, 3.6) = 0). The median from C goes to the midpoint (−0.2, 3.6) of AB, length √(10.2² + 3.6²) = √117 = 3√13.

Source: Rusczyk, Art of Problem Solving Vol. 1, ch. 11-12 (Triangles; Quadrilaterals), Problem 193 (MAG 1990)
