Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, chapter 4 (Existence): showing an arrangement exists by building one, and counting small cases.

Reading

1. Two ways to show something exists

A statement 'there exists an x with property P' can be proved constructively (write down such an x) or non-constructively (show that none existing leads to a contradiction, or count). Constructions often start with small cases and look for the pattern.

Worked example 1. There are arbitrarily long runs of consecutive composite numbers. For n = 5 take 6! + 2, …, 6! + 6 = 722, 723, 724, 725, 726: 722 = 2·361, 723 = 3·241, 724 = 4·181, 725 = 5·145, 726 = 6·121. Each is divisible by a number in 2 to 6 and is bigger than it, so all five are composite. In general (n + 1)! + 2, …, (n + 1)! + (n + 1).

Worked example 2. There are infinitely many Pythagorean triples: for k = 1, 2, 3, … the triple (2k + 1, 2k² + 2k, 2k² + 2k + 1) gives (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (7, 24, 25), and in general (2k+1)² + (2k²+2k)² = (2k²+2k+1)².

3. Existence by continuity and counting

Intermediate value theorem. A continuous function that takes values of opposite sign at the ends of an interval has a root inside. For f(x) = x³ + x − 1: f(0) = −1 and f(1) = 1, so there is a root between 0 and 1.

Pigeonhole. Among any 5 integers, two have the same remainder mod 4; so two of them differ by a multiple of 4. Among any n + 1 integers, two differ by a multiple of n.

Counting. If there are more objects than something that could distinguish them, two coincide. Example: a 10-element set has 1024 subsets, while the possible subset sums of {1, …, 10} lie in 0 to 55, which is only 56 values, so two different subsets must have the same sum.

4. Constructing examples

To disprove a claim you need only one counterexample. To prove an upper bound is best possible you must construct an example reaching it. Good habits: begin with small n; look for structure (symmetry, arithmetic progressions, powers of 2); modify an example that almost works.

Worked example. Show that a set of 8 integers can have no three terms in arithmetic progression: {1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 11, 13, 14} works (checked by computing every triple).

A bound with matching construction. The largest subset of {1, …, 20} with no two consecutive numbers has 10 elements: it cannot have more by pairing (1,2), (3,4), …, and the odd numbers reach 10.

5. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Giving an example for a single case and calling it a proof of 'for all'. Forgetting to check every condition in the construction.

Checks. Verify the example by direct computation.

Practice. Start by trying to make the claim fail; the obstruction suggests the proof.

Worked examples with Try-its: Proving something exists

Existence proofs can be constructive (build the object) or non-constructive (pigeonhole, extremal choice, intermediate value, counting). Example: there exist 5 consecutive composites (722 to 726). The Chicken McNugget problem: with packs of 6, 9 and 20 the largest unreachable total is 43. A counterexample disproves a universal claim: n² + n + 41 is prime for n = 0 to 39 but not for n = 40 (1681 = 41²).

Try it

Is n² − n + 41 prime when n = 41?

Answer. 41² − 41 + 41 = 41², composite.

Worked examples with Try-its: Construction techniques

Try small cases, look for a pattern, then prove it. Build by induction (add one element to a valid example of size n). When asked to place numbers or objects so that a property holds, start from a symmetric or extremal arrangement and adjust.

Try it

Construct 4 consecutive integers none of which is prime.

Answer. 24, 25, 26, 27 (or 8, 9, 10 for three).

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Show that there exist two powers of 2 whose difference is divisible by 1000. Solution. Consider 2⁰, 2¹, …, 2¹⁰⁰⁰, which is 1001 numbers; two share a remainder mod 1000 by pigeonhole, so their difference is divisible by 1000.

Reflection. The proof gives no explicit pair; it only shows one exists.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Intermediate value. The function f(x) = x³ − x − 1 has f(1) = −1 < 0 and f(2) = 5 > 0, so it has a root between 1 and 2 (in fact between 1.3 and 1.4, since f(1.3) < 0 < f(1.4)). Non-constructive. Either √2^√2 is rational, or (√2^√2)^√2 = 2 is a rational power of an irrational one; in either case irrational a and b exist with aᵇ rational.

Try it

Show that x⁵ + x − 1 = 0 has a real root in (0, 1).

Answer. f(0) = −1 < 0 and f(1) = 1 > 0, so by the intermediate value theorem there is a root.

Try it

Construct 5 consecutive composite numbers.

Answer. 722, 723, 724, 725, 726 (6! + 2 to 6! + 6).

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Show that there is a multiple of 7 whose decimal digits are all 1s and 0s. Solution. Consider the 8 numbers 1, 11, 111, …, 11111111. Two have the same remainder mod 7, so their difference, a number of the form 11…100…0, is divisible by 7.

Check. 1111111 − 1 = 1111110 = 7 · 158730.

Problem p1. How many ways can 8 queens be placed on an 8 × 8 chessboard so that no two attack each other?

HintPlace one queen per row and search.
Answer92
Method: Backtracking

Place the queens row by row, rejecting a column or diagonal that is already used. The search finds 92 solutions.

Method: Structure

Of these, 12 are essentially different once rotations and reflections are identified.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 (Existence)

Problem p2. How many 3 × 3 Latin squares (each row and each column uses the symbols 1, 2, 3 once) are there?

HintBuild row by row.
Answer12
Method: Count

The first row can be any of 6 permutations; for each, the second row must be a derangement of it (2 choices) and the third row is then forced: 6 · 2 · 1 = 12.

Method: Search

An exhaustive search over row choices gives 12.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 (Existence)

Answer576

Method: Check

Problem p4. How many different 3 × 3 magic squares can be made from the numbers 1 to 9 (all rows, columns and both diagonals have the same sum)? Count rotations and reflections as different.

HintThe common sum must be 15 and the centre must be 5.
Answer8
Method: Structure

The row sum is 15 and the centre is 5; there is essentially one square, with 8 rotations and reflections, so 8.

Method: Search

An exhaustive search over the 362880 arrangements finds 8.

Problem p5. What is the largest number of non-attacking kings on an 8 × 8 board?

HintSplit the board into 2 × 2 blocks.
Answer16
Method: Blocks

Each 2 × 2 block holds at most one king, and there are 16 blocks, so at most 16. Placing a king in the top-left square of every block gives 16.

Method: Check

Those kings are at least 2 apart in each direction, so they do not attack.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 (Existence)

Problem p6. What is the largest number of non-attacking knights on an 8 × 8 board?

HintA knight attacks only squares of the opposite colour.
Answer32
Method: Colour

Put all knights on squares of one colour; a knight move changes colour, so none attack. That gives 32.

Method: Bound

Cut the board into eight 2 × 4 rectangles. In each one, the 8 squares form 4 pairs a knight's move apart, so a rectangle holds at most 4 non-attacking knights, and the board at most 8 · 4 = 32.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 (Existence)

Problem p7. What is the largest number of non-attacking bishops on an 8 × 8 board?

Hint2n − 2.
Answer14
Method: Formula

2 · 8 − 2 = 14.

Method: Construction

Place bishops along the whole top row and the whole bottom row except the two corners of the bottom row: 8 + 6 = 14, and no two attack.

Problem p8. What is the fewest straight lines needed to cover all 9 points of a 3 × 3 grid of points?

HintA line contains at most 3 of the points.
Answer3
Method: Bound

Each line covers at most 3 points, so at least 3 lines are needed, and the 3 rows work.

Method: Two lines

Two lines cover at most 6 points, which is less than 9.

Problem p14. What is the smallest positive integer with distinct digits whose digits sum to 20?

HintUse as few digits as possible, larger at the end.
Answer389
Method: Three digits

Two digits give at most 9 + 8 = 17, so 3 digits are needed. The smallest hundreds digit is 3 (3 + 9 + 8 = 20), leaving 389.

Method: Check

With hundreds digit 1 or 2 the remaining two digits would need to sum to 19 or 18, impossible with distinct digits.

Hintlcm · gcd = product.
Answer72
