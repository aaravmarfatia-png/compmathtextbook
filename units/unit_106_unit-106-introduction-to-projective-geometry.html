Reading

1. Points at infinity and homogeneous coordinates

In projective geometry parallel lines meet at a point at infinity, so any two distinct lines meet in exactly one point and any two distinct points lie on exactly one line. In homogeneous coordinates the point (x, y) is written [x : y : 1], and [x : y : 0] is the point at infinity in the direction (x, y). A line ax + by + c = 0 is written [a : b : c]. The line through two points, and the point on two lines, are both given by a cross product.

Worked example. The parallel lines x + y = 1 and x + y = 3 are [1, 1, −1] and [1, 1, −3]. Their cross product is (1·(−3) − (−1)·1, (−1)·1 − 1·(−3), 1·1 − 1·1) = (−2, 2, 0), the point at infinity in the direction (−1, 1): exactly the direction of those lines.

2. Ceva, Menelaus, Desargues, Pappus

Ceva: points D, E, F on sides BC, CA, AB give concurrent lines AD, BE, CF iff (BD/DC)(CE/EA)(AF/FB) = 1. Menelaus: D, E, F on the side lines are collinear iff the same product (with signed lengths) is −1.

Worked example. If BD/DC = 1/2 and CE/EA = 2/3, the third cevian is concurrent iff AF/FB = 3, since 3 × (1/2) × (2/3) = 1 ✓. For the medians all three ratios are 1, so the medians are concurrent; for the angle bisectors the ratios are the side ratios and their product is 1, giving the incentre.

Desargues: if two triangles are in perspective from a point (the lines joining corresponding vertices are concurrent) then they are in perspective from a line (corresponding sides meet at collinear points), and conversely. Pappus: if A, B, C are collinear and D, E, F are collinear, then the intersections of AE and BD, AF and CD, BF and CE are collinear.

3. Duality and why it helps

Swapping 'point' and 'line' turns a true statement of projective geometry into another true statement. Desargues' theorem is its own dual. Pappus' theorem dualises to another form of Pappus. Dualising Ceva gives the Menelaus theorem structure.

A projective trick. A projective transformation can move any four points in general position to the corners of a square, so to prove a statement about collinearity or concurrency it is enough to prove it for a nice configuration.

Worked example. Any projective map preserves the cross ratio (Unit 129), which is the key invariant of projective geometry: for points 0, 1, 2, 3 on a line the cross ratio (0, 1; 2, 3) = 4/3.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Forgetting signs in Menelaus' theorem. Using Ceva's theorem when the points are on the extensions (use signed ratios). Assuming that a projective map preserves lengths or angles (it preserves incidence and cross ratio only).

Checks. Test with the medians (all ratios 1).

Practice. Choose a convenient triangle (a right triangle with legs on the axes) and use coordinates.

Worked examples with Try-its: Points at infinity and duality

Projective geometry adds a 'line at infinity' so that any two lines meet in exactly one point (parallel lines meet at infinity). Points and lines can then be swapped: the dual of 'three points are collinear' is 'three lines are concurrent'. Central projection from one line to another preserves the cross ratio: for the Möbius map x ↦ (2x + 1)/(x + 3), the cross ratio of the points 0, 1, 2, 3 is unchanged (checked exactly).

Try it

Under the map x ↦ 1/x, where does the point 0 go?

Answer. To infinity.

Worked examples with Try-its: Classic theorems

Desargues: two triangles are in perspective from a point exactly when they are in perspective from a line. Pappus: if A, B, C lie on one line and D, E, F on another, the points where AE meets BD, AF meets CD and BF meets CE are collinear. Pascal: for six points on a conic, the three intersection points of opposite sides of the hexagon are collinear.

Try it

In how many points do two distinct lines meet in the projective plane?

Answer. Exactly one (possibly at infinity).

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Show that cross ratio is unchanged by x ↦ (ax + b)/(cx + d). Check. For a map of this form, (f(x) − f(y)) = (ad − bc)(x − y)/((cx + d)(cy + d)). Substituting this into the cross ratio, the factors (ad − bc) and (cx + d) cancel in pairs, leaving the original ratio.

Reflection. This identity is the algebraic heart of projective geometry.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Under g(x) = (x + 1)/(x + 2) the points 0, 1, 2, 3 go to 1/2, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, and the cross ratio is unchanged (computed exactly). Harmonic conjugacy is also preserved: (0, 6; 4, 12) is harmonic, and so are the images of those four points. A projective map is determined by where it sends three points.

Try it

Where does x ↦ 1/(1 − x) send 0, 2 and ∞?

Answer. To 1, −1 and 0.

Try it

In how many points do two distinct lines meet in the projective plane?

Answer. Exactly one.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Find the projective map x ↦ (ax + b)/(cx + d) that sends 0 to 1, 1 to 2 and ∞ to 3. Solution. Sending ∞ to 3 means a/c = 3. Sending 0 to 1 means b/d = 1, so b = d. Take c = 1, a = 3, b = d: the value at 1 is (3 + d)/(1 + d) = 2, so d = 1. The map is x ↦ (3x + 1)/(x + 1).

Check. At 0: 1; at 1: 4/2 = 2; as x → ∞: 3 ✓.

Practice problems (25)

Problem p1. How many points does the projective plane over the field with 5 elements have?

Hintq² + q + 1.
Answer31
Method: Formula

25 + 5 + 1 = 31.

Method: Count

Points are nonzero triples up to scaling: (5³ − 1)/(5 − 1) = 124/4 = 31.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p2. In the projective plane over the field with 5 elements, how many lines pass through a given point?

Hintq + 1.
Answer6
Method: Formula

q + 1 = 6.

Method: Duality

By duality the number of lines through a point equals the number of points on a line, which is q + 1 = 6.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p3. How many points are there on each line of the Fano plane (the projective plane over the field with 2 elements)?

Hintq + 1.
Answer3
Method: Formula

2 + 1 = 3.

Method: Total

7 points and 7 lines, each line with 3 points and each point on 3 lines.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p4. The lines x + y = 3 and 2x − y = 0 meet at (a, b). Find a + b.

HintAdd the equations.
Answer3
Method: Solve

Adding gives 3x = 3, so x = 1 and y = 2: a + b = 3.

Method: Homogeneous

The cross product of the line vectors (1, 1, −3) and (2, −1, 0) is (−3, −6, −3) ∝ (1 : 2 : 1), the point (1, 2).

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p5. Find the harmonic conjugate of C = 4 with respect to A = 2 and B = 8 on the number line. (That is, the point D with (A, B; C, D) = −1.)

HintAC/CB = 2/4 and DA/DB must equal 1/2 externally.
Answer-4
Method: Equation

(4 − 2)(d − 8)/((4 − 8)(d − 2)) = −1 gives 2(d − 8) = 4(d − 2), so d = −4.

Method: Check

DA = 6 and DB = 12, ratio 1/2, matching AC/CB = 2/4.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p6. The projective map f(x) = (2x + 1)/(x + 1) is applied to x = 1. What is f(1), as a fraction?

HintSubstitute.
Answer3/2
Method: Substitute

(2 + 1)/(1 + 1) = 3/2.

Method: Matrix

The matrix ((2, 1), (1, 1)) sends (1 : 1) to (3 : 2).

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p7. The map f(x) = (x + 2)/(x + 1) has two fixed points. What is their product?

HintSolve x(x + 1) = x + 2.
Answer-2
Method: Quadratic

x² + x = x + 2 gives x² = 2, so the fixed points are ±√2 and their product is −2.

Method: Vieta

The equation x² − 2 = 0 has product of roots −2.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p8. How many triples of non-collinear points (triangles) are there in the Fano plane?

HintAll triples minus collinear triples.
Answer28
Method: Count

C(7,3) − 7 = 35 − 7 = 28.

Method: Direct

Choose 2 points (21 ways), then any of the 4 points not on their line: 21 · 4/3 = 28.

Source: Coxeter & Greitzer ch. 6

Problem p9. Two distinct lines in the projective plane meet in how many points?

HintParallel lines meet at infinity.
Answer1
Method: Axiom

Exactly 1 (parallel lines meet at a point at infinity).

Method: Algebra

Two independent linear equations in three homogeneous unknowns have a one-dimensional solution space, so one point.

Answer1

Two points lie on exactly one line, and dually two lines meet in exactly one point.
