Unit 101: Unit 101: Algorithms: searching, sorting and weighing

Book: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms) (25 problems)
Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, chapters 1-2 (Algorithms), covering information bounds and efficient procedures.

Reading

1. Searching: binary search

To find a hidden number from 1 to N by asking yes/no questions, each answer can at best halve the possibilities. After k questions at most 2^k possibilities can be told apart, so you need k ≥ log₂ N. For N = 100 you need 7 questions, since 2⁶ = 64 < 100 ≤ 128 = 2⁷. The strategy 'is it at most the middle value?' achieves this.

Worked example. To find a number from 1 to 1000 you need ⌈log₂ 1000⌉ = 10 questions (2⁹ = 512 < 1000 ≤ 1024).

Information bound. With r possible outcomes per test and k tests there are at most r^k distinguishable cases. This counting bound proves a lower bound for every algorithm.

2. Sorting and comparisons

Sorting n distinct items by pairwise comparisons must distinguish n! orderings, so you need at least log₂(n!) comparisons. For n = 4: 4! = 24 and 2⁴ = 16 < 24 ≤ 32 = 2⁵, so 5 comparisons are needed (and 5 suffice). For n = 5: 120 ≤ 128 = 2⁷, so at least 7 comparisons.

Merge idea. Merging two sorted lists of lengths a and b takes at most a + b − 1 comparisons. The largest of n items needs exactly n − 1 comparisons, since each comparison eliminates at most one candidate.

Worked example. Finding both the largest and the smallest of 8 numbers: pair them up (4 comparisons), compare the 4 winners (3) and the 4 losers (3): 10 comparisons, fewer than the naive 7 + 6 = 13.

3. Weighing problems

A balance has three outcomes (left heavier, right heavier, equal), so w weighings distinguish at most 3^w cases. Nine coins, one heavier: weigh 3 against 3; the result tells you which group of three holds the heavy coin; weigh two of that group. 2 weighings (3² = 9).

Twelve coins, one different (heavier or lighter, unknown). There are 24 cases (12 coins × 2) and 3³ = 27 ≥ 24, so 3 weighings might suffice, and indeed they do with a careful scheme (4 against 4 first). With 13 coins it is possible to find the odd one but not always to say whether it is heavy or light.

Worked example. With 27 coins one heavy, 3 weighings find it (3³ = 27).

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Forgetting that the counting bound is a lower bound only; you must also give an algorithm. Using 2 outcomes for a balance (it has 3).

Checks. Always compare r^k with the number of cases.

Practice. Design the first test so the cases split as evenly as possible.

Worked examples with Try-its: Searching and information

Each yes/no question can at most halve the possibilities, so finding a number from 1 to 100 needs ⌈log₂ 100⌉ = 7 questions, and binary search achieves this. Sorting 5 items by comparisons needs at least ⌈log₂ 5!⌉ = ⌈log₂ 120⌉ = 7 comparisons. A balance scale has three outcomes, so k weighings distinguish up to 3ᵏ cases: 3 weighings can find one odd coin among 27 (if you only need to find it, not whether it is heavier or lighter).

Try it

How many yes/no questions are enough to identify a number from 1 to 1000?

Answer. 10, since 2¹⁰ = 1024 ≥ 1000.

Worked examples with Try-its: Counting steps

A bubble sort on n items uses at most C(n, 2) swaps: 10 for n = 5. The Euclidean algorithm for two numbers needs at most about 5 times the number of digits in steps. An algorithm is only correct if it works for every input, so test the extremes such as an empty list or all equal items.

Try it

What is the largest number of swaps bubble sort may need for 6 items?

Answer. C(6, 2) = 15.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. There are 12 coins, one of which is counterfeit and differs in weight (heavier or lighter, unknown). Can you identify it in 3 weighings? Solution. Yes: 12 coins × 2 possibilities = 24 outcomes ≤ 27. The standard method weighs 4 against 4 first and then follows the tilt (or balance) into a second weighing of 3 against 3 or similar.

Reflection. The counting bound 24 ≤ 27 says it might be possible; the method shows it is.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Weighing. Nine coins, one lighter: two weighings suffice (3 against 3, then 1 against 1 among the lightest group). Search. A number from 1 to 1000 is found with ⌈log₂ 1000⌉ = 10 yes/no questions. Euclid. For consecutive Fibonacci numbers 89 and 55 the Euclidean algorithm takes 9 steps, the slowest case for numbers of that size.

Try it

How many weighings find one lighter coin among 27 coins?

Answer. 3.

Try it

How many comparisons does it take to find the largest of 8 numbers?

Answer. 7.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Show that finding the largest of n numbers by comparisons needs at least n − 1 comparisons. Solution. Every number except the maximum must lose at least one comparison (otherwise we cannot know it is not the maximum), and each comparison produces one loser, so at least n − 1 comparisons are needed.

Reflection. The argument is an adversary or counting bound, not just a count of one algorithm.

Practice problems (25)

Problem p1. A number between 1 and 1000 is chosen. How many yes/no questions are enough in the worst case to find it?

Hint2^k ≥ 1000.
Answer10
Method: Binary search

2⁹ = 512 < 1000 ≤ 1024 = 2¹⁰, so 10 questions suffice and 9 do not.

Method: Halving

Each question can halve the remaining range, which takes 10 steps from 1000 down to 1.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p2. Among 12 coins one is counterfeit and may be heavier or lighter. What is the least number of weighings on a balance that is guaranteed to find the fake and say whether it is heavy or light?

HintThere are 24 cases and 3 outcomes per weighing.
Answer3
Method: Information

12 coins times 2 possibilities is 24 cases, and 3² = 9 < 24 ≤ 27 = 3³, so at least 3 weighings are needed.

Method: Known result

A careful 4 against 4 first weighing makes 3 weighings sufficient.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p3. What is the least number of comparisons that could sort any 5 distinct numbers in the worst case (information bound)?

Hint⌈log₂ 5!⌉.
Answer7
Method: Bound

5! = 120 and 2⁶ = 64 < 120 ≤ 128 = 2⁷, so at least 7 comparisons.

Method: Achievable

7 comparisons are known to be enough for 5 numbers (merge-insertion sort).

Problem p4. How many comparisons are needed to find the largest of 100 distinct numbers?

HintEach comparison eliminates one candidate.
Answer99
Method: Elimination

Each comparison eliminates one number from the running for maximum, and 99 must be eliminated: 99.

Method: Tournament

A knockout tournament of 100 players has 99 matches.

Problem p5. How many comparisons are enough to find both the largest and smallest of 8 distinct numbers?

HintCompare in pairs first: 3n/2 − 2.
Answer10
Method: Pairs

Compare 4 pairs (4 comparisons), then find the max among the 4 winners (3) and the min among the 4 losers (3): 10.

Method: Formula

3 · 8/2 − 2 = 10.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p6. A single-elimination tournament has 64 players. How many games are played?

HintEach game eliminates exactly one player.
Answer63
Method: Eliminations

All but one player is eliminated, so 63 games.

Method: Rounds

32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 63.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p7. In a knockout tournament of 8 players where the better player always wins, how many games are enough to determine both the best and the second-best player?

Hintn + ⌈log₂ n⌉ − 2.
Answer9
Method: Formula

8 + 3 − 2 = 9: 7 matches find the winner, then the 3 players who lost directly to the winner play off for second place (2 more matches).

Method: Reasoning

The second best must have lost only to the best, so it is among the winner's 3 opponents.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p8. A list of 6 numbers is in exactly reverse order. Bubble sort swaps adjacent out-of-order pairs. How many swaps are needed?

HintEach adjacent swap removes exactly one inversion.
Answer15
Method: Inversions

A reversed list of 6 has C(6,2) = 15 inversions, so 15 swaps.

Method: Passes

The passes make 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15 swaps.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p9. How many division steps does Euclid's algorithm use on (89, 55)?

HintFibonacci inputs are the slowest.
Answer9
Method: Run

89 = 1·55 + 34, 55 = 1·34 + 21, 34 = 1·21 + 13, 21 = 1·13 + 8, 13 = 1·8 + 5, 8 = 1·5 + 3, 5 = 1·3 + 2, 3 = 1·2 + 1, 2 = 2·1: 9 steps.

Method: Why

Consecutive Fibonacci numbers make every quotient 1.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p11. Nine coins look identical, one is lighter. What is the fewest number of balance weighings that always finds it?

HintSplit into three groups.
Answer2
Method: Thirds

Weigh 3 against 3; this finds the group of 3 containing the light coin, and one more weighing of 1 against 1 finds it.

Method: Bound

Each weighing has 3 outcomes, and 3² = 9 covers 9 coins.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem p12. Twenty-seven coins look identical, one is lighter. What is the fewest balance weighings that always finds it?

Hint3ⁿ.
Answer3
Method: Thirds

Each weighing narrows 27 to 9 to 3 to 1.

Method: Bound

3³ = 27 outcomes are enough and 3² = 9 is too few.

Problem p13. What is the fewest comparisons needed to find the largest of 10 distinct numbers?

HintEvery non-maximum must lose a comparison.
Answer9
Method: Tournament

Each of the 9 non-winners must lose at least once, and each comparison produces one loser: 9.

Method: Scan

Keep the largest so far and compare with the next 9 numbers.

HintAdd the pass lengths.
Answer15
Method: Sum

5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15.

Method: Pairs

It is C(6, 2) = 15.

Problem p15. How many inversions (pairs out of order) does 3, 1, 4, 2, 5 have?

HintCount pairs i < j with the earlier entry larger.
Answer3
Method: List

(3, 1), (3, 2) and (4, 2): 3.

Method: By element

3 exceeds 1 and 2 after it; 1 exceeds none; 4 exceeds 2; 2 and 5 exceed none: 2 + 0 + 1 = 3.

Answer5
Method: Information

2⁴ = 16 < 24 ≤ 32 = 2⁵, so at least 5 comparisons are needed, and merge insertion achieves 5.

Method: Counting

4! = 24 orderings must be distinguished.

Problem p17. How many division steps does the Euclidean algorithm take to find gcd(89, 55)?

HintCount each division with remainder, up to the one with remainder 0.
Answer9
Method: Steps

89 = 1·55 + 34, 55 = 1·34 + 21, 34 = 1·21 + 13, 21 = 1·13 + 8, 13 = 1·8 + 5, 8 = 1·5 + 3, 5 = 1·3 + 2, 3 = 1·2 + 1, 2 = 2·1 + 0: 9 divisions.

Method: Fibonacci

The quotients are all 1 (except the last), which is the slowest case for Euclid's algorithm; 55 and 89 are consecutive Fibonacci numbers.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

Problem x18. 61 objects are placed into 12 boxes. What is the largest number n for which some box must contain at least n objects?

HintPigeonhole principle.
Answer6
Method: Pigeonhole

⌈61/12⌉ = 6.

Method: Contradiction

If every box had at most 5 objects, there would be at most 12 · 5 = 60 < 61 objects in all.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 1-2 (Algorithms)

HintCondition on the last symbol.
Answer144
