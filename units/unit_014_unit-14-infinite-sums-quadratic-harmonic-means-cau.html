Reading

1. Infinite geometric series and repeating decimals

You met the sum of a geometric progression in Unit 7: a + ar + ar² + … + arⁿ⁻¹ = a(1 − rⁿ)/(1 − r). When |r| < 1 the power rⁿ becomes as small as you please as n grows, so the infinite sum a + ar + ar² + … has the value a/(1 − r). This is what we mean by 'adding infinitely many numbers': the partial sums get closer and closer to a limit, and the limit is the value of the series. When |r| ≥ 1 the terms do not shrink and no limit exists.

Worked example 1. 1 + 1/3 + 1/9 + … has a = 1 and r = 1/3, so the sum is 1/(1 − 1/3) = 3/2. Check with partial sums: 1, 1.333, 1.444, 1.481, 1.494, … approaching 1.5 ✓. Worked example 2 (repeating decimals). 0.2727… = 0.27 + 0.0027 + 0.000027 + … has a = 0.27 and r = 0.01, so the value is 0.27/0.99 = 27/99 = 3/11. This is the reason every repeating decimal is a fraction. In particular 0.999… = 0.9/(1 − 0.1) = 1: not 'almost' 1 but exactly 1.

Worked example 3 (a bouncing ball). A ball dropped from 10 m rebounds to 60% of its previous height each time. The total distance travelled is 10 + 2(6 + 3.6 + 2.16 + …) = 10 + 2 · 6/(1 − 0.6) = 10 + 30 = 40 m. Each bounce contributes the up-and-down pair, which is why the bounces are doubled.

When can an infinite sum of positive terms be finite? Only if the terms shrink fast enough. The terms of the harmonic series 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + … shrink to zero but the sum is nevertheless infinite, as the next section shows.

Try it

Find the sum of 6 − 2 + 2/3 − 2/9 + … and write 0.1666… as a fraction.

Answer. a = 6, r = −1/3: sum = 6/(1 + 1/3) = 6 · 3/4 = 9/2. And 0.1666… = 0.1 + 0.06 + 0.006 + … = 0.1 + 0.06/(1 − 0.1) = 0.1 + 1/15 = 1/6.

2. Series that telescope and series that diverge

Not every infinite sum is geometric. Some are tamed by telescoping: 1/(1·2) + 1/(2·3) + 1/(3·4) + … Each term is 1/k − 1/(k + 1), so the first n terms add to 1 − 1/(n + 1), which approaches 1. For n = 1000 the partial sum is exactly 1000/1001 ≈ 0.999, and the infinite sum is 1.

The harmonic series diverges. Group the terms: 1 + 1/2 + (1/3 + 1/4) + (1/5 + 1/6 + 1/7 + 1/8) + … The first bracket is greater than 2 × 1/4 = 1/2, the second is greater than 4 × 1/8 = 1/2, and in general the bracket from 1/(2ᵏ + 1) to 1/2ᵏ⁺¹ has 2ᵏ terms, each at least 1/2ᵏ⁺¹, so it exceeds 1/2. Adding infinitely many halves is infinite, so the sum grows without bound, though extremely slowly: the first 1024 terms add to only about 7.5.

Contrast with 1/k². The sum 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + … does converge, to π²/6 ≈ 1.6449 (a famous result of Euler); one proof of convergence compares 1/k² with 1/(k(k − 1)) = 1/(k − 1) − 1/k, which telescopes. So the terms 1/k are 'too big' and 1/k² 'small enough'.

A rule of thumb. If the terms do not tend to 0, the series diverges. If they tend to zero like a geometric progression, it converges. In between (like 1/k) you need a finer argument. The next sections are about finite sums and finite means, but this picture is helpful background.

Try it

Evaluate 1/(2·3) + 1/(3·4) + … + 1/(9·10).

Answer. Each term is 1/k − 1/(k + 1) from k = 2 to 9, so the sum is 1/2 − 1/10 = 2/5.

3. Four means and how they compare

For positive numbers a and b there are four standard 'averages': the harmonic mean HM = 2/(1/a + 1/b) = 2ab/(a + b); the geometric mean GM = √(ab); the arithmetic mean AM = (a + b)/2; and the quadratic mean (root mean square) QM = √((a² + b²)/2). The harmonic mean is the right average for rates: if you drive 60 km/h out and 40 km/h back over the same road, the average speed is HM = 2 × 60 × 40/100 = 48 km/h, not 50.

The chain: HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ QM, with equality throughout exactly when a = b. For a = 4 and b = 9: HM = 72/13 ≈ 5.538, GM = 6, AM = 6.5, QM = √48.5 ≈ 6.964, and indeed 5.538 < 6 < 6.5 < 6.964. The middle link GM ≤ AM is the AM–GM inequality of Unit 13. The last link follows from (a − b)² ≥ 0: 2(a² + b²) ≥ (a + b)², i.e. QM² ≥ AM². The first link is AM–GM applied to 1/a and 1/b: (1/a + 1/b)/2 ≥ 1/√(ab), and inverting gives GM ≥ HM.

Worked example (average speed). A car travels 120 km at 60 km/h and returns at 40 km/h. Total time: 2 h + 3 h = 5 h for 240 km, so the average speed is 48 km/h, the harmonic mean. The arithmetic mean 50 is too large because the car spends more time at the slow speed. Generally, the harmonic mean is for averaging rates over equal distances; the arithmetic mean for averaging rates over equal times.

Worked example (a proportion). If two positive numbers have AM 10 and GM 8, what are they? Then a + b = 20 and ab = 64, so they are the roots of t² − 20t + 64 = 0, namely 4 and 16 (check: √64 = 8 ✓).

Try it

Find the HM, GM, AM of 3 and 6 and check that HM ≤ GM ≤ AM.

Answer. HM = 2 · 18/9 = 4, GM = √18 ≈ 4.243, AM = 4.5, so 4 ≤ 4.243 ≤ 4.5 ✓.

4. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

One of the most powerful inequalities in all of mathematics is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality: for real numbers a, b, c, d, (a² + b²)(c² + d²) ≥ (ac + bd)², with equality exactly when a/c = b/d (the pairs are proportional). Proof: the difference of the two sides equals (ad − bc)², because (a² + b²)(c² + d²) = a²c² + a²d² + b²c² + b²d² while (ac + bd)² = a²c² + 2abcd + b²d², and subtracting leaves a²d² − 2abcd + b²c² = (ad − bc)².

It generalises to any number of terms: (a₁² + … + aₙ²)(b₁² + … + bₙ²) ≥ (a₁b₁ + … + aₙbₙ)². In words, 'the square of a dot product is at most the product of the squared lengths': geometrically the cosine of an angle never exceeds 1.

Worked example 1. Find the maximum of 3x + 4y subject to x² + y² = 1. By Cauchy–Schwarz (3x + 4y)² ≤ (3² + 4²)(x² + y²) = 25, so 3x + 4y ≤ 5. Equality occurs when (x, y) is proportional to (3, 4): x = 3/5, y = 4/5, and check: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1 ✓ and 9/5 + 16/5 = 5 ✓.

Worked example 2. If 2x + 3y = 13, find the minimum of x² + y². By Cauchy–Schwarz (2x + 3y)² ≤ (2² + 3²)(x² + y²), i.e. 169 ≤ 13(x² + y²), so x² + y² ≥ 13. Equality when (x, y) is proportional to (2, 3): x = 2, y = 3, and indeed 4 + 9 = 13 ✓. Geometrically 13 = (distance from the origin to the line 2x + 3y = 13)².

The 'Engel form' (Titu's lemma). For positive x, y: a²/x + b²/y ≥ (a + b)²/(x + y). It follows from Cauchy–Schwarz applied to (a/√x, b/√y) and (√x, √y). Example: for x + y = 1 the minimum of 1/x + 4/y is (1 + 2)²/(x + y) = 9, reached at x = 1/3, y = 2/3: 3 + 6 = 9 ✓.

Try it

If x² + y² = 4, what is the largest value of x + y? When does it occur?

Answer. (x + y)² ≤ (1 + 1)(x² + y²) = 8, so x + y ≤ 2√2, attained when x = y = √2.

5. Using the inequalities together

Many problems ask for an extreme value subject to a constraint, and the choice among AM–GM, Cauchy–Schwarz and the mean chain is a matter of recognising the shape. Signs of AM–GM: a sum with a fixed product (or the reverse). Signs of Cauchy–Schwarz: a linear expression ax + by with a constraint on squares, or sums of fractions like a²/x.

Worked example 1. Positive numbers a, b, c satisfy a + b + c = 1. Show that 1/a + 1/b + 1/c ≥ 9. By Titu's lemma, 1/a + 1/b + 1/c = 1²/a + 1²/b + 1²/c ≥ (1 + 1 + 1)²/(a + b + c) = 9, with equality at a = b = c = 1/3. Worked example 2. For positive x and y with x + y = 10, the minimum of x² + y² is 50 (QM ≥ AM: (x² + y²)/2 ≥ ((x + y)/2)² = 25, equality at x = y = 5).

Worked example 3 (comparing the tools). To bound (x + y)(1/x + 1/y), AM–GM gives ≥ 4 by expanding, and Cauchy–Schwarz gives the same: (x + y)(1/x + 1/y) ≥ (√x · 1/√x + √y · 1/√y)² = 4. Having two proofs of the same fact is useful: if one fails to generalise, the other may.

Reminder about equality. A bound is only the exact extreme value if equality can be achieved while satisfying all the constraints. In Example 1 the equality point a = b = c = 1/3 satisfies a + b + c = 1, so 9 is the exact minimum.

6. A longer problem

Problem. A wire of length 24 is bent into a rectangle with sides x and y. (a) Show that the diagonal d is at least 6√2. (b) Show that the area is at most 36. (c) For which rectangle do both extremes occur, and what is the connection between them?

Solution. The perimeter condition is x + y = 12. (a) The diagonal squared is d² = x² + y². By QM ≥ AM, (x² + y²)/2 ≥ ((x + y)/2)² = 36, so d² ≥ 72 and d ≥ √72 = 6√2 ≈ 8.485. (b) By AM–GM, xy ≤ ((x + y)/2)² = 36. (c) Both extremes hold exactly when x = y = 6, the square. The connection: d² = (x + y)² − 2xy = 144 − 2xy, so making the area as big as possible makes the diagonal as small as possible; indeed d² = 144 − 2 × 36 = 72 ✓.

Reflection. One identity d² = (x + y)² − 2xy ties the two inequalities together. When two extremal questions arise from the same data, look for an identity linking them: one proof then settles both.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Treating an infinite sum as finite arithmetic: a/(1 − r) needs |r| < 1. (2) Believing that terms tending to 0 guarantee a finite sum (the harmonic series is the counterexample). (3) Averaging rates with the AM when the HM is correct. (4) Applying Cauchy–Schwarz without checking that the equality case is consistent with the constraint. (5) Forgetting that the inequalities need positive numbers (for the means) or real numbers (for Cauchy–Schwarz).

Checks. Test every bound with an equality case and a non-equality case. Check partial sums of an infinite series numerically for a few terms. Verify the chain HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ QM with one example before using it.

How to practise. For each problem write: 'quantity', 'constraint', 'tool', 'equality point'. In the Unlimited practice section use the families on infinite sums, means and Cauchy–Schwarz.

Problem e1. (Achilles and the turtle) Find 1 + 1/10 + 1/100 + 1/1000 + … (as a fraction).

HintCall the sum S and multiply by 10.
Answer10/9
Method: Self-similarity

S = 1 + 1/10 + 1/100 + …; 10S = 10 + 1 + 1/10 + … = 10 + S. So 9S = 10 and S = 10/9.

Method: Decimal

1 + 0.1 + 0.01 + … = 1.111… = 1 + 1/9 = 10/9 (since 0.111… = 1/9).

Method: Formula

1/(1 − q) with q = 1/10: 1/(9/10) = 10/9.

Method: Physical

Achilles catches the turtle after distance S; the turtle runs S/10 in that time and started 1 ahead: S = 1 + S/10, S = 10/9.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, §45 (Zeno's paradox)

Problem e8. A ball is dropped from 8 m and after each bounce rises to half its previous height. What total distance does it travel (up and down) before stopping? (metres)

Hint8 + 2(4 + 2 + 1 + …).
Answer24
Method: Series

Down 8, then up and down 4 (twice 4), up and down 2, …: 8 + 2(4 + 2 + 1 + …) = 8 + 2 · 8 = 24, using 4 + 2 + 1 + … = 4/(1 − 1/2) = 8.

Method: Self-similarity

Let U be the total distance travelled after the first drop, starting from the top of a bounce to height h = 4. Going up and down at height 4 costs 8, then the same pattern repeats at half the size. So U = 8 + U/2, giving U = 16. Add the first drop of 8: total 24.

Problem cs2. If x² + y² = 1, what is the largest possible value of 3x + 4y?

Hint(3x + 4y)² ≤ (3² + 4²)(x² + y²).
Answer5
Method: Cauchy–Schwarz

(3x + 4y)² ≤ (9 + 16)(x² + y²) = 25, so 3x + 4y ≤ 5. Equality at (x, y) = (3/5, 4/5): 9/5 + 16/5 = 5 and (3/5)² + (4/5)² = 1.

Method: Geometry

3x + 4y = 5(3/5 · x + 4/5 · y) is 5 times a dot product of the point (x, y) on the unit circle with the unit vector (3/5, 4/5); the largest dot product is 1, when the vectors point the same way.
