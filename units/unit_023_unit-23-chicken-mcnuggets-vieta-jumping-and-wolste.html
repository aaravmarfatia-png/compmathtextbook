1. The coin problem for two denominations

A fast-food restaurant sells nuggets in boxes of 6, 9 and 20. Which quantities can you buy exactly? You can buy 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 20, 21, 24, … but not 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, 17, 19, 22, 23, 25, 28, 31, 34, 37 or 43. This is the Frobenius coin problem: given positive integers a₁, …, aₖ with gcd 1, find the largest integer that cannot be written as a non-negative integer combination of them. For the nugget boxes the answer is 43 (found by dynamic programming up to 200; after 43 every number can be made).

Two coprime denominations. For two coprime values a and b the largest unattainable number is ab − a − b. With 3 and 5: 15 − 8 = 7. The unattainable amounts are exactly 1, 2, 4, 7 (a count that confirms). With 4 and 7: 28 − 11 = 17; with 7 and 11: 77 − 18 = 59; with 9 and 13: 117 − 22 = 95; all checked by computer. The number of unattainable amounts is (a − 1)(b − 1)/2: for 7 and 11 it is 6 × 10/2 = 30.

Why ab − a − b cannot be made. Suppose ax + by = ab − a − b with x, y ≥ 0. Then a(x + 1) + b(y + 1) = ab. Modulo a we get b(y + 1) ≡ 0, so a divides y + 1, and y + 1 ≥ a; similarly b divides x + 1, so x + 1 ≥ b. But then a(x + 1) + b(y + 1) ≥ ab + ab, a contradiction. So it cannot be made. That every larger number can be made needs a small additional argument: among any a consecutive values of by (y = 0, …, a − 1) every residue modulo a occurs, so every N is of the form by + (a multiple of a), and for N > ab − a − b the multiple of a is non-negative.

Try it

What is the largest amount that cannot be paid exactly with 5-cent and 8-cent coins?

Answer. 5 × 8 − 5 − 8 = 27.

2. Counting the ways to make an amount

How many ways can 40 be written as 3x + 5y with x, y ≥ 0? Solve for the unknowns systematically: y must make 40 − 5y divisible by 3, so y ≡ 2 mod 3 (since 40 ≡ 1 and 5 ≡ 2 mod 3, we need 2y ≡ 1, y ≡ 2). The values are y = 2, 5, 8 (y ≤ 8), giving (x, y) = (10, 2), (5, 5), (0, 8). Three ways, as the computer search confirms.

General solution. If (x₀, y₀) is one solution to ax + by = n then all integer solutions are x = x₀ + bt, y = y₀ − at. For non-negative solutions count the integers t in the interval that keeps both x and y non-negative; roughly n/(ab) solutions exist.

Three or more denominations. For the nuggets (6, 9, 20) there is no simple formula; we compute by 'building up': a number is attainable if subtracting 6, 9 or 20 leaves an attainable number. The attainable numbers from 44 to 49 are 44 = 6 · 4 + 20, 45 = 9 · 5, 46 = 6 + 20 + 20, 47 = 9 + 9 + 9 + 20, 48 = 6 · 8, 49 = 9 + 20 + 20. Six consecutive attainable numbers means that all larger numbers are attainable too (just add 6 repeatedly). That is how one proves that 43 is the largest unattainable amount.

The 'consecutive run' trick. If k denominations include the value m, and you find m consecutive attainable numbers, then every larger number is attainable.

Try it

Is 44 a combination of 6, 9 and 20? Is 43?

Answer. 44 = 4 × 6 + 20 yes. 43: using 0, 1 or 2 twenties leaves 43, 23 or 3, none of which is a combination of 6 and 9 (43 and 23 are not multiples of 3 while 6 and 9 are; 3 is too small), so no.

3. Vieta jumping

Vieta jumping is a method for problems of the type: 'Let a and b be positive integers such that (a² + b²)/(ab + 1) is an integer. Prove that it is a perfect square.' The idea: set k to be that integer. Then a² − kab + b² = k, which is a quadratic in a with coefficients depending on b. If a is one root, Vieta's formulas say the other root is a′ = kb − a, also an integer (and also a = (b² − k)/a′ …). So from one solution (a, b) we get another solution (a′, b). By always jumping to the smaller solution, we descend until we reach a boundary case, from which the value of k can be read off.

The descent. Suppose (a, b) is a solution with a ≥ b > 0 and a + b as small as possible. The other root a′ = kb − a is an integer, and since a · a′ = b² − k we get a′ = (b² − k)/a < b²/a ≤ b ≤ a. If a′ ≥ 1, then (a′, b) would be a solution with a smaller sum, contradicting minimality. If a′ < 0, then a′² − kba′ + b² = a′² + b² + kb|a′| exceeds k (it is at least 1 + 1 + k), but it should equal k: impossible. So a′ = 0, and the equation reads b² = k: k is a perfect square. A computer search over 1 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ 200 finds only perfect-square values (1, 4, 9, 16, …) of the quotient.

Jumping in practice. From (2, 8) with k = 4 (since 4 + 64 = 68 = 4 · 17) the jump gives (8, 30): 8² + 30² = 964 = 4 · 241 ✓. And in the other direction (2, 8) comes from (0, 2): 0 + 4 = 4 = 4 · 1. So the whole family sits on a single ladder that ends at a boundary pair (0, √k).

What to remember. Fix one variable, treat the equation as a quadratic in the other, use that the sum and product of the roots are integers, and descend to a boundary.

Try it

Check that (a, b) = (2, 8) satisfies (a² + b²)/(ab + 1) = 4 and find the next solution by jumping.

Answer. (4 + 64)/(16 + 1) = 68/17 = 4. Next: a′ = kb − a = 4 · 8 − 2 = 30, giving (8, 30): (64 + 900)/241 = 4 ✓.

4. Markov-type equations and more jumping

The equation x² + y² + z² = 3xyz has the obvious solution (1, 1, 1). Fix y and z and regard it as a quadratic in x: x² − 3yz x + (y² + z²) = 0. If x is one root, the other is x′ = 3yz − x. So from (1, 1, 1) we jump to (2, 1, 1) (indeed 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 = 3 · 2), then to (2, 5, 1) (that is (1, 2, 5): 1 + 4 + 25 = 30 = 3 · 10), then (1, 5, 13), (2, 5, 29), (1, 13, 34), and so on: infinitely many solutions in positive integers, called Markov triples. Each triple above was verified by direct substitution.

Why this matters. Jumping can generate infinitely many solutions from one, or prove that none exist beyond a boundary family. The two directions — descent for proofs of impossibility, ascent for constructing solutions — are the same technique.

Try it

Starting from (1, 1, 1) of x² + y² + z² = 3xyz, jump the first coordinate to find a new solution.

Answer. x′ = 3 · 1 · 1 − 1 = 2, giving (2, 1, 1): 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 = 3 · 2 ✓.

5. Wolstenholme's theorem

Fermat's little theorem says that for a prime p and 0 < k < p, the binomial coefficient C(p, k) is divisible by p. A much deeper statement is Wolstenholme's theorem: for a prime p ≥ 5, C(2p − 1, p − 1) ≡ 1 (mod p³). Check: p = 5 gives C(9, 4) = 126 = 125 + 1 ✓. p = 7 gives C(13, 6) = 1716 = 5 · 343 + 1 ✓. For p = 3 the statement fails: C(5, 2) = 10 is not ≡ 1 mod 27, which is why the condition p ≥ 5 is needed. A computer check for p = 5, 7, 11, 13 confirms the congruence and also C(2p, p) ≡ 2 (mod p³) for p = 5, 7, 11.

The harmonic form. The same theorem says that when 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/(p − 1) is written as a fraction in lowest terms, the numerator is divisible by p². For p = 5 the sum is 25/12, and 25 is divisible by 25; for p = 7 it is 49/20 and 49 is divisible by 49 ✓. The idea of the proof: pair k with p − k to get 1/k + 1/(p − k) = p/(k(p − k)), so the sum equals p times a sum of fractions, and a second pairing argument shows the remaining sum is itself divisible by p.

How it is used. In competitions, Wolstenholme's theorem is a way of establishing a divisibility by p² or p³ that ordinary Fermat arguments cannot reach. Often the actual proof one is asked to give is only the pairing step k ↔ p − k, because it already gives divisibility by p.

Try it

Verify Wolstenholme's congruence for p = 5 by computing C(9, 4) mod 125.

Answer. C(9, 4) = 126 = 125 + 1, so the residue is 1.

6. A longer problem

Problem. (a) Find the largest amount that cannot be paid with coins of values 7 and 11, and the number of amounts that cannot be paid. (b) Show that 23 cannot be formed from 6, 9 and 20, but that 44 through 49 can. (c) Find a pair (a, b) with a < b and (a² + b²)/(ab + 1) = 9.

Solution. (a) 7 · 11 − 7 − 11 = 59 and (7 − 1)(11 − 1)/2 = 30 amounts. (b) For 23 the options for the number of twenties are 0 and 1: 23 and 3 must be combinations of 6 and 9, but neither is a multiple of 3. The numbers 44 through 49 were built in section 2. (c) Take k = 9: the boundary solution is (a, b) = (0, 3), and the jump a′ = kb − a gives (3, 27): 9 + 729 = 738 = 9 · 82 and 3 · 27 + 1 = 82 ✓.

Reflection. The three parts use three different ideas — a formula plus verification, a residue argument, and descent — but all of them reduce to controlling the integer solutions of a linear or quadratic equation.

7. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Applying ab − a − b when a and b are not coprime. (2) Stopping a coin-problem search too early: always find a run of consecutive attainable values as long as the smallest coin. (3) In Vieta jumping, forgetting to verify that the new root is a positive integer. (4) Forgetting the boundary case a′ = 0. (5) Quoting Wolstenholme for p = 3.

Checks. Substitute the claimed combination into the equation. For a jump, verify the new pair satisfies the original equation. For the coin problem, test the largest unattainable number with a direct search.

How to practise. Build the table of attainable numbers for two small coprime values by hand; then predict the largest gap before checking with the formula. In Unlimited practice, work the coin-problem and Vieta families.
