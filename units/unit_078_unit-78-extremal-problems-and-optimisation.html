Reading

1. Optimisation with algebra and inequalities

We often want the largest or smallest value of a quantity. Tools: completing the square (Unit 10), AM–GM (Unit 13), Cauchy–Schwarz (Unit 14), and checking the endpoints.

Worked example 1. A rectangle has area 100; its perimeter is 2(x + 100/x) ≥ 2 × 2√100 = 40, with equality for the square 10 × 10.

Worked example 2. Triangles with two sides of length 5 and 8 have area ½ × 5 × 8 × sin θ ≤ 20, with equality when θ = 90°.

Worked example 3: splitting a number. Split 10 into positive integers to maximise the product. Using 3s is best: 3 + 3 + 4 gives 36 and 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 also gives 36; no other split beats 36. For 12: 3⁴ = 81. The idea: parts bigger than 4 can be split to give more (n < 2(n − 2) when n > 4), and 1s should be absorbed.

2. Geometry optimisation

Shortest path by reflection (Unit 53). Heron's problem: the point P on a line minimising AP + PB lies where the angles of incidence and reflection are equal. Fermat point: the point minimising the sum of distances to the vertices of a triangle with all angles < 120° is where the sides subtend 120° angles. For an equilateral triangle of side 1 the minimum total is √3.

Worked example. Among all rectangles inscribed in a circle of radius R, the largest area is the square, 2R². With sides 2R cos θ and 2R sin θ (a rectangle whose diagonal is the diameter), the area is 4R² sin θ cos θ = 2R² sin 2θ ≤ 2R².

Isoperimetric principle. Among shapes of a given perimeter, the circle has the largest area; among rectangles, the square.

3. Discrete optimisation

With integers, check the values near the real optimum. To maximise n(20 − n) over integers, the real maximum is at n = 10, and the integer answer is 100.

Linear programming. Maximise 3x + 2y subject to x + y ≤ 4, x ≤ 3, x, y ≥ 0. The feasible region is a polygon with vertices (0, 0), (3, 0), (3, 1), (0, 4); the objective values are 0, 9, 11, 8; the maximum is 11 at (3, 1). A linear function on a polygon is maximised at a vertex.

Principle. For linear objectives, look at the vertices of the allowed region; for quadratics, at the vertex of the parabola and the endpoints.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Ignoring the domain. Using AM–GM when equality cannot be reached. Forgetting to check the endpoints. Returning the optimising point instead of the optimal value (or vice versa).

Checks. Compare the optimum with nearby points.

Practice. State what you maximise, what is fixed, and the allowed range.

Worked examples with Try-its: Optimisation by inequalities

AM-GM says (x + y)/2 ≥ √(xy) for non-negative x and y, with equality when x = y. Hence if x + y = 10 the product xy is at most 25, and if xy = 36 the sum x + y is at least 12. A rectangle of perimeter 20 has area at most 25 (a square), and a rectangle of area 36 has perimeter at least 24 (a 6 × 6 square). Also x + 1/x ≥ 2 for x > 0.

Sums of squares. If x + y = 10 then x² + y² ≥ (x + y)²/2 = 50, with equality at x = y = 5. Cauchy–Schwarz. (3x + 4y)² ≤ (3² + 4²)(x² + y²), so if x² + y² = 25 then 3x + 4y ≤ 25, attained at (3, 4).

Try it

What is the minimum of x + 9/x for x > 0?

Answer. 6, at x = 3.

Worked examples with Try-its: Completing the square and boundaries

A quadratic ax² + bx + c with a > 0 has minimum c − b²/4a at x = −b/2a. For x² − 6x + 11 the minimum is 2 at x = 3. For problems on a closed interval check the endpoints as well as the vertex. In integer problems the extreme may sit at a boundary where equality in the inequality is impossible, so check that equality can actually be attained.

Try it

Largest value of 12x − x²?

Answer. 36, at x = 6.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. A farmer has 40 m of fence for a rectangular pen against a long straight wall (no fence needed on the wall side). Maximise the area. Solution. If the sides perpendicular to the wall have length x, the parallel side is 40 − 2x and the area is x(40 − 2x) = −2(x − 10)² + 200, at most 200 when x = 10.

Reflection. Without the wall the best pen would be a 10 × 10 square of area 100; the wall doubles the result.

More worked examples: Worked examples

AM-GM. For x > 0, x² + 16/x² ≥ 2 · √16 = 8, with equality at x² = 4. Trigonometry. sin θ + cos θ = √2 sin(θ + 45°) ≤ √2. Geometry. A rectangle inscribed in a circle of radius 5 has diagonal 10, so its area xy ≤ (x² + y²)/2 = 50, attained by the square. Among triangles of perimeter 12, the equilateral one has area 4√3 ≈ 6.93, larger than the 3-4-5 triangle's 6.

Try it

Maximise the area of a rectangle with perimeter 36.

Answer. A 9 × 9 square: 81.

Try it

Find the minimum of x + 25/x for x > 0.

Answer. 10, at x = 5.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Find the maximum of xy given 2x + 3y = 12 with x, y > 0. Solution. By AM-GM 12 = 2x + 3y ≥ 2√(6xy), so xy ≤ 6, attained at 2x = 3y = 6, i.e. x = 3, y = 2.

Check. At (3, 2): 6 + 6 = 12 and xy = 6 ✓.

Problem p1. Integers x and y satisfy x + y = 20. What is the largest possible value of xy?

HintMake the numbers equal.
Answer100
Method: Equal parts

x = y = 10 gives 100.

Method: Square

xy = x(20 − x) = 100 − (x − 10)² ≤ 100.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5

Problem p2. What is the minimum value of x² − 8x + 20?

HintComplete the square.
Answer4
Method: Square

(x − 4)² + 4 ≥ 4.

Method: Vertex

The vertex is at x = 4, where the value is 16 − 32 + 20 = 4.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5

Problem p3. Positive integers sum to 12. What is the largest possible product?

HintUse as many 3s as possible.
Answer81
Method: Threes

3 · 3 · 3 · 3 = 81.

Method: Smoothing

Parts of 1 can be merged into a neighbour, a 4 is no worse as 2 · 2, a 5 splits to 2 · 3 = 6 > 5, so only 2s and 3s remain, and 3s beat 2s.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5

HintMake it as square as possible.
Answer81
Method: Square

The semi-perimeter is 18, so a = b = 9 gives 81.

Method: Table

Sides 8 and 10 give 80, 7 and 11 give 77, so 81 is largest.

Problem p5. A farmer has 100 m of fence and uses a straight river as one side. The fenced rectangle has two sides of length x perpendicular to the river. What is the largest possible area?

HintArea = x(100 − 2x).
Answer1250
Method: Vertex

x(100 − 2x) is a downward parabola with vertex at x = 25, giving 25 · 50 = 1250.

Method: Equal parts

Write 2x · (100 − 2x)/2 and maximise the product of two numbers summing to 100: 50 · 50/2 = 1250.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5

Problem p6. What is the minimum value of |x − 3| + |x − 7| over real x?

HintDistance to two points.
Answer4
Method: Distance

The sum of the distances from x to 3 and to 7 is at least 7 − 3 = 4, achieved for x between them.

Method: Pieces

For 3 ≤ x ≤ 7 the sum is (x − 3) + (7 − x) = 4, and outside it is larger.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5

Problem p7. What is the largest number of integers from 1, 2, …, 10 that can be chosen with no two consecutive?

Answer5
Method: Alternate

1, 3, 5, 7, 9 gives 5.

The pairs (1,2), (3,4), …, (9,10) allow at most one number each, so at most 5.

Problem x19. The roots of x² − 10x + 21 = 0 are r and s. Find r² + s².

HintUse r + s and rs.
Answer58
Method: Vieta

r + s = 10 and rs = 21, so r² + s² = (r + s)² − 2rs = 100 − 42 = 58.

Method: Recurrence

Let pₙ = rⁿ + sⁿ. Then p₀ = 2, p₁ = 10 and p₂ = 10 · p₁ − 21 · p₀ = 100 − 42 = 58.

Source: Zeitz, The Art and Craft of Problem Solving, ch. 4 (extremes) and Engel ch. 5
