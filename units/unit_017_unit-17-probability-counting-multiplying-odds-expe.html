Reading

1. Probability as counting

If an experiment has a finite number of equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event is (number of outcomes in the event) / (total number of outcomes). It is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Most competition problems are counting problems in disguise: the denominators and numerators come from Units 15 and 16.

Two dice. There are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered outcomes (distinguish the dice, e.g. a red and a blue one). The sum is 7 in 6 of them: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1), so P(sum 7) = 6/36 = 1/6. The sum is at least 10 in 6 outcomes (10 in 3, 11 in 2, 12 in 1), probability 1/6 as well. The sum 8 has 5 outcomes, probability 5/36. A listing by computer confirms all these counts.

A common trap: are the outcomes equally likely? The sums 2, 3, …, 12 are not equally likely, so the probability of 7 is not 1/11; it is the 36 ordered pairs that are equally likely. Likewise for two coins the outcomes HH, HT, TH, TT are equally likely, so P(one head and one tail) = 2/4 = 1/2, not 1/3.

Cards. The probability that two cards dealt from a standard deck are both aces: C(4,2)/C(52,2) = 6/1326 = 1/221, or sequentially (4/52)(3/51) = 12/2652 = 1/221. The probability that a 5-card hand is all hearts is C(13,5)/C(52,5) = 1287/2 598 960 = 33/66 640, about 0.05 %.

Try it

Two fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that the larger number shown is 6?

Answer. 11 of the 36 outcomes contain a 6 (6 on the first die: 6; second die: 6; overlap 1), so 11/36.

2. Independent events and multiplying probabilities

Two events are independent if knowing that one happens does not change the chance of the other. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). A fair coin has probability 1/2 of heads on each toss regardless of the past. The chance of exactly 2 heads in 5 tosses: there are 2⁵ = 32 equally likely sequences, C(5,2) = 10 have two heads, so the probability is 10/32 = 5/16. Another way: any given sequence of two H and three T has probability (1/2)⁵ = 1/32, and there are 10 such sequences.

With replacement versus without. A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. Draw two balls with replacement (put the first back): P(both red) = (5/8)² = 25/64. Without replacement the events are not independent: P(both red) = (5/8)(4/7) = 20/56 = 5/14, which equals C(5,2)/C(8,2) = 10/28 = 5/14 ✓. The second factor 4/7 is the conditional probability of red given the first was red.

Conditional probability. P(B given A) = P(A and B)/P(A). Two children: of the four equally likely families BB, BG, GB, GG, the condition 'at least one is a boy' leaves BB, BG, GB, so P(both boys given at least one boy) = 1/3 — not 1/2. Be careful always to say what you are given.

The 'at least one' trick. P(at least one) = 1 − P(none). The chance of at least one six in 3 rolls of a die is 1 − (5/6)³ = 1 − 125/216 = 91/216, about 42 %. This is the probability version of complementary counting.

Try it

A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two are drawn without replacement. What is the probability that they have different colours?

Answer. (5/8)(3/7) + (3/8)(5/7) = 15/56 + 15/56 = 30/56 = 15/28.

3. Odds and addition of probabilities

For mutually exclusive events (they cannot both happen), P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). When they can overlap you must subtract the overlap: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). Example: a card is drawn. P(heart or king) = 13/52 + 4/52 − 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13, the king of hearts being counted twice if we do not subtract. On two dice P(sum 7 or doubles) = 6/36 + 6/36 = 12/36 = 1/3, since a double can never have sum 7 (the overlap is zero).

Odds. If an event has probability p, the odds in favour are p : (1 − p). Probability 3/5 has odds 3 : 2 in favour; conversely, odds of 3 : 2 mean the probability is 3/(3 + 2) = 3/5. A common error is to read 'odds of 3 to 2' as probability 3/2 or 2/3.

Complement. P(not A) = 1 − P(A), so odds against are the reverse of odds in favour. When a question has several ways to succeed and one way to fail, the complement is almost always faster. Worked example (distinct birthdays in a small world). If there were only 7 days in a year, the probability that 4 people all have different birthdays is (7 × 6 × 5 × 4)/7⁴ = 840/2401 = 120/343, about 35 %; the probability that two share is the remaining 223/343.

Try it

The probability of rain is 0.3. Express this as odds in favour and as odds against.

Answer. In favour: 0.3 : 0.7 = 3 : 7. Against: 7 : 3.

4. Expected value

The expected value of a random quantity is the long-run average: multiply each possible value by its probability and add. For one fair die, E = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5 (you never roll 3.5; it is an average). For the sum of two dice, E = 7 (computed by adding all 36 sums and dividing by 36).

Fair games. You win 10 points if a die shows 6, and lose 2 points otherwise. E = (1/6)(10) + (5/6)(−2) = 10/6 − 10/6 = 0, so the game is exactly fair. If the prize for a 6 were 12, the expected gain per play would be 12/6 − 10/6 = 1/3.

Linearity of expectation. The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values, even when the quantities are dependent. So the expected total of two dice is 3.5 + 3.5 = 7 without listing 36 cases. This simple fact solves many hard-looking problems: in a random arrangement of n letters in n envelopes the expected number of letters in the correct envelope is n × (1/n) = 1.

Maximum of two dice. E(max) = (1 × 1 + 2 × 3 + 3 × 5 + 4 × 7 + 5 × 9 + 6 × 11)/36 = 161/36 ≈ 4.47. Here the number of ordered outcomes with maximum m is 2m − 1.

Try it

A coin is tossed until the first head appears. What is the probability that the first head is on the third toss, and how many tosses are expected?

Answer. P = (1/2)(1/2)(1/2) = 1/8. The expected number of tosses is 2 (a fair coin needs on average two tosses to show its first head).

5. Tree diagrams and conditional reasoning

A tree diagram lists the stages of a random process: each branch carries the probability of that step given the path so far, and the probability of a complete path is the product along it. Different paths ending in the same event are then added. Worked example. Urn A holds 3 red and 1 blue ball, urn B holds 1 red and 3 blue. An urn is chosen by a fair coin and a ball is drawn. The probability of red is (1/2)(3/4) + (1/2)(1/4) = 3/8 + 1/8 = 1/2.

Reversing the condition. Suppose you are told the ball was red. What is the chance it came from urn A? Use P(A given red) = P(A and red)/P(red) = (3/8)/(1/2) = 3/4. Notice the answer differs from the 1/2 chance of picking urn A at the start: the evidence has shifted our belief. This reversal of a conditional probability is the heart of Bayes' rule, and nothing more is needed than the definition above.

Conditioning on a dice sum. Two dice are rolled and the sum is 8. The 5 equally likely outcomes are (2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2); exactly one is a double, so the chance of a double given sum 8 is 1/5, even though the unconditional chance of a double is 1/6.

Try it

Using the two urns above, what is the probability that the ball is blue and from urn B?

Answer. (1/2)(3/4) = 3/8.

6. Repeated trials and 'first to win' games

If a trial succeeds with probability p and is repeated n times independently, the chance of exactly k successes is C(n, k) pᵏ (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ: choose which trials succeed, then multiply. For a coin biased with p = 1/3 for heads, the chance of exactly 3 heads in 6 tosses is 20 × (1/27) × (8/27) = 160/729. For a fair coin, at least 2 heads in 4 tosses is (6 + 4 + 1)/16 = 11/16.

Best-of-three. A player wins each game with probability 2/3. She wins a best-of-three series by winning the first two games, or by winning exactly one of the first two and then the third: p² + 2p²(1 − p) = 4/9 + 2 × (4/9) × (1/3) = 4/9 + 8/27 = 20/27. Notice that the series winner has a better chance (20/27 ≈ 0.74) than in a single game (2/3).

Alternating turns. Two players roll one die in turn, and the first to roll a 6 wins. Player 1 wins on the first roll with probability 1/6; if both fail (probability 25/36) the game restarts. So P(1 wins) = (1/6)(1 + 25/36 + (25/36)² + …) = (1/6)/(1 − 25/36) = (1/6)/(11/36) = 6/11. The infinite geometric series of Unit 14 appears again; the first player has a small advantage.

Try it

A fair coin is tossed 4 times. What is the probability of exactly 2 heads?

Answer. C(4,2)/16 = 6/16 = 3/8.

7. Geometric probability

When the outcomes form a continuous range, probabilities become ratios of lengths or areas. Choose two numbers x and y independently and at random from [0, 1]. The sample space is the unit square of area 1; the event 'x + y ≤ 1' is the lower-left triangle of area 1/2, so its probability is 1/2. The event that the numbers differ by less than 1/4 is the complement of two corner triangles each with legs 3/4, of total area (3/4)² = 9/16, so the probability is 1 − 9/16 = 7/16.

Checks. A geometric probability is always an area ratio between 0 and 1. Draw the square and shade the event; if your shading covers most of the square, the answer should be near 1.

8. A longer problem

Problem. Three fair dice are rolled. (a) How many outcomes have sum 10? (b) What is the probability of a sum of 10? (c) What is the probability that all three show different numbers? (d) What is the probability that at least one die shows a 6?

Solution. There are 6³ = 216 equally likely ordered outcomes. (a) Counting the ordered triples with sum 10 gives 27 (the unordered patterns 1+3+6, 1+4+5, 2+2+6, 2+3+5, 2+4+4, 3+3+4 contribute 6 + 6 + 3 + 6 + 3 + 3 = 27 ✓). (b) 27/216 = 1/8. (c) 6 × 5 × 4 = 120, so 120/216 = 5/9. (d) 1 − (5/6)³ = 91/216.

Reflection. Parts (b)–(d) show three styles of probability computation: direct enumeration, multiplication of successive choices, and complement. If a sum of probabilities exceeds 1, an error has been made.

9. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Treating non-equally-likely outcomes as equally likely. (2) Multiplying probabilities for events that are not independent. (3) Adding probabilities for events that overlap. (4) Mixing ordered and unordered counts in numerator and denominator: use ordered in both or unordered in both. (5) Reading odds as probability. (6) Forgetting to simplify the final fraction.

Checks. A probability must lie between 0 and 1; the probabilities of all possible outcomes must add to 1; if the problem is symmetric the answer should be too. Compute the answer by two methods, e.g. counting and step-by-step multiplication.

How to practise. For each probability problem write the sample space size first, then count favourable outcomes. In Unlimited practice, keep the probability family and the expected-value family in rotation.

Problem q1. A box has two yellow balls and one red ball. One ball is drawn at random. What is the probability it is yellow?

HintFavourable / possible.
Answer2/3
Method: Count

2 yellow out of 3 balls: 2/3.

Method: Opposite

P(red) = 1/3, so P(yellow) = 1 − 1/3 = 2/3.

HintC(3, 2) = 3 ways to choose two balls.

3 pairs of balls in all: Y1Y2, Y1R, Y2R. Two of them contain the red: 2/3.

The only other outcome is two yellows, which is the same as leaving the red behind: probability 1/3. So 1 − 1/3 = 2/3.

Problem q7. A 4-digit number (not starting with 0) is chosen at random. What is the probability that its four digits are all different? Give a reduced fraction.

Hint9000 four-digit numbers.
Answer63/125
Method: Count

Favourable: 9 · 9 · 8 · 7 = 4536 (first digit not 0, second any except the first, …). Total 9 · 10 · 10 · 10 = 9000. 4536/9000 = 63/125.

Method: Multiply probabilities

The first digit is any of the 9 allowed (already 'not zero'), so it is not a risk. The second digit differs from it with probability 9/10, the third differs from both with probability 8/10, the fourth with 7/10. Multiply: (9/10)(8/10)(7/10) = 504/1000 = 63/125.

Problem q12. A spike puts the ball away 60% of the time. What is the probability her first spike is returned and her second is not? Give a reduced fraction.

Answer6/25
