Reading

1. Euler's formula

For any convex polyhedron (or any connected planar graph), V − E + F = 2, where V, E, F count vertices, edges and faces. Examples: tetrahedron 4 − 6 + 4; cube 8 − 12 + 6; octahedron 6 − 12 + 8; dodecahedron 20 − 30 + 12; icosahedron 12 − 30 + 20; all equal 2. A soccer-ball polyhedron (truncated icosahedron) has 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons: F = 32, E = (12 × 5 + 20 × 6)/2 = 90, V = 2 + 90 − 32 = 60 ✓.

Why it works (sketch). Start with a tree (V − E = 1, one face, giving 2). Adding an edge that closes a cycle adds one edge and one face, so V − E + F is unchanged.

Worked example. A convex polyhedron has 10 faces, all triangles or quadrilaterals, with 4 triangles. Then E = (4 × 3 + 6 × 4)/2 = 18 and V = 2 + 18 − 10 = 10.

2. The Platonic solids

A polyhedron whose faces are all the same regular polygon, with the same number of faces at each vertex, is a Platonic solid. There are exactly five. Suppose each face is a regular p-gon and q faces meet at each vertex. At a vertex the face angles must add to less than 360°: q(1 − 2/p)·180° < 360°, i.e. (p − 2)(q − 2) < 4. The solutions are (p, q) = (3, 3), (3, 4), (3, 5), (4, 3), (5, 3): tetrahedron, octahedron, icosahedron, cube, dodecahedron.

Counting. With p-gon faces and q faces at each vertex: pF = 2E and qV = 2E, so V − E + F = 2E/q − E + 2E/p = 2 gives E = 2/(2/q + 2/p − 1). For the cube p = 4, q = 3: 2/3 + 1/2 − 1 = 1/6, so E = 12 ✓.

Duality. Joining the centres of the faces of a cube gives an octahedron, and vice versa; the dodecahedron and icosahedron are a dual pair; the tetrahedron is self-dual.

3. Angle defect and counting

Descartes' angle defect. At a vertex, the defect is 360° minus the sum of the face angles there. The sum of the defects over all vertices is always 720° for a convex polyhedron. For the cube each vertex has defect 360 − 3 × 90 = 90°, and 8 × 90 = 720 ✓. For a regular tetrahedron: 4 × (360 − 180) = 720 ✓.

Worked example. A polyhedron has only vertices where 3 faces meet. Then 3V = 2E, and Euler's formula gives F = 2 + E/3. If all faces are pentagons or hexagons then there must be exactly 12 pentagons. (Each vertex has defect from face angles; the sum 720° forces 12 pentagons, as in the soccer ball.)

Colouring faces. The faces of a cube cannot be 2-coloured so that adjacent faces differ, because three mutually adjacent faces meet at every corner; three colours are needed.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Counting each edge twice (or once) incorrectly. Applying V − E + F = 2 to a polyhedron with a hole (a torus gives 0). Forgetting that the outer region counts as a face for planar graphs.

Checks. Verify Euler's formula for the cube before using it.

Practice. Use pF = 2E and qV = 2E systematically.

Worked examples with Try-its: Euler's formula

For a convex polyhedron V − E + F = 2. The five Platonic solids (V, E, F): tetrahedron (4, 6, 4), cube (8, 12, 6), octahedron (6, 12, 8), dodecahedron (20, 30, 12), icosahedron (12, 30, 20); all satisfy it. A soccer ball (truncated icosahedron) has 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons, F = 32, E = (12 · 5 + 20 · 6)/2 = 90 and V = 60, so 60 − 90 + 32 = 2.

Try it

A polyhedron has 8 faces and 12 vertices. How many edges?

Answer. 12 − E + 8 = 2, so E = 18.

Worked examples with Try-its: Why only five

At each vertex of a regular polyhedron at least 3 faces meet and the face angles must add up to less than 360°. Triangles (60°) allow 3, 4 or 5 per vertex, squares (90°) allow 3, pentagons (108°) allow 3, and hexagons would give exactly 360° (flat). That gives exactly five solids. The angle defect at a vertex is 360° minus the sum of face angles; the total over all vertices is always 720°: for the cube 8 · 90° = 720°.

Try it

What is the angle defect at a vertex of a regular tetrahedron? How many vertices are there and what is the total?

Answer. 360° − 3 · 60° = 180°; 4 · 180° = 720°.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. A convex polyhedron has 12 pentagonal faces and some hexagonal faces, with 3 faces at every vertex. If it has 32 faces in total how many hexagons are there and how many vertices? Solution. 20 hexagons. Edges: (12 · 5 + 20 · 6)/2 = 90 and vertices 3V = 2E gives V = 60. Check: 60 − 90 + 32 = 2.

Reflection. Count edge–face incidences to get E.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Truncated tetrahedron. V = 12, E = 18, F = 8 (4 triangles and 4 hexagons): 12 − 18 + 8 = 2. Cuboctahedron. V = 12, E = 24, F = 14: 12 − 24 + 14 = 2. A polyhedron with 10 triangular faces has E = 10 · 3/2 = 15 edges and, by Euler's formula, V = 2 + E − F = 7.

Try it

A polyhedron has 6 vertices and 12 edges. How many faces?

Answer. 2 − 6 + 12 = 8 faces (an octahedron has this count).

Try it

How many edges does a polyhedron with 5 quadrilateral faces have?

Answer. 5 · 4/2 = 10.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Show that no polyhedron has exactly 7 edges. Solution. Each face has at least 3 edges and each edge borders two faces, so 2E ≥ 3F, and each vertex has degree at least 3, so 2E ≥ 3V. With E = 7: F ≤ 4 and V ≤ 4, but V − E + F = 2 requires V + F = 9, impossible.

Reflection. The inequalities 2E ≥ 3F and 2E ≥ 3V together with V − E + F = 2 limit E to 6 or at least 8.

Problem p9. How many Hamiltonian cycles does the graph of the cube (vertices are the 8 corners, edges are the 12 cube edges) have, counted as undirected cycles?

HintA search is the safest method.
Answer6
Method: Search

Enumerating the cyclic orders of the corners that use only cube edges and halving for direction gives 6.

Method: Structure

The cube graph is bipartite with 4 + 4 vertices, and such cycles alternate between the two sets.
