Reading

1. Matchings and Hall's theorem

A matching in a graph is a set of edges with no shared vertex. In a bipartite graph with boys on one side and girls on the other, a matching pairs some boys with girls they like. Hall's theorem: every boy can be matched to a girl he likes iff every group of k boys likes at least k girls between them.

Worked example 1. Boys a, b, c like girls: a → {1, 2}, b → {2, 3}, c → {1, 3}. Matching: a–1, b–2, c–3. Each group satisfies Hall's condition.

Worked example 2. Boys a, b, c with a → {1, 2}, b → {1, 2}, c → {1, 2}: three boys, only two girls in total. Hall fails, so no matching exists.

Kőnig's theorem. In a bipartite graph the largest matching has the same size as the smallest set of vertices touching every edge (a vertex cover).

2. Euler trails and Hamilton cycles

A graph has an Euler circuit (a closed walk using each edge once) iff it is connected and every degree is even. It has an Euler trail iff exactly 0 or 2 vertices have odd degree. A Hamilton cycle visits every vertex once; there is no simple criterion, but Dirac's theorem says: if n ≥ 3 and every vertex has degree at least n/2, then a Hamilton cycle exists.

Worked example 1. Is there a walk through a 3 × 3 grid of rooms (doors between neighbouring rooms) using every door exactly once? The corners have degree 2, edge-midpoints degree 3, the centre degree 4: four vertices of odd degree, so no.

Worked example 2. K_n has an Euler circuit iff n is odd (all degrees n − 1 are even). K₅ has one.

3. Planarity and colouring

For a connected planar graph, V − E + F = 2. A simple planar graph with V ≥ 3 has E ≤ 3V − 6; if it has no triangle, E ≤ 2V − 4. So K₅ (10 edges, 5 vertices, 3V − 6 = 9) is not planar, and K₃,₃ (9 edges, 6 vertices, 2V − 4 = 8) is not planar. By Kuratowski's theorem, every non-planar graph contains a subdivision of K₅ or K₃,₃.

The four-colour theorem. Every planar graph can be properly coloured with four colours. The easier six-colour theorem follows from the fact that every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5 (sum of degrees ≤ 6V − 12), plus the greedy argument.

Worked example. A planar graph has 12 vertices, every vertex of degree 4. Then E = 24 ≤ 3 × 12 − 6 = 30, consistent; and F = 2 − V + E = 14.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Applying Hall's theorem in the wrong direction. Forgetting that the Euler condition also needs connectivity. Applying E ≤ 3V − 6 to non-planar or non-simple graphs.

Checks. Test the Hall condition on every subset of size 1 and 2 for small examples.

Practice. Draw the bipartite graph for matching problems.

Worked examples with Try-its: Euler trails

A connected graph has an Euler circuit (a closed walk using every edge once) exactly when every vertex has even degree, and an Euler trail (open) exactly when it has exactly two odd vertices. The Königsberg bridges graph has four odd vertices (degrees 3, 3, 3, 5), so no walk crosses every bridge once. K₅ has all degrees 4, so it has an Euler circuit; K₄ has all degrees 3, so it has neither.

Try it

A connected graph has degrees 2, 2, 3, 3, 4. Does it have an Euler trail? A circuit?

Answer. Exactly two odd vertices: a trail yes, circuit no.

Worked examples with Try-its: Matchings and planarity

A matching pairs up vertices along edges. Hall's theorem: a bipartite graph with parts X and Y has a matching covering X if and only if every subset S of X has at least |S| neighbours. The complete bipartite K₃,₃ has 3! = 6 perfect matchings. Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 holds for K₄ drawn in the plane: 4 − 6 + 4 = 2.

Try it

How many perfect matchings does K₄,₄ have?

Answer. 4! = 24.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Can a 5 × 5 chessboard with two opposite corner squares removed be tiled by dominoes? Solution. The board has 23 squares, which is odd, so no tiling exists. (On the 8 × 8 board the corners leave 62 squares, but the two removed corners have the same colour, so there are 30 squares of one colour and 32 of the other, and again no tiling exists.)

Reflection. Counting colours is a matching argument in disguise.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Matchings. The 6-cycle has exactly 2 perfect matchings (alternate edges) and K₄ has 3. Planarity bound. A simple planar graph on 6 vertices has at most 3 · 6 − 6 = 12 edges. Euler circuits. A connected graph on 6 vertices with degrees 2, 2, 2, 2, 4, 4 has an Euler circuit since all degrees are even.

Try it

Does K₆ have an Euler circuit?

Answer. Each vertex has degree 5 (odd), so no.

Try it

How many perfect matchings does a path on 4 vertices have?

Answer. 1.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. A bipartite graph has parts X = {a, b, c} and Y = {1, 2, 3} with edges a1, a2, b2, c2, c3. Is there a perfect matching? Solution. Yes: a–1, b–2, c–3. Hall's condition holds: any 2 of the vertices in X have at least 2 neighbours, and all three have 3.

Check. Removing the edge a1 would leave a and b both adjacent only to 2, so no perfect matching: Hall fails for {a, b}.

Problem p1. K₆ has every vertex of degree 5. What is the fewest edges that must be deleted so that the remaining graph has an Euler circuit?

HintEvery vertex must have even degree.
Answer3
Method: Degrees

Every vertex needs to lose an odd number of edges, so each of the 6 vertices loses at least 1 edge. Each deleted edge touches 2 vertices, so at least 3 edges are deleted, and a perfect matching of 3 edges works.

Method: Check

After deleting a perfect matching every degree is 4, which is even.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 5

Problem p2. How many perfect matchings does the complete bipartite graph K₄,₄ have?

HintMatch each left vertex to a distinct right vertex.
Answer24
Method: Permutations

A perfect matching is a bijection between the two sides: 4! = 24.

Method: Step by step

The first left vertex has 4 choices, the next 3, then 2 and 1: 24.

Problem p3. A planar bipartite graph has 10 vertices. What is the greatest number of edges it can have?

HintE ≤ 2V − 4 when there are no triangles.
Answer16
Method: Bound

2 · 10 − 4 = 16.

Method: Euler

Every face has at least 4 edges, so 4F ≤ 2E; with V − E + F = 2 this gives E ≤ 2V − 4.

Problem p4. What is the chromatic number of the wheel graph made of a hub joined to every vertex of a 5-cycle?

Answer4
Method: Reasoning

The 5-cycle is odd and needs 3 colours, and the hub is adjacent to all of them, so it needs a fourth: 4.

A search over colourings finds none with 3 colours and one with 4.

Problem p10. How many vertices of the complete graph K₆ have odd degree?

HintEach vertex has degree 5.
Answer6
Method: Degrees

Every vertex is joined to the other 5, so all 6 have odd degree.

Method: Euler circuit

Since odd vertices exist, K₆ has no Euler circuit, while K₅ (all degrees 4) does.

Problem p11. A connected graph has exactly 6 vertices of odd degree. What is the fewest number of trails (each edge used once overall) needed to cover all its edges?

HintEach trail has two ends.
Answer3
Method: Ends

Each odd vertex must be an end of a trail, and each trail has 2 ends, so at least 6/2 = 3 trails.

Method: Construction

Pairing odd vertices and adding 3 temporary edges makes the graph Eulerian; removing them splits the circuit into 3 trails.

Problem p12. What is the size of a largest matching in the complete bipartite graph K₃,₅?

HintEach edge uses one vertex from the part of 3.
Answer3
Method: Bound

Each matching edge uses one of the 3 vertices on the small side, so at most 3, and 3 is achieved.

Method: Hall

Every subset of the small side has at least as many neighbours as its size.
