1. Take-away games and losing positions

In a combinatorial game two players alternate moves, there is no chance, and the player who cannot move (or who takes the last object, depending on the rules) loses (or wins). A position is a P-position if the previous player wins (the player to move loses) and an N-position if the next player wins. Rules: every move from a P-position leads to an N-position; from every N-position some move leads to a P-position.

Subtraction game. From a pile you may take 1, 2 or 3 objects; the player who takes the last wins. The P-positions are the multiples of 4 (0, 4, 8, …): from a multiple of 4 any move leaves a non-multiple, and from a non-multiple one can move to a multiple. So with 100 coins and moves 1–5 the first player takes 100 mod 6 = 4 and then answers every move m of the opponent with 6 − m. If the moves are 1, 3 or 4, the P-positions are 0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16, …, found by marking positions from which every move leads to an N-position.

Try it

Take-away game with moves 1, 2 or 3; pile 27. Who wins and how?

Answer. 27 mod 4 = 3, so the first player wins by taking 3 and thereafter completing 4 with each pair of moves.

2. Nim

In Nim there are several piles; a move removes any positive number of objects from one pile; the player taking the last object wins. Bouton's theorem: a position is a P-position iff the bitwise XOR of the pile sizes is 0. For (1, 2, 3): 1 ⊕ 2 ⊕ 3 = 0, so the player to move loses. For (3, 4, 5): 3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 2 ≠ 0, so the first player wins: to restore XOR 0, reduce the pile whose size decreases when XORed with 2, namely 3 → 1 (since 3 ⊕ 2 = 1 < 3), giving (1, 4, 5) with 1 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 0.

Why XOR works. From XOR 0, any move changes exactly one pile, so the XOR becomes non-zero; from XOR ≠ 0 take the highest set bit of the XOR: some pile has that bit set, and reducing it appropriately makes the XOR zero.

Strategy stealing and symmetry. Other games are solved by pairing moves (a symmetric strategy) or by strategy stealing: in 'chomp' on a rectangular board the first player wins, since if the second player had a winning reply to the first player's move of taking just the top-right square, the first player could have made that move himself.

Try it

Is the Nim position (2, 5, 7) a win for the player to move? Give a winning move if so.

Answer. 2 ⊕ 5 ⊕ 7 = 0, so it is a loss for the player to move.

3. A longer problem

Problem. Two players alternately remove 1, 2 or 3 stones from a pile of 30; the player who removes the last stone loses (misère). Who wins?

Solution. Work backwards. With 1 stone the player to move loses (must take the last stone). So 2, 3, 4 stones are wins (leave 1). With 5 stones every move leaves 4, 3 or 2, all wins for the opponent: loss. The losing positions are 1, 5, 9, 13, … ≡ 1 (mod 4). 30 ≡ 2 (mod 4), a winning position: the first player takes 1 leaving 29 ≡ 1 (mod 4).

Reflection. Misère rules shift the losing positions by one. Always re-derive from the base cases.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Mixing normal and misère rules. (2) Forgetting to check that every move from a P-position leads to an N-position. (3) Using XOR for games that are not sums of Nim piles.

Checks. Compute the P-positions of the first dozen positions by hand and look for a pattern.

How to practise. Always work out small cases first. In Unlimited practice, rotate the game and Nim families.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Grundy values. In the game where a player removes 1 or 2 stones from a pile, the Grundy value of a pile of n is n mod 3: 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, … For several piles, XOR the Grundy values: piles of 4 and 5 give 1 ⊕ 2 = 3 ≠ 0, so the first player wins; piles of 3 and 6 give 0 ⊕ 0 = 0, so the second player wins.

Try it

In the take-1-or-2 game with piles 4, 5 and 6, who wins?

Answer. Grundy values 1, 2, 0 give XOR 3, so the first player wins.

Try it

What is the Grundy value of a Nim pile of size 7?

Answer. 7.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Two players alternately remove 1, 2 or 3 stones from a pile of 21; the player taking the last stone wins. Describe the winning strategy. Solution. The losing positions are the multiples of 4. Since 21 = 5 · 4 + 1, the first player removes 1 and thereafter answers each opponent move by x with a move of 4 − x.

Check. After the first move 20 remain, and the totals after each round are 16, 12, 8, 4, 0, so the first player takes the last stone.

Problem p1. 21 stones are in a pile. Players alternately take 1, 2 or 3 stones; the player taking the last stone wins. How many stones should the first player take to win?

HintLeave a multiple of 4.
Answer1
Method: Multiples of 4

Multiples of 4 are losing for the player to move: whatever they take (1–3), you take 4 minus that. 21 − 1 = 20.

Method: Backward table

Positions 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 are losing, and the first move to 20 takes 1.

Problem p4. Players take 1, 3 or 4 stones from a pile; the player taking the last stone wins. Among piles of size 0 to 20, how many are losing for the player to move?

HintBuild a table.
Answer6
Method: Table

Position n is losing if every move leads to a winning position (0 is losing). Working up: 0 L; 1 W (to 0); 2 L (its only move goes to 1, a winning position); 3 W (to 0); 4 W (to 0); 5 W (to 2); 6 W (taking 4 reaches 2); 7 L (moves to 6, 4, 3 are all winning); and the pattern repeats every 7. The losing positions are 0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16: six of them.

Method: Computer check

Marking n losing when none of n − 1, n − 3, n − 4 is losing, for n up to 20, gives exactly 0, 2, 7, 9, 14, 16.

Source: Engel, Problem-Solving Strategies, ch. 13; Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics

Problem x19. A staircase has 9 steps and you climb 1 or 2 steps at a time. How many ways are there to climb it?

HintConsider the last move.
Answer55
Method: Recurrence

Ways(n) = Ways(n − 1) + Ways(n − 2): 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, 55, so 55 ways.

Method: By number of 2-steps

With j two-steps there are C(9 − j, j) orders; summing over j gives 55.

Problem x21. 100 objects are placed into 9 boxes. What is the largest number n for which some box must contain at least n objects?

HintPigeonhole principle.
Answer12
Method: Pigeonhole

⌈100/9⌉ = 12.

Method: Contradiction

If every box had at most 11 objects, there would be at most 9 · 11 = 99 < 100 objects in all.
