Reading

1. Multiplying a sum by a sum

In Unit 1 you saw that (a + b)(m + n) = am + an + bm + bn: every term in the first bracket meets every term in the second. Here we turn that into a reliable procedure and look at why it works from several angles. Picture a rectangle whose width is a + b and whose height is m + n. Cut it along the dividing lines and you get four smaller rectangles with areas am, an, bm and bn. The whole area is the sum of the four, and it is also (a + b)(m + n), so both expressions are the same thing.

The procedure. To expand a product of two brackets: (1) multiply the first term of the first bracket by every term of the second; (2) multiply the second term of the first bracket by every term of the second; (3) add everything and combine like terms. Many students learn the shortcut FOIL (First, Outer, Inner, Last) for two terms in each bracket, but the procedure above works for any number of terms, and FOIL does not. If one bracket has 3 terms and the other has 2, you must get 6 products before combining.

Worked example 1. Expand (x + 3)(x + 5). The products are x · x = x², x · 5 = 5x, 3 · x = 3x and 3 · 5 = 15. Add: x² + 5x + 3x + 15 = x² + 8x + 15. Notice that the middle coefficient 8 is the sum 3 + 5 and the constant 15 is the product 3 × 5. This pattern is the key to factoring later: to undo the multiplication you look for two numbers with a given sum and product.

Worked example 2. Expand (x² + 2x + 3)(x − 4). Multiply the whole first bracket by x: x³ + 2x² + 3x. Multiply the whole first bracket by −4: −4x² − 8x − 12. Add and combine like terms: x³ + (2x² − 4x²) + (3x − 8x) − 12 = x³ − 2x² − 5x − 12. A good check is to substitute x = 1: the original gives (1 + 2 + 3)(1 − 4) = 6 × (−3) = −18, and the answer gives 1 − 2 − 5 − 12 = −18. ✓.

Worked example 3. Expand (x + 1)(x² − x + 1). The products are x³ − x² + x + x² − x + 1. The x² terms cancel and so do the x terms, leaving x³ + 1. This is a special product that you will see again in the section on cubes, and it shows that a long expansion can collapse to something very short.

2. The square of a sum and of a difference

The most useful product in elementary algebra is a bracket multiplied by itself. The square of a sum is (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². Expanding it by the procedure gives a² + ab + ba + b², and since ab = ba the two middle terms join into 2ab. The picture is a big square of side a + b, cut into an a × a square, a b × b square and two a × b rectangles. The two rectangles are the middle term, and students who write a² + b² have forgotten them.

The square of a difference is (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b². The only change is the sign of the middle term, because the cross products are negative. The last term b² is still positive, since (−b)(−b) = +b².

Worked example 1: numbers. These identities make mental arithmetic fast. 101² = (100 + 1)² = 10,000 + 200 + 1 = 10,201. 98² = (100 − 2)² = 10,000 − 400 + 4 = 9,604. 104² = (100 + 4)² = 10,000 + 800 + 16 = 10,816. Split the number into a round part and a small part, and the square falls out in three pieces.

Worked example 2: letters. Expand (2x − 5)². Here a = 2x and b = 5, so a² = 4x², 2ab = 2 × 2x × 5 = 20x and b² = 25. The result is 4x² − 20x + 25. Always square the entire first term, including its coefficient: (2x)² is 4x², not 2x².

Worked example 3: recognising a square. The expression x² + 6x + 9 matches a² + 2ab + b² with a = x and b = 3, since 2 × x × 3 = 6x and 3² = 9. So it equals (x + 3)². To see whether a quadratic is a perfect square, take the square root of the first and last terms and check that the middle is twice their product. For x² + 6x + 8 this check fails (twice the product would have to be 6x, but 8 is not a perfect square), and indeed x² + 6x + 8 = (x + 2)(x + 4).

An application. Since (a + b)² − (a − b)² = 4ab, the square of a sum is always at least the square of a difference when a and b are positive. This is the core of a famous inequality you will meet in Unit 13: for positive numbers, (a + b)²/4 ≥ ab, so a sum of two numbers with a fixed total has the biggest product when they are equal.

3. The difference of squares

The product (a + b)(a − b) is a² − ab + ab − b² = a² − b². The two middle terms cancel exactly, so the answer has just two terms. This is called the difference of squares and it works in both directions. When you see a² − b² you may write (a + b)(a − b), and when you see (a + b)(a − b) you may write a² − b².

Worked example 1: mental arithmetic. To compute 97 × 103, notice that both numbers are 3 away from 100: 97 × 103 = (100 − 3)(100 + 3) = 10,000 − 9 = 9,991. To compute 53 × 47 use 50 ± 3 and get 2,500 − 9 = 2,491. This trick works whenever two numbers have the same distance from a convenient middle number.

Worked example 2: factoring. Factor 49x² − 25. Since 49x² = (7x)² and 25 = 5², this is (7x)² − 5² = (7x + 5)(7x − 5). Factor x⁴ − 16: it is (x²)² − 4² = (x² + 4)(x² − 4), and x² − 4 factors again as (x + 2)(x − 2), so x⁴ − 16 = (x² + 4)(x + 2)(x − 2). Always check whether a factor can be factored further. The factor x² + 4 cannot be split using real numbers.

Worked example 3: rationalising. To remove the square root from the denominator of 1/(√5 − 2), multiply top and bottom by √5 + 2. The bottom becomes (√5)² − 2² = 5 − 4 = 1, so the fraction equals √5 + 2. The difference of squares removes the square root because squaring a square root cancels it.

Worked example 4: a surprising factorisation. Is 2,491 prime? It equals 53 × 47, so it is composite. In general, any odd number that is a difference of two squares can be factored: N = a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b). Finding a and b is a classic factoring method that goes back to Fermat.

4. Cubes and Pascal's triangle

Multiply (a + b) by itself three times. First (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². Multiply by (a + b) again: a³ + 2a²b + ab² + a²b + 2ab² + b³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³. So (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³. For a difference, change the sign of every odd power of b: (a − b)³ = a³ − 3a²b + 3ab² − b³. For example (x + 2)³ = x³ + 6x² + 12x + 8, since 3 × x² × 2 = 6x² and 3 × x × 4 = 12x.

Two factorisations of cubes. The sum and difference of cubes factor as a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²) and a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²). You can verify the first by multiplying out: (a + b)(a² − ab + b²) = a³ − a²b + ab² + a²b − ab² + b³ = a³ + b³. In practice, 27x³ − 8 = (3x)³ − 2³ = (3x − 2)(9x² + 6x + 4).

Pascal's triangle. Look at the coefficients we have found. For (a + b)⁰ it is 1. For (a + b)¹ it is 1, 1. For (a + b)² it is 1, 2, 1, and for (a + b)³ it is 1, 3, 3, 1. These rows form Pascal's triangle: each number is the sum of the two above it, with 1s on the edges. The next rows are 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 and 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. So (x + y)⁴ = x⁴ + 4x³y + 6x²y² + 4xy³ + y⁴.

Why the triangle works. To get (a + b)⁵ from (a + b)⁴, multiply by (a + b). Each term of the old expansion contributes once with a and once with b, so a new term gets a contribution from the term to its left (multiplied by a) and the one to its right (multiplied by b). That is exactly the rule “add the two above”. Rows also have a nice total: the numbers in row n add to 2ⁿ, because setting a = b = 1 gives (1 + 1)ⁿ.

Worked example. Expand (x − 1)⁴. Use the row 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 with a = x and b = −1. The terms are x⁴, 4x³(−1) = −4x³, 6x²(−1)² = 6x², 4x(−1)³ = −4x and (−1)⁴ = 1, so (x − 1)⁴ = x⁴ − 4x³ + 6x² − 4x + 1. The signs alternate whenever you expand a difference, which is a handy check. Putting x = 1 gives 1 − 4 + 6 − 4 + 1 = 0, as (1 − 1)⁴ = 0.

5. Polynomials

A polynomial is a sum of terms, each a number times a power of x with a whole-number exponent: 3x⁴ − 2x² + 7x − 5. The highest exponent that appears (with a non-zero coefficient) is the degree, which is 4 here, and the coefficient of that term is the leading coefficient. A number like 7 is a polynomial of degree 0, and the zero polynomial has no degree. A polynomial of degree 1 is linear, degree 2 quadratic and degree 3 cubic.

Adding, subtracting and multiplying. Add by combining like terms: (3x² + 2x − 1) + (x² − 5x + 4) = 4x² − 3x + 3. Subtract by changing every sign in the second polynomial first. Multiply using the procedure from the first section. Two facts about degrees are very useful: the degree of a product is the sum of the degrees (the highest terms multiply to give the highest term), and the degree of a sum is at most the larger degree. A product of a degree-3 and a degree-2 polynomial has degree 5.

When two polynomials are equal. If two polynomials give the same value for every x, then their coefficients must match term by term. This idea, called comparing coefficients, is how you find unknown numbers in an identity. For example, if (x + a)(x + b) = x² + 7x + 12, then a + b = 7 and ab = 12, so a and b are 3 and 4. Likewise if x² + px + 9 is a perfect square (x + s)², then 2s = p and s² = 9, so p = ±6.

Worked example. Find the coefficient of x² in (x + 2)(x² + 3x + 4)(x − 1). First multiply the first and last brackets: (x + 2)(x − 1) = x² + x − 2. Then multiply by the middle bracket, collecting only x² terms: x² × 4 gives 4x², x × 3x gives 3x², and (−2) × x² gives −2x². The sum 4 + 3 − 2 = 5, so the coefficient of x² is 5. You never need to expand everything if you only want one coefficient, and this is a skill that saves a great deal of time.

Evaluating at a point. The value of a polynomial at x = 1 is the sum of its coefficients, and the value at x = 0 is its constant term. These shortcuts appear again in the next unit.

6. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Forgetting the middle term 2ab in a square. Squaring only part of a term: (3x)² is 9x², not 3x². Getting the sign of the middle term wrong in (a − b)². Dropping a term when multiplying brackets with more than two terms. Assuming that a² + b² factors like a² − b² (it does not, over the real numbers).

How to check an expansion. Substitute a simple number such as x = 1 or x = 2 into the original and the result. They must agree. Also check the degree and the leading coefficient: the highest term of the answer is the product of the highest terms of the factors. And count terms: before combining, the number of products is the product of the numbers of terms.

How to use the practice set. Try each problem on your own and use the methods to compare. In this unit one problem is often solvable by expansion, by a special product, and by plugging in numbers, and the fastest method depends on the numbers. Problem 62. Each of the two factors of a product becomes 10 percent bigger. By what percent does the product increase?

HintA number 10% bigger is 1.1 times as big.
Answer21
Method: Algebra

The new product is (1.1a)(1.1b) = 1.21ab, which is 21% more than ab.

Method: Square of a sum

1.1 = 1 + 0.1, so 1.1² = 1 + 2(0.1) + 0.1² = 1 + 0.2 + 0.01 = 1.21. The extra 0.01 is the b² term: the two 10% changes also multiply each other.

Method: Try a number

10 × 10 = 100. Make each factor 10% bigger: 11 × 11 = 121. That is 21 more than 100, i.e. 21%.

Method: Area picture

A rectangle a by b grows to 1.1a by 1.1b. The extra area is a strip along each side (0.1 of ab each) and a small corner (0.01 of ab): 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.01 = 0.21.

Problem 70. 13 · 15 + 1 is a perfect square. What integer is it the square of?

HintThe two factors differ by 2. What number is halfway between them?
Answer14
Method: Compute

13 · 15 = 195, and 195 + 1 = 196 = 14². (Since 14² = 196, the integer is 14.)

Method: The middle number n

Let n = 14, the number halfway between 13 and 15. Then 13 · 15 = (n − 1)(n + 1) = n² − 1, so adding 1 gives n² = 14². This works for any two integers differing by 2.

Problem 83. What is the coefficient of a⁴b³ in the expansion of (a + b)⁷?

HintBuild Pascal's triangle down to the row for n = 7.
Answer35
Method: Pascal's triangle

Rows: 1; 1 1; 1 2 1; 1 3 3 1; 1 4 6 4 1; 1 5 10 10 5 1; 1 6 15 20 15 6 1; 1 7 21 35 35 21 7 1. The terms in order are a⁷, a⁶b, a⁵b², a⁴b³, …, so a⁴b³ has the fourth entry: 35.

(a + b)⁷ is seven brackets multiplied. A term a⁴b³ arises when you pick b from exactly 3 of the 7 brackets and a from the others. The number of ways to choose 3 brackets out of 7 is (7·6·5)/(3·2·1) = 35.

Problem 90b. (1 + x)(1 + x²)(1 + x⁴)(1 + x⁸) equals 1 + x + x² + … + xⁿ. What is n?

HintMultiply two brackets at a time.
Answer15
Method: Multiply step by step

(1 + x)(1 + x²) = 1 + x + x² + x³. Multiply by (1 + x⁴): 1 + x + … + x³ + x⁴ + … + x⁷ = 1 + x + … + x⁷. Multiply by (1 + x⁸): the sum now goes up to x¹⁵.

Method: Highest power

The highest power comes from picking the biggest term in each bracket: x · x² · x⁴ · x⁸ = x¹⁵. Since the problem says the answer has every power up to xⁿ, n = 15.

Method: Binary numbers

Choosing x¹, x², x⁴, x⁸ or 1 from each bracket is exactly writing a number from 0 to 15 in binary (Unit 1). Each number appears once, so every power from 0 to 15 appears exactly once.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 90b

Method: Factor

x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). Cancel (x − 1) from both sides: (x + 1)(x + a) = (x + 3)(x + b). Matching, a = 3 and b = 1.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 92

Problem 93. George checks whether (x + 1)² − (x − 1)² and x² + 4x − 1 are equal by substituting x = 1 and x = −1. Is that enough to be sure they are equal? Answer yes or no.

HintCompute both sides at x = 2 as well.
Answerno
Method: Try another value

At x = 1: (4) − 0 = 4 and 1 + 4 − 1 = 4. At x = −1: 0 − 4 = −4 and 1 − 4 − 1 = −4. They agree! But at x = 2: 9 − 1 = 8 and 4 + 8 − 1 = 11. They disagree, so the polynomials are different.

Method: Simplify with algebra

(x + 1)² − (x − 1)² = (x² + 2x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 1) = 4x. This is not x² + 4x − 1. Matching values at two points did not prove equality.

Source: Gelfand & Shen, Algebra, Problem 93
