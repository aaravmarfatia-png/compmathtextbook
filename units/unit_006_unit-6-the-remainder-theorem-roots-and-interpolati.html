1. What a polynomial is, and how to compute with one

A polynomial in x is a sum of terms, each a number times a whole-number power of x: 4x³ − 7x + 2 is one, and so is x⁵ or the plain number 9. The numbers in front are the coefficients. The largest power that actually appears (with a non-zero coefficient) is the degree; 4x³ − 7x + 2 has degree 3, and its leading coefficient is 4. A missing power just means a coefficient of 0: here the x² coefficient is 0. Things that are not polynomials: 1/x, √x, 2ˣ, because x appears in a denominator, under a root or in an exponent.

Two ideas make everything in this unit easier. First, a polynomial is a machine: you feed in a number and it gives back a number. We write p(x) = 4x³ − 7x + 2 and then p(2) = 4·8 − 14 + 2 = 20 means 'feed in 2, receive 20'. Second, you can add and multiply polynomials just like numbers, where the powers of x are like place values you must keep in separate columns.

Worked example (evaluating fast). To compute p(x) = 2x³ − 3x² + x + 5 at x = 2 you could square and cube, but there is a neater way called Horner's method: write p(x) = ((2x − 3)x + 1)x + 5. Then at x = 2: 2 × 2 − 3 = 1; 1 × 2 + 1 = 3; 3 × 2 + 5 = 11. So p(2) = 11 (check the long way: 16 − 12 + 2 + 5 = 11 ✓). Horner's method uses only multiplications by x and additions, and it is exactly the arithmetic that appears in synthetic division below.

Worked example (multiplying). (x + 2)(x² − 3x + 4) means every term of the first meets every term of the second: x·x² = x³, x·(−3x) = −3x², x·4 = 4x, 2·x² = 2x², 2·(−3x) = −6x, 2·4 = 8. Collect like powers: x³ + (−3 + 2)x² + (4 − 6)x + 8 = x³ − x² − 2x + 8. The degree of a product is the sum of the degrees (1 + 2 = 3), and the leading coefficient is the product of the leading coefficients.

Try it

Evaluate p(x) = x³ − 2x² + 3x − 4 at x = −2 using Horner's method, then check it by direct substitution.

Answer. Horner: (((1)(−2) − 2)(−2) + 3)(−2) − 4. Step by step: 1·(−2) − 2 = −4; −4·(−2) + 3 = 11; 11·(−2) − 4 = −26. Direct: −8 − 8 − 6 − 4 = −26 ✓.

2. Dividing polynomials

Division of whole numbers gives a quotient and a remainder: 47 = 5 × 9 + 2. Polynomials behave the same way. For polynomials p(x) and d(x) there are unique polynomials q(x) (the quotient) and r(x) (the remainder) with p(x) = d(x)·q(x) + r(x), where the remainder has smaller degree than the divisor. If you divide by something of degree 1 like x − a, the remainder has degree 0, so it is just a number.

Long division, step by step. Divide x³ + 2x² − 5x − 6 by x − 2. Look only at the leading terms: x³ ÷ x = x². Multiply back: x²(x − 2) = x³ − 2x². Subtract: (x³ + 2x²) − (x³ − 2x²) = 4x², and bring down −5x to get 4x² − 5x. Next, 4x² ÷ x = 4x; multiply back: 4x(x − 2) = 4x² − 8x; subtract: 3x, bring down −6 to get 3x − 6. Next, 3x ÷ x = 3; multiply back 3(x − 2) = 3x − 6; subtract: 0. The quotient is x² + 4x + 3 and the remainder is 0. So x³ + 2x² − 5x − 6 = (x − 2)(x² + 4x + 3).

Synthetic division is the same calculation with the unnecessary symbols removed. To divide by x − a, write the coefficients, drop the first one, then repeatedly multiply by a and add. For x⁴ + 3x³ + 0x² − 2x + 5 divided by x + 1 (so a = −1): bring down 1; 1·(−1) + 3 = 2; 2·(−1) + 0 = −2; (−2)(−1) + (−2) = 0; 0·(−1) + 5 = 5. The bottom row 1, 2, −2, 0 is the quotient x³ + 2x² − 2x + 0 and the last number 5 is the remainder. Notice that the placeholder 0 for the missing x² term was essential: skipping it is the single most common error.

Why long division works. Each step removes the highest power from what is left, exactly as in the long division of numbers you learned in school. The process must stop, because the degree of what remains drops by at least one each time, and it stops when the leftover has smaller degree than the divisor.

Try it

Use synthetic division to divide x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 by x − 1.

Answer. Bring down 1; 1·1 − 6 = −5; (−5)·1 + 11 = 6; 6·1 − 6 = 0. Quotient x² − 5x + 6, remainder 0. So x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 1)(x² − 5x + 6) = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3).

3. The remainder theorem

The synthetic division above hides a beautiful shortcut. When p(x) is divided by x − a we have p(x) = (x − a)q(x) + r where r is a number. Now put x = a: the term (x − a)q(x) becomes zero, so p(a) = r. The remainder theorem: the remainder when p(x) is divided by x − a is p(a). You never need to do the division if you only want the remainder.

Worked example 1. What is the remainder when x⁴ + 3x³ − 2x + 5 is divided by x + 1? Here x + 1 = x − (−1), so a = −1 and the remainder is p(−1) = 1 − 3 + 2 + 5 = 5, matching the synthetic division above.

Worked example 2 (a missing coefficient). For what value of a is x³ − ax² + 2x + 6 divisible by x + 1? Divisible means the remainder is 0, so p(−1) = −1 − a − 2 + 6 = 3 − a = 0, giving a = 3. Check with a = 3: p(x) = x³ − 3x² + 2x + 6, and p(−1) = −1 − 3 − 2 + 6 = 0 ✓.

Worked example 3 (a quadratic divisor). Suppose a polynomial p(x) leaves remainder 3 when divided by x − 1 and remainder 5 when divided by x − 2. What is its remainder when divided by (x − 1)(x − 2)? That remainder has degree at most 1, so write it as ax + b. Then p(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2)q(x) + ax + b, and putting x = 1 and x = 2 kills the first term: a + b = 3 and 2a + b = 5. Subtracting gives a = 2 and b = 1, so the remainder is 2x + 1.

Worked example 4 (a huge power). What is the remainder when x¹⁰⁰ is divided by x² − 1? Write x¹⁰⁰ = (x²)⁵⁰. Since x² = 1 + (x² − 1), the power (x²)⁵⁰ leaves remainder 1ⁿ = 1. So the remainder is 1. (Alternatively, the remainder ax + b satisfies a + b = 1 from x = 1 and −a + b = 1 from x = −1, so a = 0, b = 1.)

Try it

Find the remainder when 2x³ − 3x² + x + 5 is divided by x − 2, and by x + 1.

Answer. Divide by x − 2: p(2) = 16 − 12 + 2 + 5 = 11. Divide by x + 1: p(−1) = −2 − 3 − 1 + 5 = −1. (If you want a check, Horner's method from section 1 gave 11 at x = 2.)

4. The factor theorem and finding roots

A root (or zero) of p is a number a with p(a) = 0. Combining the remainder theorem with the idea of divisibility gives the factor theorem: x − a is a factor of p(x) if and only if p(a) = 0. In words, finding a root and finding a linear factor are the same job.

Worked example 1 (a cubic with whole-number roots). Solve x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0. Try the divisors of the constant term 6: p(1) = 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0, so x − 1 is a factor. Dividing (see the Try it above) leaves x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3), so the roots are 1, 2 and 3.

The rational root test. If a polynomial with whole-number coefficients has a fraction p/q (in lowest terms) as a root, then p divides the constant term and q divides the leading coefficient. This makes the search for roots a short list rather than a guess. For 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 the possible numerators are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6 and the possible denominators are 1 and 2, so the candidates are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6, ±½, ±3/2.

Worked example 2. Continue with 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6. Testing x = 3: 54 − 27 − 33 + 6 = 0, so 3 is a root and x − 3 is a factor. Synthetic division by 3: bring down 2; 2·3 − 3 = 3; 3·3 − 11 = −2; (−2)·3 + 6 = 0. The quotient is 2x² + 3x − 2, which factors as (2x − 1)(x + 2). So 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = (x − 3)(2x − 1)(x + 2) with roots 3, ½ and −2.

Worked example 3 (a polynomial built from its roots). Find the cubic with roots 1, 2, 3 and value −6 at x = 0. Any such cubic has the form a(x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3), because those three factors are forced and the only thing left to choose is the constant multiplier a. At x = 0 the value is a(−1)(−2)(−3) = −6a. Setting that equal to −6 gives a = 1, so the cubic is (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6.

Try it

Factor x³ − 13x + 12 completely.

Answer. The constant term is 12, so try ±1, ±2, ±3, ±4, ±6, ±12. p(1) = 1 − 13 + 12 = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor; p(3) = 27 − 39 + 12 = 0 and p(−4) = −64 + 52 + 12 = 0. A cubic has at most three roots, so x³ − 13x + 12 = (x − 1)(x − 3)(x + 4).

5. How many roots can a polynomial have?

A polynomial of degree n has at most n roots. The reason follows from the factor theorem: each root a gives a factor x − a, and the degrees of the factors cannot add up to more than n. (A quadratic has at most 2 roots, a cubic at most 3, and so on.) This simple fact is the engine of several powerful arguments.

The identity argument. Suppose two polynomials of degree at most n take the same value at n + 1 different numbers. Then their difference has degree at most n but n + 1 roots, which is impossible unless the difference is the zero polynomial. So the two polynomials are identical. For example, if a polynomial of degree at most 2 satisfies p(0) = p(1) = p(2) = 0, then p is the zero polynomial; and x³ − x has the three roots 0, 1, −1 so it is, up to a constant, the only cubic with those roots and leading coefficient 1.

Worked example. A quadratic p(x) takes the values 5, 5, 5 at x = 1, 2, 3. What is p? Consider p(x) − 5. It is a polynomial of degree at most 2 with three roots, so it must be zero. Hence p(x) = 5 for every x, a constant, and no genuine quadratic fits the data.

Why the bound matters. It tells you when to stop searching. If you have found n roots of a degree-n polynomial, you have found them all. It also explains why the equation x² − 5x + 6 = 0 has exactly the two solutions 2 and 3 and no third one hiding somewhere.

Try it

A cubic p has p(1) = p(2) = p(3) = 0 and p(0) = −6. Find p(4).

Answer. p(x) = a(x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3) and p(0) = −6a = −6 gives a = 1. Then p(4) = 3 · 2 · 1 = 6.

6. Interpolation: building a polynomial through given points

Given points, can we find a polynomial passing through all of them? Yes, always, and the answer is unique if we ask for the smallest possible degree. Through n + 1 points with different x-values there is exactly one polynomial of degree at most n. Existence is shown by construction; uniqueness is the identity argument from the previous section.

Method 1: solve for coefficients. To pass through (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 7) try p(x) = ax² + bx + c. From x = 0 we get c = 1. From x = 1: a + b + 1 = 3, so a + b = 2. From x = 2: 4a + 2b + 1 = 7, so 4a + 2b = 6, i.e. 2a + b = 3. Subtracting gives a = 1, so b = 1. The polynomial is x² + x + 1, and indeed it gives 1, 3, 7.

Method 2: finite differences. When the x-values are equally spaced, write the values and keep subtracting neighbours. For the values 1, 3, 7, 13 (at x = 0, 1, 2, 3) the first differences are 2, 4, 6 and the second differences are 2, 2. A constant second difference means a quadratic. In general, the n-th differences of a degree-n polynomial are constant, and the (n + 1)-th are zero. Here the quadratic is x² + x + 1 again, and the next value (x = 4) is 21, because the differences continue 8 and then 21 − 13 = 8.

Method 3: Lagrange's idea. Build the answer from pieces that each equal 1 at one given point and 0 at the others. To pass through (1, 2), (2, 5), (4, 17) take the piece for x = 1: (x − 2)(x − 4)/((1 − 2)(1 − 4)) = (x − 2)(x − 4)/3, which is 1 at x = 1 and 0 at x = 2 and 4. Doing the same for the other two points and adding them with weights 2, 5, 17 gives a quadratic; it simplifies to x² + 1, which indeed takes the values 2, 5, 17. The beauty of the method is that no equations need to be solved.

A warning. Interpolation says that some polynomial fits, not that the data came from a polynomial. The sequence 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 follows a pattern (powers of 2) but a degree-4 polynomial through these five values predicts 31 for the next term, not 32. Always ask what pattern is really claimed.

Try it

A quadratic takes the values 2, 5, 10 at x = 1, 2, 3. Find p(4) using differences, and find p(x).

Answer. First differences 3, 5; second difference 2. The next first difference is 7, so p(4) = 10 + 7 = 17. The quadratic is x² + 1 (check: 2, 5, 10 ✓), and p(4) = 17 ✓.

7. Putting it together: a larger example

Problem. The polynomial p(x) = x⁴ − 10x³ + 35x² − 50x + 24 has roots among the small whole numbers. Find them all, and then find the remainder when p is divided by x − 5.

Solution. The constant term is 24, so test small divisors. p(1) = 1 − 10 + 35 − 50 + 24 = 0, so x − 1 is a factor. Synthetic division by 1: bring down 1; 1 − 10 = −9; −9 + 35 = 26; 26 − 50 = −24; −24 + 24 = 0. The quotient is x³ − 9x² + 26x − 24. Test x = 2: 8 − 36 + 52 − 24 = 0, so x − 2 is a factor. Synthetic division by 2: 1; 2 − 9 = −7; −14 + 26 = 12; 24 − 24 = 0, quotient x² − 7x + 12 = (x − 3)(x − 4). So p(x) = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3)(x − 4) and the roots are 1, 2, 3, 4. By the remainder theorem the remainder on division by x − 5 is p(5) = 4 · 3 · 2 · 1 = 24.

Notice how the pieces cooperate: the rational root test shortened the search, the factor theorem turned each root into a factor, synthetic division did the factoring, and the remainder theorem answered the last question with a single multiplication. When you face a polynomial problem, ask first which of these four tools matches what is asked.

8. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. (1) Forgetting a zero placeholder for a missing power in synthetic division. (2) Using the wrong sign: dividing by x + 3 means a = −3, not 3. (3) Assuming every polynomial has whole-number roots; many do not, and then the rational root test shows there are none among the candidates. (4) Counting roots with multiplicity wrongly: (x − 2)² has the single root 2 but counts twice. (5) Thinking interpolation 'discovers' the true rule behind data.

Checks. After dividing, multiply back: divisor × quotient + remainder must give the original. After finding roots, test one in the original equation. After a remainder question, check the degree of the answer is below the degree of the divisor.

How to practise. Do five problems of each type until each takes under three minutes: evaluate by Horner, divide synthetically, find the remainder by substitution, find a missing coefficient, and interpolate through three points. In the Unlimited practice section the family 'Remainder theorem' generates fresh numbers every time.
