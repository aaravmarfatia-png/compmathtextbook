Reading

1. Residues of squares and cubes

The squares mod 10 end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9, never 2, 3, 7 or 8. Squares mod 4 are 0 or 1. Squares mod 8 are 0, 1 or 4. Cubes mod 9 are 0, 1 or 8, and cubes mod 7 are 0, 1 or 6. Residues are found by squaring or cubing every residue class.

Worked example 1. No perfect square has the form 4k + 3. So no number made of the digits 1 only (111…1 with at least two digits) is a square: such a number is 11 mod 100, which is 3 mod 4.

Worked example 2. Show x³ + y³ = 9z + 4 has no integer solutions. Cubes mod 9 are 0, 1, 8, so x³ + y³ mod 9 ∈ {0, 1, 2, 7, 8}, and 4 is not among them.

2. Digits

A number is congruent to its digit sum mod 9 (and mod 3), and to its alternating digit sum mod 11. The last digit gives the number mod 10, the last two digits mod 100, the last three mod 8.

Worked example. A number with digit sum 20 is neither a perfect square nor a perfect cube: 20 ≡ 2 mod 9, squares mod 9 are 0, 1, 4, 7, and cubes mod 9 are 0, 1, 8.

Palindromes and 11. Every palindrome with an even number of digits is divisible by 11 (alternating digit sum is 0).

3. Repunits

The repunit R_n = 11…1 (n ones) = (10ⁿ − 1)/9. R_3 = 111 = 3 × 37; R_6 = 111111 = 3 × 7 × 11 × 13 × 37 (checked). R_m divides R_n when m divides n. R_n is divisible by 3 iff 3 divides n.

Worked example. Show R_6 is divisible by 7: 111111 = 7 × 15873. In general 10⁶ ≡ 1 mod 7 so 10⁶ − 1 is divisible by 7, and R_6 = (10⁶ − 1)/9 with 9 coprime to 7.

Numbers like 9, 99, 999. 10ⁿ − 1 = 99…9, so a number consisting of nines is always one less than a power of 10.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Using the digit-sum test for 9 on numbers represented in another base. Concluding that a number is a square from its residue (residues only exclude). Forgetting that squares can be 0 mod anything.

Checks. Compute residues by squaring each class.

Practice. Memorise: squares mod 3 (0, 1), mod 4 (0, 1), mod 5 (0, 1, 4), mod 8 (0, 1, 4).

Worked examples with Try-its: Repunits and digit tricks

A repunit Rₙ = 11…1 (n ones) equals (10ⁿ − 1)/9; R₃ = 111 = 3 · 37. No repunit with n ≥ 2 is a perfect square: it ends in 11, so it is ≡ 3 (mod 4), but squares are 0 or 1 (mod 4). The digit sum determines the remainder mod 9 and mod 3, and the last two digits determine the remainder mod 4.

Try it

Is 1111 divisible by 11?

Answer. Alternating digit sum 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 = 0, so yes: 1111 = 11 · 101.

Worked examples with Try-its: Residues of squares and cubes

Squares are 0 or 1 mod 4, 0, 1 or 4 mod 8, and 0, 1, 4, 7 mod 9. Cubes are 0, 1 or 8 mod 9. These restrictions rule out many equations: x² + y² = 4k + 3 has no integer solutions, and x³ + y³ + z³ ≡ 4 or 5 mod 9 cannot be solved.

Try it

Can a perfect square end in 7?

Answer. No: squares end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. Show that 4ⁿ + 1 is never a perfect square for n ≥ 1. Solution. (2ⁿ)² = 4ⁿ < 4ⁿ + 1 < 4ⁿ + 2 · 2ⁿ + 1 = (2ⁿ + 1)², and there is no square strictly between consecutive squares.

Reflection. Sandwiching between consecutive squares is a standard argument.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Repunit factors. 111111 = 3 · 7 · 11 · 13 · 37 (a factorisation program agrees), because R₆ = (10⁶ − 1)/9 and 10⁶ − 1 = 999999 = 3³ · 7 · 11 · 13 · 37. Residues. A square ends in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9; a cube can end in any digit. Mod 9, cubes are 0, 1 or 8.

Try it

Is 2222222 divisible by 11?

Answer. Alternating digit sum 2 − 2 + 2 − 2 + 2 − 2 + 2 = 2, so no.

Try it

What is the remainder when 12345678 is divided by 9?

Answer. Digit sum 36, so remainder 0.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Find the smallest positive integer made only of 1s that is divisible by 7. Solution. R₆ = 111111 = 7 · 15873 and the smaller repunits 1, 11, 111, 1111, 11111 leave remainders 1, 4, 6, 5, 2 on division by 7, so the answer is 111111.

Check. 7 · 15873 = 111111 ✓.

Problem x18. What is the last digit of 12^99?

HintFind the pattern of remainders of successive powers.
Answer8
Method: Cycle of remainders

The remainders of 12, 12², 12³, … are 2, 4, 8, 6, and then they repeat from position 1 with period 4. Exponent 99 lands on 8.

Method: Repeated squaring

Write 99 = 1100011 in binary. The squares 12^(2^i) mod 10 are 2, 4, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6; multiplying the ones for the 1-bits gives 8.

Source: Andreescu & Gelca, 3.1-3.5
