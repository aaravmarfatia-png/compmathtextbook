Reading

1. Linear equations in two unknowns

The equation ax + by = c has integer solutions iff gcd(a, b) divides c (Units 9 and 32). Use the extended Euclidean algorithm to find one solution, then the general solution follows. For 17x + 5y = 1: 17 = 3 × 5 + 2, 5 = 2 × 2 + 1. Back-substituting: 1 = 5 − 2 × 2 = 5 − 2(17 − 3 × 5) = 7 × 5 − 2 × 17. So (x, y) = (−2, 7) works, and every solution is (−2 + 5t, 7 − 17t).

Counting solutions. How many non-negative solutions does 3x + 5y = 100 have? We need 100 − 5y divisible by 3, i.e. y ≡ 2 (mod 3), with 0 ≤ y ≤ 20: y = 2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, seven solutions.

2. Linear congruences

The congruence ax ≡ b (mod n) has solutions iff gcd(a, n) divides b, and then there are gcd(a, n) solutions mod n. For 7x ≡ 3 (mod 11): the inverse of 7 is 8 (56 = 5 × 11 + 1), so x ≡ 24 ≡ 2 (mod 11); check 14 = 11 + 3 ✓. For 6x ≡ 9 (mod 15): g = 3 divides 9, so there are 3 solutions mod 15. Divide by 3: 2x ≡ 3 (mod 5), x ≡ 4 (mod 5), so x ≡ 4, 9, 14 (mod 15). Check: 24 ≡ 9, 54 ≡ 9, 84 ≡ 9 ✓.

Systems. The Chinese remainder theorem solves several congruences with pairwise coprime moduli. x ≡ 1 (mod 3), x ≡ 2 (mod 5), x ≡ 3 (mod 7): from the first two, x ≡ 7 (mod 15) (7 = 2 × 3 + 1 ✓ and 7 ≡ 2 mod 5 ✓). Then x = 7 + 15t ≡ 3 (mod 7): 7 + 15t ≡ t ≡ 3 (mod 7), so t = 3 and x = 52 (mod 105). Check: 52 = 17 × 3 + 1, 52 = 10 × 5 + 2, 52 = 7 × 7 + 3 ✓.

3. Applications

Worked example 1. A number leaves remainder 3 when divided by 4 and 2 when divided by 5. The smallest such number is 7: 7 = 4 + 3 and 7 = 5 + 2. All of them are 7 + 20t.

Worked example 2. Show that 3x + 6y = 10 has no integer solutions. gcd(3, 6) = 3 does not divide 10.

Worked example 3: stamps. With 8¢ and 13¢ stamps, what is the largest postage you cannot make? 8 × 13 − 8 − 13 = 83. For 83 we would need 83 − 13k to be a multiple of 8 for some k from 0 to 6: the values 83, 70, 57, 44, 31, 18, 5 are not, so 83 is impossible. Meanwhile 82 = 13 × 2 + 8 × 7 works.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Dividing a congruence by a number not coprime to the modulus without dividing the modulus too. Forgetting to count all solutions mod n. Using the wrong inverse.

Checks. Substitute each solution.

Practice. Find the gcd first.

Worked examples with Try-its: Solving ax + by = c

A solution exists exactly when gcd(a, b) divides c. For 7x + 5y = 100 one solution is (0, 20), and all others are (5t, 20 − 7t); with x, y ≥ 0 this leaves (0, 20), (5, 13) and (10, 6). Find a first solution with the Euclidean algorithm, then add multiples of (b/g, −a/g).

Try it

How many positive integer solutions does 3x + 4y = 25 have?

Answer. (7, 1), (3, 4): two solutions.

Worked examples with Try-its: Congruences

To solve 7x ≡ 3 (mod 11) multiply by the inverse of 7, which is 8 because 56 = 5 · 11 + 1; then x ≡ 24 ≡ 2, and indeed 14 ≡ 3. The Chinese Remainder Theorem: x ≡ 2 (mod 3) and x ≡ 3 (mod 5) together are equivalent to x ≡ 8 (mod 15). Frobenius coin problem. With coins of 3 and 5 the largest amount that cannot be paid is 3 · 5 − 3 − 5 = 7.

Try it

Find x with x ≡ 1 (mod 4) and x ≡ 2 (mod 5).

Answer. x ≡ 17 (mod 20).

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. A shop sells pens at 7 dollars and notebooks at 5 dollars. In how many ways can exactly 100 dollars be spent on at least one of each? Solution. The solutions found above with both counts positive are (5, 13) and (10, 6): two ways.

Reflection. Always list the parametric family and apply the bounds at the end.

Solvability. 12x + 18y = 30 reduces to 2x + 3y = 5 with solutions (1, 1), (−2, 3), (4, −1), …; and 4x + 6y = 7 has none because gcd(4, 6) = 2 does not divide 7. Frobenius. With coins of 4 and 7 the largest unreachable amount is 4 · 7 − 4 − 7 = 17. Chinese remainder. x ≡ 3 (mod 4) and x ≡ 2 (mod 7) give x ≡ 23 (mod 28).

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Find the smallest positive integer that leaves remainder 2 on division by 3, 3 on division by 5 and 2 on division by 7. Solution. x ≡ 2 (mod 21) from the first and third conditions, so x = 2, 23, 44, 65, …; the second condition x ≡ 3 (mod 5) first holds for x = 23. Check: 23 = 3 · 7 + 2, 23 = 4 · 5 + 3, 23 = 3 · 7 + 2.

Reflection. Combine two congruences at a time.

Problem p1. How many ordered pairs of positive integers (x, y) satisfy 3x + 5y = 47?

HintTest y = 1, 2, ….
Answer3
Method: Cases

47 − 5y must be a positive multiple of 3: y = 1 (42 → x = 14), y = 4 (27 → x = 9), y = 7 (12 → x = 4). That is 3 pairs.

Method: General solution

One solution is (14, 1) and the others differ by (−5, +3): (9, 4), (4, 7), then x would be negative. So 3.

Problem p8. How many integers n from 1 to 100 satisfy n² ≡ 1 (mod 8)?

HintOdd squares are 1 mod 8.
Answer50

Problem x22. What is the remainder when 10^31 is divided by 7?

HintFind the pattern of remainders of successive powers.
Answer3
Method: Cycle of remainders

The remainders of 10, 10², 10³, … are 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, and then they repeat from position 1 with period 6. Exponent 31 lands on 3.

Method: Repeated squaring

Write 31 = 11111 in binary. The squares 10^(2^i) mod 7 are 3, 2, 4, 2, 4; multiplying the ones for the 1-bits gives 3.
