Reading

1. Counting lines, regions and diagonals

Lines from points. n points, no three on a line, determine C(n, 2) lines. Regions from lines. n lines in general position (no two parallel, no three concurrent) divide the plane into 1 + n(n + 1)/2 regions: 2 lines give 4, 3 lines give 7, 4 lines give 11. Regions from circles. n circles, every two meeting in two points and no three through a point, give n² − n + 2 regions: 1 circle: 2; 2 circles: 4; 3 circles: 8.

Worked example. How many regions do 10 lines cut the plane into if no two are parallel and no three concurrent? 1 + 10 × 11/2 = 56.

Diagonals. A convex n-gon has n(n − 3)/2 diagonals; a triangulation uses n − 3 non-crossing diagonals and has n − 2 triangles.

2. Convex position

A set of points is in convex position if each is a vertex of the convex hull. Happy ending theorem (small case): among any 5 points in the plane, no three collinear, some 4 form a convex quadrilateral. Proof: look at the convex hull. If it has 4 or 5 vertices, take 4 hull vertices. If it has 3 vertices (a triangle ABC) with two points D, E inside, the line DE leaves one side of the triangle on one side and two vertices on the other, say A, B and then the points A, B, D, E form a convex quadrilateral.

Worked example. Four points can fail to be in convex position (one inside the triangle of the other three), so the 'happy ending' result needs five points. For pentagons the number needed is nine: among any 9 points in general position some 5 form a convex pentagon, while 8 points can avoid it.

3. Covering, packing and combinatorial arguments

Averaging. If the points of a unit square carry total weight W spread unevenly, some region has at least average weight. If 9 points lie in a unit square, some three of them form a triangle of area at most 1/8: divide the square into 4 strips and use pigeonhole.

Sylvester–Gallai. For a finite set of points not all collinear, there is a line through exactly two of them. (The proof takes a point and a line with the smallest positive distance and shows a contradiction otherwise.)

Helly (1D). If every two of a finite family of intervals on a line intersect, then all of them share a common point: the left-most right endpoint is to the right of every left endpoint.

4. Mistakes, checks and how to practise

Common mistakes. Allowing three concurrent lines in a count that assumes general position. Not checking collinear cases. Forgetting that the count of regions depends on the configuration.

Checks. Draw 3 or 4 objects and count regions to test the formula.

Practice. State the general-position assumptions at the start.

Worked examples with Try-its: Counting regions and intersections

Lines. n lines in general position (no two parallel, no three through a point) cut the plane into n(n + 1)/2 + 1 regions: 5 lines give 16. Each new line crosses all earlier ones and so adds (number of earlier lines) + 1 regions. Circles. n circles, each pair meeting in two points and no three through one point, make n² − n + 2 regions: 4 circles give 14.

Polygons. A convex n-gon has n(n − 3)/2 diagonals (an octagon has 20) and, if no three diagonals are concurrent, C(n, 4) interior intersection points (15 for a hexagon). Joining n points on a circle by all chords gives C(n, 4) + C(n, 2) + 1 regions: 31 for n = 6.

Try it

How many regions do 6 lines in general position make?

Answer. 6 · 7/2 + 1 = 22.

Worked examples with Try-its: Convex position and angle sums

The interior angles of a convex n-gon add up to (n − 2) · 180°, so a regular hexagon has angles of 120°. Any 5 points in the plane with no three collinear contain 4 that form a convex quadrilateral (a classical result of Esther Klein). Pick's theorem and Euler's formula (earlier units) also appear in this area.

Try it

What is each interior angle of a regular 12-gon?

Answer. 10 · 180/12 = 150°.

Worked examples with Try-its: A longer problem

Problem. How many triangles are formed by the diagonals of a convex hexagon in the sense that three vertices of the hexagon give a triangle? And how many pairs of diagonals cross inside it? Solution. Triangles from vertices: C(6, 3) = 20. Crossing pairs of diagonals inside correspond to choosing 4 vertices: C(6, 4) = 15.

Reflection. Each crossing of two diagonals is determined by the four endpoints, which is why C(n, 4) appears.

More worked examples: Worked examples

Seven lines in general position make 7 · 8/2 + 1 = 29 regions, and 5 circles make 5² − 5 + 2 = 22. A convex decagon has 10 · 7/2 = 35 diagonals, and a convex heptagon with no three diagonals concurrent has C(7, 4) = 35 interior intersection points of diagonals.

Try it

A convex polygon has 27 diagonals. How many sides?

Answer. n(n − 3)/2 = 27 gives n = 9.

Try it

Into how many regions do 3 lines divide the plane if two are parallel and the third crosses both?

Answer. 6.

More worked examples: A second longer problem

Problem. Prove that in any convex n-gon the diagonals from one vertex divide it into n − 2 triangles. Solution. From a vertex there are n − 3 diagonals, which cut the polygon into (n − 3) + 1 = n − 2 triangles, so the angle sum is (n − 2) · 180°.

Check. A hexagon gives 4 triangles and 720°.

Problem p1. How many diagonals does a convex 12-gon have?

Hintn(n − 3)/2.
Answer54
Method: Formula

12 · 9/2 = 54.

Method: Subtract sides

All pairs of vertices: C(12,2) = 66, minus the 12 sides gives 54.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 and Andreescu & Gelca

Problem p2. In how many ways can a convex hexagon be cut into triangles by non-crossing diagonals?

HintCatalan number C₄.
Answer14
Method: Catalan

C₄ = 14.

Method: Recursion

Fix one side; the triangle on it has apex at one of the other 4 vertices, giving 1·5 + 1·2 + 2·1 + 5·1 = 14 with T(3), T(4), T(5) = 1, 2, 5.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 and Andreescu & Gelca

Problem p7. What is the largest number of acute angles a convex polygon can have?

HintLook at the exterior angles.
Answer3
Method: Exterior angles

Each acute interior angle has an exterior angle above 90°, and exterior angles sum to 360°, so at most 3 are above 90°.

Method: Example

A triangle has 3 acute angles, and a quadrilateral with 3 acute angles exists, so 3 is reached.

Source: Sriram, Olympiad Combinatorics, ch. 4 and Andreescu & Gelca

Problem p8. Five horizontal and four vertical lines make a grid. How many rectangles use these lines as sides?

HintChoose 2 horizontal and 2 vertical lines.
Answer60
Method: Choose

C(5,2) · C(4,2) = 10 · 6 = 60.

Method: Sum

The grid has 4 × 3 cells; counting rectangles by width and height gives the same 60.

Problem p15. A convex pentagon has all its diagonals drawn, with no three concurrent. How many regions is it divided into?

HintRegions = C(n, 4) + C(n − 1, 2).
Answer11
Method: Formula

C(5, 4) + C(4, 2) = 5 + 6 = 11.

Method: Euler

The 5 diagonals form a central pentagon, 5 small triangles pointing outward from it, and 5 triangles along the sides: 1 + 5 + 5 = 11 regions.
